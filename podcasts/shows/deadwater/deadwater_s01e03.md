# DEADWATER
## Episode 3: "The Department"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] In 2016, the chief of the South Shore Police Department was arrested.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Not for anything connected to the Deadwater case. For something else entirely - a brutal assault on a man in custody, followed by a years-long cover-up that implicated a dozen officers and reached into the highest levels of the department's leadership. He pleaded guilty. He went to prison.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The reason I'm telling you this is not to relitigate that case. It's to give you a picture of the institution that was responsible for investigating the murders of ten women. Because you cannot understand why those women went unidentified for so long without understanding what was happening inside the department that was supposed to be finding them.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh joined the SSPD in 1998, three years before the first bodies were found near Gilgo. She worked her way up through patrol, then narcotics, then homicide. She is one of the few women who reached the rank of Detective Sergeant during the period when the department was under the leadership of Chief Gary Holden.

[MARSH] Holden was what you'd call a department man. He came up through the old guard - the culture where loyalty to the badge meant loyalty to each other, full stop. Not to the public. Not to the victims. To the people in the building. And that culture had a long history on Long Island that I won't pretend I didn't notice.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] The thing about working in that environment is that you learn very quickly which battles you can fight and which ones will just cost you everything without changing anything. I chose my battles carefully. I regret some of those choices. I think about them.

[NARRATOR] One of the battles Carol Marsh chose not to fight - in the early years of the investigation - was over resources.

[MARSH] In the first two years we had forty people working Deadwater. By year three it was twelve. By year five it was four, including me and Danny. I went to Holden twice and asked for more personnel and both times I was told the case was being appropriately resourced given the available leads. "Available leads." Like the leads weren't there because we didn't have the people to develop them.

[REESE] There's a dynamic in departments, especially under certain kinds of leadership, where a case that is publicly embarrassing - where the victims are women who were doing work that certain people don't think warrants full effort - that case gets quietly de-prioritized. Not in writing. Nobody ever writes a memo saying we care less about these women. It just shows up in where the people go. Where the money goes. What gets fast-tracked and what sits.

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese says this carefully, fully aware that he is criticizing an institution he spent his career in. He has retired now. He has less to lose. But he still chooses his words with the same precision he brought to the investigation.

[REESE] I want to be specific: there were people in that department who worked Deadwater with everything they had. Carol Marsh is the most obvious example. There were others. This is not a story about a corrupt investigation. It's a story about a culture that decided, not always consciously, that some victims mattered more than others. And that culture cost us time. And time, in a murder investigation, is the thing you can never get back.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In 2013, an internal review of the early Deadwater investigation is quietly commissioned by the department. It is never publicly released.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh is not told it is happening.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She finds out about it three years later when a source inside the department sends her a two-page summary - not the full document, just a summary - as an email attachment at eleven-thirty on a Tuesday night.

[MARSH] I sat up until three in the morning reading that two pages. And then I read them again. And I will tell you honestly: it made me angrier than anything in my career up to that point. Not because it said the investigation was mishandled - I knew that already. Because it identified specific early leads that had been logged and not followed up, and those leads - some of them - pointed in a direction that would have taken us to Voss years earlier.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Years. We might have had him years earlier. I can't prove what that would have meant. I don't let myself go down that road very often because it's not useful and it is genuinely painful. But the possibility is there. And it sits with me.

[NARRATOR] The review identified several failures. Missing persons reports that had been filed on victims but not linked to the Deadwater case when the bodies were discovered. A vehicle description provided by a witness in the second year of the investigation that was logged and then not actioned. And a record - a parking citation, issued at one of the barrier island access points in a year prior to the discovery of the bodies - connected to a vehicle registered to a business address in Manhattan.

[REESE] A parking citation. That's what it was. One of our patrol officers, doing routine enforcement on a winter day when the beach access lots are supposed to be closed to the public, writes a citation for a vehicle that shouldn't be there. Files it. And it sits in a database that nobody cross-references against the Deadwater geographic profile because we didn't have a protocol for that. Because in the early years of the investigation, we were moving too fast on too many fronts with too few people.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That vehicle was registered to Roland Voss's architecture firm.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The citation was issued six months before the bodies were found.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He was there. He was walking that road. He was scouting it in the winter, when no one was around, and a patrol officer caught him by accident and wrote him a parking ticket and filed it and moved on. And for twelve years, nobody connected those dots.

[MARSH] When I found that in the review summary - that specific detail - I had to put the papers down and walk away for ten minutes. Because the implications of it are almost too much to sit with.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The internal review is never officially acted on. Chief Holden retires in 2019, months before his arrest. The department goes through two interim commissioners and a period of genuine institutional chaos during which the Deadwater file, according to Marsh, "is essentially running on fumes."

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And then something changes.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In early 2022, a new commissioner is appointed. He comes from outside the department - a federal background, a reputation for institutional reform, and apparently no patience for the kinds of accommodations that had allowed cases like Deadwater to stagnate.

[REESE] His first month in, he does a full review of cold cases. Every major unsolved homicide in the department's jurisdiction. He sits down with the files personally. And he gets to Deadwater and he calls Carol Marsh into his office.

[MARSH] He said: this case has more preserved evidence than any cold case I've ever seen. Why isn't it solved? And I said: insufficient resources, insufficient technology when the investigation began, and some institutional failures I'd be happy to brief him on in detail. He said: brief me. So I did. It took four hours.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] At the end of it he said: you're getting a task force. I said: how many people? He said: as many as you need. I said: I need twelve detectives, a digital forensics team, a genealogical DNA consultant, and access to the full case archive including the internal review from 2013 that I was never officially given. He didn't even blink.

[NARRATOR] The task force is formally constituted in February 2022. It includes the SSPD, the county sheriff's office, the state police, and the FBI.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Twelve years after the bodies were found, the real investigation is finally beginning.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey gets a call from Carol Marsh the week the task force is announced. She hasn't spoken to Marsh in eight months. Life had intervened - a health crisis, her son starting college, the grinding practical weight of grief that doesn't announce itself, it just shows up.

[DONNA] She called me on a Thursday afternoon. She said: I need to tell you something is happening. She couldn't give me details. But she said, Donna, I want you to know that I have not forgotten Renee. And I said, I know you haven't. Because in twelve years of fighting this case, Carol Marsh was the one person in any official capacity who I never had to remind of that.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I cried after I hung up. Not sad crying. Relief crying. The kind that comes out of nowhere after you've been holding yourself together for a very long time.

[NARRATOR] She does not know yet what the task force will find. She does not know yet that they will have a name within six weeks.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: The List. The task force builds its suspect profile - and a single witness account, overlooked for ten years, becomes the thread that pulls everything open.
