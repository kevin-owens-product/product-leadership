### The problem: you can't shut it down

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer problem. Why is it so valuable to fix things without shutting down?

**JAMES:** Because in process industries, downtime is brutally expensive. A refinery unit, a chemical plant, a power station, a big pipeline, they're designed to run continuously. Shutting down isn't like turning off a light. You have to bring temperatures and pressures down safely, drain or purge lines, make the repair, then restart, which itself carries risk. Every hour offline is lost production.

**NORA:** So plants plan their shutdowns.

**JAMES:** They do. In the industry, a planned shutdown for major maintenance is usually called a turnaround. Plants schedule them carefully, sometimes years in advance, and pack a lot of work into them. The problem is what happens between turnarounds. Things leak. Valves fail. A new connection is needed for a project. If you can't wait for the next turnaround, you need a way to do the work live.

**NORA:** And that's where Colt comes in.

**JAMES:** That's the niche. Emergency and unplanned work where the asset has to stay in service. And because it's often urgent and high-stakes, the buyer is paying for speed and competence more than for the lowest price. That's our inference from the service model, but Colt's own site makes the same point by stressing certified procedures and expert technicians.

**NORA:** What about safety? Working on a live, pressurised line sounds dangerous.

**JAMES:** It is, and that's exactly why customers hire specialists. A leaking line of steam, hydrocarbons or chemicals is dangerous in itself. Doing anything to it while it's live requires engineered procedures, qualified people and strict controls. A good contractor reduces risk. A bad one can make a bad situation catastrophic. Safety record is one of the first things a plant will check before letting a contractor on site.

**NORA:** And these repairs, are they permanent?

**JAMES:** Often they're intended to be temporary, to keep the plant running safely until the next planned shutdown, when the component can be properly repaired or replaced. That's general industry practice. Which creates an interesting information problem. A big plant may have many temporary repairs in place at once, and someone has to keep track of where they are, how old they are and when they need permanent attention.

**NORA:** Which is exactly the kind of list a contractor could keep.

**JAMES:** Exactly, and we'll come back to it, because it's a natural bridge from emergency work to an ongoing service.

**NORA:** And the water and utility side?

**JAMES:** Similar logic, different stakes. If a city needs to add a connection to a large water main, or isolate a section to repair it, shutting the main means cutting off customers. Hot tapping and line stopping let the utility keep water flowing while the work happens. The Garrison acquisition, a utility contractor, suggests Colt sees that as a growth area.

**NORA:** Who's the actual buyer inside a refinery?

**JAMES:** Usually maintenance and reliability teams, sometimes operations, and procurement for the contracts. In general terms, large plants often have approved contractor lists and master service agreements, so being on the list before the emergency happens matters enormously. When the leak starts at three a.m., nobody is running a tender. They call the firm they already trust.

**NORA:** What does being on that list actually involve?

**JAMES:** In general terms, a lot. Then on each visit, site inductions and work permits before anyone touches anything. It's a heavy administrative load, and it's also a barrier to entry. A new competitor can't just show up at a refinery gate.

**NORA:** And power and nuclear?

**JAMES:** Those can be even stricter. Nuclear sites in particular have demanding quality assurance rules for any contractor working on them. Colt lists power and nuclear as a market, but we didn't find details of its nuclear work, so I won't speculate on how big it is. The general point is that each segment has its own qualification hurdles, and clearing them takes years.

### How the work gets done

**NORA:** Let's go deeper on the services. Start with on-line leak repair.

**JAMES:** In general terms, the classic approach is to engineer an enclosure or clamp that fits around the leaking component, a flange, a valve, a section of pipe, and then inject a sealant compound into the enclosure under pressure to stop the leak. The trick is that every leak is a bit different. Different sizes, temperatures, pressures, fluids. So the enclosure usually has to be designed and made for that specific job, often very quickly.

**NORA:** Which is why the in-house engineering and fabrication matter.

**JAMES:** Exactly. If you can take measurements on site, have an engineer design the clamp, and have your own shop machine it overnight, you can be back on site the next day. If you have to outsource that, you lose time.

**NORA:** Hot tapping?

**JAMES:** Hot tapping is making a new connection to a pipe while it's in service. You weld or bolt a fitting onto the outside of the live pipe, attach a valve and a special drilling machine, cut through the pipe wall inside a sealed chamber, then withdraw the cutter and close the valve. Now you have a new branch connection, and the main line never stopped.

**NORA:** And line stopping?

**JAMES:** Line stopping usually builds on a hot tap. Once you've got access into the pipe, you insert a plugging head that blocks the flow, so you can isolate a section downstream for repair or replacement, often with a temporary bypass to keep the product moving. When the work's done, you remove the plug and cap the fitting.

**NORA:** And field machining?

**JAMES:** That's taking machine tools to the equipment rather than taking the equipment to a machine shop. Resurfacing a damaged flange face in place, cutting and bevelling pipe, that kind of work. We'll actually cover a company that specialises in that in our next episode.

**NORA:** So what's common across all of these?

**JAMES:** Three things. Every job is a bit custom. Every job needs a qualified procedure and qualified people. And every job generates information. Measurements, drawings, materials, sealant choices, what worked, what didn't. Over forty-five years, that's a lot of information. Whether it's been captured well is the big question.

### A leak at three in the morning

**NORA:** Let's do the walk-through. A hypothetical call-out, invented customer, invented people.

**JAMES:** Let's go back to that refinery. The reliability engineer, call him Ray, sees a steam leak at a flange on a line feeding a processing unit. It's not catastrophic yet, but it's growing. He calls the leak repair contractor on his approved list, a company like Colt.

**NORA:** What happens next today?

**JAMES:** A dispatcher takes the call and asks the basics. What's leaking, the line size, the temperature, the pressure, the fluid. Then a technician drives out to the site, goes through the plant's safety induction and permits, and measures the flange carefully. Those measurements and photos go back to an engineer, who designs an enclosure and specifies a sealant suited to that temperature and fluid. The design goes to the shop, which machines the clamp, perhaps overnight.

**NORA:** And Ray's unit keeps running while that happens.

**JAMES:** It does, though the leak is being watched closely. Next day, the crew installs the enclosure and injects the sealant. The leak stops. The crew documents the job, the plant signs off, and an invoice follows. Ray adds a note to his maintenance system that this flange has a temporary repair to be addressed at the next turnaround.

**NORA:** Where's the time going?

**JAMES:** In my view, three places. First, the information gathering. Back-and-forth between dispatcher, technician and engineer, getting the right details. Second, the engineering. Each design takes an engineer's time, even when the job is similar to one done before. Third, the paperwork after the job. Reports, records, invoicing.

**NORA:** Now replay it with AI.

**JAMES:** Ray calls in, and the dispatcher's system captures the details in a structured way, maybe even from a photo Ray sends. The system immediately searches Colt's records. Have we repaired this kind of flange, at this kind of temperature and pressure, with this fluid, before? Maybe even at this same refinery. It pulls up the five most similar past jobs: the clamp designs used, the sealants, any problems encountered.

**NORA:** So the engineer isn't starting from a blank page.

**JAMES:** Exactly. The engineer reviews the closest previous design, adapts it to the new measurements, and checks it. That could cut design time significantly, though we have no numbers to say how much. The technician arrives on site already knowing what's been tried at this facility. After the job, the system drafts the report from the technician's notes and photos, and the job record goes back into the knowledge base, better structured than before.

**NORA:** And the engineer still signs off.

**JAMES:** Always. This is safety-critical engineering on live, pressurised systems. The AI is a librarian and a drafting assistant. The professional engineer owns the design.

**NORA:** What does Ray, the customer, notice about the difference?

**JAMES:** Speed and confidence, mostly. The quote arrives faster and is grounded in similar past jobs. The crew turns up with a clamp that fits first time. And afterwards, Ray gets a clean digital record that slots into his own maintenance system, with the location, the design and a recommended date for permanent repair. When his next turnaround is being planned, that record is there, not lost in an email inbox.

**NORA:** So the benefit to Colt shows up as loyalty.

**JAMES:** Loyalty and share of wallet, in my view. The contractor whose records make the reliability engineer's job easier is the one who gets the next call, and probably a bigger slice of the turnaround work too. That's a hypothesis, but it's how relationships work in this kind of trade.

**NORA:** And are there AI-native challengers?

**JAMES:** We didn't find any in leak repair itself. But there's a broader trend toward predictive maintenance, using sensors and historical data to anticipate failures. **NORA:** Let's go segment by segment. How do the markets differ?

**JAMES:** Colt lists five broad areas. Refining and chemical is the core, in my view, given where Colt is based. That's high-temperature, high-pressure, often hazardous fluids, and plants that run around the clock. Downtime is very expensive, so the value of on-line repair is highest there.

**NORA:** Water?

**JAMES:** Municipal and industrial water is lower temperature and pressure, but the pipes are large and the customers can't be cut off. Hot tapping and line stopping are especially relevant, for new connections and isolating sections for repair. The buyer is often a public utility, which brings different procurement rules, more formal tenders, and slower decisions. Garrison, as a utility contractor, likely brings experience there.

**NORA:** And pipelines and utilities?

**JAMES:** Pipelines need hot taps and line stops when operators add connections or repair sections without stopping flow. Utilities, including gas distribution, need similar work on their networks. Power covers generating stations, where steam systems and cooling water lines leak just like they do in refineries. And through RJ Stacey, there's steel, with blast furnace repair. The mix matters because the segments move on different cycles. When refining slows, water and utilities may hold up, and the reverse.

### The new owner's playbook

**NORA:** Let's talk about the buy-and-build. What does the new owner actually do with a company like this?

**JAMES:** The stated plan is add-on acquisitions and investment in people, capabilities and geographic footprint. In practice, that typically means buying regional leak repair and hot tapping firms in areas where Colt is thin, adding capabilities like specialised machining or composites, and investing in the national network of crews and equipment.

**NORA:** What's the integration challenge?

**JAMES:** Every acquired firm has its own way of quoting, dispatching, documenting jobs and invoicing. Its own rate cards. Its own safety procedures, which need to be brought up to a common standard. Its own records of past jobs, probably in different formats, some on paper. RJ Stacey in Michigan and Garrison in New Jersey will each have their own systems and habits. And a company that has changed hands twice in a couple of years may have standardisation work still unfinished. That's our inference.

**NORA:** And why does that matter for AI?

**JAMES:** Because the knowledge base idea only works if records from every part of the group end up in one searchable, consistent place. If RJ Stacey's forty years of blast furnace repairs sit in a filing cabinet in Michigan, they don't help a technician in Texas. The integration project and the AI project are, in my view, the same project.

**NORA:** What about pricing?

**JAMES:** There's a straightforward opportunity to standardise rate cards across acquired businesses. When you buy small firms, you often find inconsistent pricing for similar work. Bringing that into line, carefully, is a classic buy-and-build lever. And over time, better data on job costs lets you price more accurately, which matters a lot in emergency work where quotes are often made fast.

**NORA:** Is quoting itself an AI opportunity?

**JAMES:** I think so. In emergency work, quotes are often made under time pressure, sometimes by phone, and the risk of underpricing a complex job is real. With good job-cost history, an assistant could suggest a price range based on similar past jobs: size, temperature, pressure, fluid, travel distance, likely crew size, fabrication needed. The estimator still decides, but starts from evidence rather than instinct. Over hundreds of jobs a year, more accurate quoting adds up.

That's our inference about the industry generally, not a finding about Colt.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's name the challenges. What's hard for Colt, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, key-person dependence. Specialty services businesses depend heavily on experienced technicians and engineers, and a founder-era company may also depend heavily on a few senior leaders. If knowledge isn't captured, retirements and departures hurt.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Integration. A buy-and-build adds firms with different systems, prices and cultures. Doing that fast without diluting the responsiveness Colt sells is genuinely hard.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Cyclicality. Refining, chemicals and pipelines go through investment cycles. When oil prices fall or margins tighten, plants cut maintenance spending where they can. Emergency repairs can't be cut, but planned work and projects can be deferred. Diversification into water, utilities, power and steel helps, but the core is still energy and process industry.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**JAMES:** Workforce. Certified welders and skilled field technicians are in demand across many industries, and the work involves travel, odd hours and hazardous conditions. Recruiting and keeping them is a constant challenge, though we didn't find Colt-specific data on it.

**NORA:** And how long does it take to make a good leak repair technician?

**JAMES:** We don't have a figure for Colt, but in general, skilled trades like this take years to master. You learn by doing jobs alongside experienced people, seeing many kinds of leaks, learning what works on which fluid at which temperature. That apprenticeship model is slow, and it depends on the senior people having time to teach. In my view, this is one of the most underrated places for AI. Not replacing the apprenticeship, but giving a newer technician access to the experience of the whole company when the senior person isn't standing next to them.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Turning emergency work into recurring revenue. Emergency call-outs are valuable but lumpy and hard to forecast. Investors generally value recurring, contracted revenue more highly. The challenge, and the opportunity, is to build more predictable revenue streams, which is where monitoring comes in.

**NORA:** Is there an AI-specific challenge?

**JAMES:** Data quality and trust. Technicians and engineers in safety-critical work are rightly sceptical of tools that might be wrong. A knowledge base that surfaces an inappropriate past repair as a template could mislead someone. So the tools need to be designed as aids to qualified people, with clear provenance, and introduced with the engineers rather than imposed on them.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's talk opportunities. Where would you look first?

**JAMES:** The first is AI-assisted repair engineering, the knowledge base we walked through. Every past job, procedure, design and engineer's note, searchable by the characteristics of the leak. The value lever is labour hours, faster estimates and less rework, and faster response, which is what customers pay for.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** A predictive offering built on ColtCONNECT. If Colt can install monitoring on customers' at-risk equipment, flanges with past repairs, ageing lines, critical valves, and alert them before a leak becomes an emergency, that turns ad hoc call-outs into a contracted service. The value lever is recurring revenue and margin. And it changes the relationship from the firm you call when things go wrong to the firm that helps you keep things from going wrong.

**NORA:** Let's make that one concrete too. A second walk-through.

**JAMES:** Sure. Invented example again. A chemical plant has a history of leaks on a few dozen flanges in a hot service area, many of which have temporary repairs waiting for the next turnaround. Today, the plant waits for leaks to appear, then calls for help. With a monitoring subscription, sensors are installed on the highest-risk points, measuring things like temperature or acoustic signals that can indicate a developing leak.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** A dashboard shows the plant, and Colt, which points are stable and which are trending. When one starts to drift, Colt's team gets an alert, checks the history of that flange in its records, and calls the plant to suggest an inspection or a planned repair before it becomes a three a.m. emergency. Colt might even pre-fabricate the enclosure because the dimensions are already on file.

**NORA:** So the plant pays a monthly fee for fewer emergencies.

**JAMES:** That's the idea. And it also feeds the turnaround plan. Before the next shutdown, Colt can give the plant a prioritised list of every temporarily repaired point and its condition. In my view, that's a much more valuable relationship than waiting for the phone to ring. But I want to be clear, this is a hypothesis. We don't know how far ColtCONNECT already goes toward it.

**NORA:** What about the risk of cannibalising emergency revenue?

**JAMES:** It's a real tension. If monitoring prevents emergencies, Colt does fewer high-margin emergency jobs. But in my view, someone will sell plants predictive monitoring anyway. Better for Colt to own that relationship than to lose it to a sensor startup. And planned repairs are still repairs, just scheduled, and probably more efficient to deliver.

**NORA:** Third opportunity?

**JAMES:** Dispatch and scheduling optimisation across a national network. Which crew, with which equipment and qualifications, can reach which site fastest? As Colt grows through acquisitions, that becomes a harder problem and a bigger lever. The key measure is emergency response time, along with labour and travel cost.

**NORA:** What else?

**JAMES:** The stated ones: more add-on acquisitions in uncovered regions, and investment in geographic footprint. Standardised rate cards across acquired businesses. And cross-selling Colt's manufacturing and fabrication capacity, the CNC and composites work, to existing repair customers. Plus, in my view, a fairly simple one: AI-drafted job reports and invoices from technicians' notes and photos, which saves time and speeds up cash collection.

**NORA:** You hinted at training earlier.

**JAMES:** Yes. The same knowledge base that helps an engineer design a clamp can help train a new technician. Short case studies drawn from real past jobs: here's the leak, here's what we did, here's what went wrong the first time and why. Built into onboarding, that could shorten the time to competence. It's also a recruiting message. Join us and you'll learn from forty-five years of jobs, not just from whoever happens to be on your crew.

### The first 100 days and the best AI bet

**NORA:** This is the episode where the hundred-day question is literal. The deal just closed. What should the first hundred days look like?

**JAMES:** I'd split it into three tracks: understand, quick wins, and set up the big bet. Days one to thirty are mostly understanding. Meet the field leaders, engineers and the leaders of the acquired businesses. Map how a job flows today in each business, from the call to the invoice. Inventory the data: where are the historical job records, in what format, how complete. And find out exactly what ColtCONNECT is, where it's installed and what it measures.

**NORA:** And quick wins?

**JAMES:** Days thirty to sixty. Pick one or two things that make technicians' and engineers' lives easier fast, because that builds trust. For example, a common digital job report format across the group, with AI drafting from notes and photos. Or a simple searchable archive of past repair designs from the main engineering office. Something people can use within weeks.

**NORA:** And setting up the big bet?

**JAMES:** Days sixty to a hundred. Decide, based on what you learned, which of the two big bets to fund first: the repair knowledge base or the predictive monitoring service. Pick an owner. Agree how you'll measure it. And pick pilot customers or a pilot region.

**NORA:** So which bet would you pick?

**JAMES:** If the historical records are reasonably complete and digital, the repair knowledge base first. It uses data Colt already owns, it helps the people who are the core of the business, it improves response speed immediately, and it doesn't need customers to buy anything new. Value lever: engineering and technician hours, faster quotes, fewer repeat call-outs.

**NORA:** And if the records are a mess?

**JAMES:** Then step one is digitising and structuring the records, which is itself useful, and starting predictive monitoring with a few willing customers in parallel. Monitoring generates new, clean data from day one. Either way, both bets reinforce each other. The knowledge base tells you what failed and how it was fixed. Monitoring tells you what's about to fail.

**NORA:** How would you run the knowledge base pilot?

**JAMES:** Start with the Gulf Coast engineering team. Load the last several years of job records, designs and photos. Have engineers use the search on every new job for a couple of months and log, for each job, whether it found something useful and how much time it saved. Have senior engineers review the results. If engineers say it's saving them real time, extend it to the acquired businesses and make adding records a standard part of closing every job.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** A senior engineer with credibility in the field, paired with someone who can handle the data and tools. If it's seen as a head-office IT project, technicians will ignore it. If it's seen as the engineering team's own tool, they'll feed it.

**NORA:** And governance?

**JAMES:** From day one: every design is still reviewed and approved by a qualified engineer, the tool shows the source of every suggestion, and customer data from one plant is handled under that customer's terms. Many refineries and pipelines are critical infrastructure with strict rules on data and site information. A clear policy written early is much easier than one written after a problem.

**NORA:** And what about the technicians themselves? These are people working on pipe racks, not at desks.

**JAMES:** That's a crucial design point. Any tool for the field has to work on a phone or a rugged tablet, often with gloves on, in noise, heat and sometimes poor signal. Voice notes and photos are far more practical than typing. An assistant that turns a technician's thirty-second voice note and four photos into a structured job record would be used. A long form would not.

**NORA:** So the interface matters as much as the intelligence.

**JAMES:** In field services, it often matters more. The best model in the world is useless if the technician won't open it at the end of a twelve-hour shift. Design for the worst moment of the worst day, and you'll get adoption.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Colt?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, your job history is a hidden asset. Every field services company has years of records about what broke and how it was fixed. Most treat them as paperwork. Structured and searchable, they become a competitive advantage that a new entrant can't buy.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, the first hundred days set the data foundations. When a new owner arrives, it's the best moment to agree a common way of recording jobs across every business, because everyone expects change. Wait two years, and every acquired business has dug in on its own habits.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, move from reactive to predictive where you can. Emergency work is valuable, but recurring, contracted service is more valuable and more predictable. If your customers would pay to avoid the emergency, find a way to sell them that.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, build AI with the experts, not for them. In safety-critical work, engineers and technicians are the people whose trust you need. Give them tools that make them faster and better, show them where every suggestion came from, and let them own the review. Then they'll make the tools better.

**NORA:** I'd add one. Don't let growth slow you down.

**JAMES:** That's a good one. A business that sells speed has to make sure each acquisition makes it faster, not slower. Dispatch, shared engineering and a shared knowledge base are how you make scale an advantage rather than a burden.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-enabled entrants. Sensor and monitoring startups could take the predictive slice of the value chain, leaving Colt with only the emergency work. Two, customer insourcing. If AI-guided procedures make some routine repairs easier, larger plants might do more themselves, reducing call volumes. Both of those are our inferences, not things we've seen happen.

**NORA:** Three?

**JAMES:** Three, integration risk. Rapid add-ons can dilute the responsiveness and safety standards that Colt sells. One serious safety incident at an acquired business would hurt the whole brand. Four, cyclicality in oil, gas and petrochemical spending. And five, execution risk on technology. Building a knowledge base or a monitoring service takes investment and focus, in a company whose core skill is field work, not software.

**NORA:** And cybersecurity? If Colt starts connecting sensors on customers' plants, that's a new exposure.

**JAMES:** It's a serious one. Refineries, pipelines, power plants and water systems are critical infrastructure, and their operators are very careful about anything connecting to their networks. A monitoring service has to be designed with security from the start, probably with sensors on separate networks from the plant's control systems, strong access controls, and a clear agreement on who owns the data. Get that wrong, and you don't just lose a contract, you damage the trust that the whole business rests on. Get it right, and a strong security story becomes part of the sales pitch.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** It's the beginning of the hold, so the risk is mostly about setting the right course. Buying a platform in a fragmented market pays off if the add-ons and the operating improvements come through. The upside case, in my view, is a national specialist with a shared knowledge base, faster response and a growing recurring monitoring business. That's a different and more valuable company than a collection of regional repair shops.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, the first add-on under the new owner, and where it is. That tells you the geographic priority. Second, any announcement about ColtCONNECT, new customers, new capabilities, or a subscription offering. And third, any sign of investment in digital systems, a common job platform or a technology leadership hire. Any of those would show the AI story moving from possible to real.
