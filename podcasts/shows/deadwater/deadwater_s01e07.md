# DEADWATER
## Episode 7: "Double Life"
### Season 1

**Duration:** ~35 minutes

[NARRATOR] Twelve days.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] That is how long it takes investigators to search the house on the quiet street in Massapequa Park. The house is modest by any measure - a red Cape Cod, two floors, about fourteen hundred square feet of living space, a basement, a detached garage, a small backyard that backs up against a fence and a neighbor's property. It is the kind of house you would drive past a thousand times without registering as significant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The search takes twelve days because what investigators find inside it requires that kind of time. Not because the house is large - it is not. But because Roland Voss had spent years carefully, methodically, constructing a space that contained two realities in the same physical footprint. And separating those realities, understanding them, documenting them for evidentiary use - that is work that cannot be rushed.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The search team enters at seven in the morning on the day of the arrest. They have their warrant. They have their protocol. They have a forensic team of six people whose job is to document everything before it is touched, everything that is moved, everything that is taken. They have a digital forensics sub-team whose job is specifically the electronic devices. There are, they discover, more of those than they anticipated.

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater, Episode Seven.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The physical search of the house produces evidence in several categories that the prosecution will eventually organize into three pillars of the case against Roland Voss.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The first pillar is materials. Items recovered from the house that are consistent with the binding method used on the victims. Not the same material necessarily - some of it is new, unused, stored. Some of it shows signs of use that forensic analysis will examine in detail. The specific details of what was found and where are subject to ongoing legal proceedings that limit what I can describe here, but the significance of the materials evidence is that it establishes the presence, in Voss's home, of items associated with the crimes.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The second pillar is structural. The house itself. During the search, investigators discover modifications to the basement and to one of the garage walls that are not reflected in the original construction permits. Concealed storage spaces. Access panels that are not visible unless you know where to look. A modified section of the basement floor.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Investigators spend three days on the basement alone.

[REESE] I was not on-site for the physical search - that was a separate team with specific forensic expertise that I am not the right person to lead. But I reviewed the daily evidence logs. And when the basement report came in on day three, I read it twice. The level of construction involved - the specific modifications, the concealed spaces, the evidence that this had been done over time, incrementally, with the kind of patient planning that characterized everything else about the way Roland Voss operated - it was one of the more unsettling things I have encountered in my career.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] He had built himself a specific kind of privacy. Not just the social privacy of someone who keeps his life quiet. A physical, architectural privacy. Applied by someone who knew exactly what he was doing, because building things was his profession.

[NARRATOR] The third pillar is digital.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss has seven electronic devices in the house when the search team enters. Two laptops - one personal, one professional. A desktop computer in what was evidently his home office. A tablet. Two external hard drives, found in a concealed compartment in the basement. And a phone.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Not a smartphone. A basic prepaid handset, new, still in its packaging, hidden in a drawer in the home office beneath a layer of professional documents. The hardware signature matches the physical handset identified in the cell tower analysis.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is the burner phone.

[DR. PARK] When the search team found the handset and transmitted the hardware identification to the task force operations center, I ran the comparison against our analysis within forty minutes. It matched. The device in that drawer is the device that appears in the cell tower records. The device that made contact with victims across multiple years. He kept it. After everything, he kept it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Why he kept it is a question that forensic psychologists will debate in the context of his eventual guilty plea. The most common explanation offered is that the device represented, in some way, a connection to his hidden life that he was not willing to destroy. Others suggest a degree of operational arrogance - a belief, built on twenty years of not being caught, that he was safe enough to keep what he kept.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Whatever the reason, keeping it is a significant error. And Roland Voss, in twenty-plus years of this, has made very few significant errors.

[REESE] The burner phone changes the case in a specific way. Previously we could establish that a handset with this device signature was present in the corridor and in Massapequa Park on specific dates. We could argue, and we could prove statistically, that the behavioral overlap with Voss was too specific to be coincidence. But having the actual physical device in the drawer of his home office is different. That is direct physical possession. That is his hand on it, in his space, with his knowledge.

[MARSH] When the report on the handset came in I allowed myself one moment of feeling something. Just one moment. And then I went back to work.

[NARRATOR] The digital forensics work on the seven devices takes weeks. The material recovered is extensive. The most significant category is what investigators call behavioral mapping - the digital record of Roland Voss's search and communication activity, spanning years, that reveals the shape of his hidden life in a way that no single other source of evidence could.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He has been monitoring the investigation since the discovery of the bodies. This is established through search history and browsing records that date to the first week of press coverage and continue, consistently and methodically, through the present day. Every significant piece of news coverage. Every press briefing statement. Every analytical piece about the case published by a newspaper, a true crime website, a podcast, a television program. He has read it all, and in many cases he has returned to it - re-reading the same articles days or weeks apart, presumably to re-evaluate what they revealed.

[REESE] The monitoring activity is one of the most psychologically detailed things in the digital record. You can map his anxiety against the timeline of the investigation, to some extent, by watching what he reads and when and how often. After the initial discovery he reads everything. After the wrongly reported story claiming investigators have a suspect, his reading gets very concentrated and very specific - he is clearly trying to understand exactly how close investigators actually are. And then, over the years as the coverage thins and the investigation visibly slows down in public reporting, the reading relaxes. He still monitors. But less intensively. With something that looks, in the data, like confidence.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] In the period after the task force is constituted - which is publicly announced, because task force formation at that scale cannot be kept quiet - the reading intensifies again. He has noticed. He is paying attention. And in those months, between the public announcement of the task force and his arrest, there is a period of what I would call heightened alertness in his digital behavior. More frequent monitoring. Some changes in routine that the E-ZPass data reflects. He knows something is happening. He does not know what.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] He does not know it is already over.

[NARRATOR] Among the other digital evidence recovered from the devices: financial records that track purchases of prepaid SIM cards over a period of years - purchased in cash at various retail locations, never at the same place twice, documenting the operational discipline with which he managed the burner phone rotation. Communication records from encrypted platforms that investigators are able to decrypt with warrants and technical assistance from federal law enforcement. Professional files from his architecture work that, in several instances, contain correspondence related to projects in the geographic area of the crimes - projects that gave him legitimate professional reasons to be near the barrier island corridor on specific dates, providing cover for his presence there.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He had thought of this. The professional trips to the area. The legitimate reason to be on the road. He had arranged his professional work to provide cover for his personal movements, in case anyone ever asked.

[MARSH] He had a cover story for everything. Not a lie - a carefully constructed overlap between legitimate and hidden activity that made every individual element of his behavior explicable without the context of the whole. You would have to see the whole picture to understand what you were looking at. That is exactly why it took twelve years to see it.

[NARRATOR] The arraignment takes place five days after the arrest. Roland Voss enters the courtroom in a gray prison uniform. He is led to the defense table. He sits down. He does not look at the gallery where, in the public seating section, three family members who drove to the courthouse that morning are sitting.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His attorney, Mitchell Graves, is one of the most experienced criminal defense lawyers in the metropolitan area. He has been retained within hours of the arrest. He is sixty-two years old, silver-haired, and possessed of the particular kind of authority that comes from being very good at something for a very long time.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He enters a plea of not guilty. He requests bail on the grounds that his client has been a law-abiding resident of the community for twenty-five years with no prior criminal history. The prosecution argues strenuously against bail. The judge denies it. Voss is remanded to custody.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] As he is led out, he passes within twelve feet of the family members in the gallery. He does not look at them.

[GRAVES] In representing Roland Voss, I had certain professional and legal obligations that I fulfilled to the best of my ability. I want to say one thing clearly: whatever my client did or did not do, every person accused of a crime in this country is entitled to the strongest legal defense available to them. I provided that. What the evidence and the legal proceedings ultimately showed is a separate matter.

[NARRATOR] Graves files his first significant motion three weeks after the arraignment. The motion challenges the admissibility of the forensic genealogy evidence - the family tree analysis that identified Voss as a candidate before the direct DNA comparison from the pizza crust was obtained.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The challenge has legal substance. Forensic genealogy raises genuinely complex questions about consent and privacy. The people in the commercial ancestry database who are matches for Voss's relatives consented to having their DNA analyzed for ancestry purposes. They did not consent to have it used as a resource in a criminal investigation. The legal framework governing this use is still being developed.

[TULLY] We anticipated the challenge and we had prepared for it extensively before we sought the warrant for the genealogical analysis. Every step of the genealogy process was covered by a warrant reviewed and approved by a federal magistrate. The privacy questions are real and they are worth having as a society, but in this case the process was legally proper and we were confident it would be upheld.

[NARRATOR] The admissibility challenge becomes one of the central legal battlegrounds of the case, taking over two years to resolve. The court ultimately rules the genealogical evidence admissible. The ruling cites the warrant process, the specificity of the investigative application, and the precedent established in comparable cases.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is a significant ruling. And it does not go the way Graves argued it should.

[GRAVES] I continue to believe the legal question around forensic genealogy consent is not fully resolved by the rulings in this case. I think courts will continue to grapple with it. In this specific case, the DNA from the pizza crust - the direct comparison evidence - would have been sufficient on its own to establish guilt. The genealogical analysis was, in effect, a pathway that led investigators to the direct evidence. Whether that pathway was constitutionally sound is, in my view, still a live legal question.

[MARSH] What I know is that without the genealogical analysis, we might not have known to go looking for Roland Voss's DNA in a pizza box. The pathway mattered. The evidence it led to matters. And the families of ten women who waited twelve years for an answer deserved both of those things.

[NARRATOR] Eight months after the initial arrest, new charges are filed. The ongoing investigation has, since Voss's arrest, been working backward through the full timeline of his activity. The physical evidence from the house, the digital records, and additional forensic analysis of remains from the original discovery have together established connections to victims not charged in the initial complaint.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Sandra - the earliest confirmed victim, whose remains were estimated at fifteen to twenty years old at the time of discovery - is now fully connected to Voss through a combination of physical and DNA evidence recovered from the house search. She is formally charged.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Two additional victims, whose remains were found in locations adjacent to the main discovery site in the years following the initial search, are connected through forensic genealogy and physical evidence. They are charged.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The confirmed victim count rises to eight women, spanning a period of murders from 1993 to 2011. Eighteen years. He had been doing this for eighteen years before the bodies were found. He had been doing it for twelve more years, in complete concealment, while the investigation ran at low resource around him.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Thirty years.

[REESE] When the count reached eight, and when the full timeline was established from 1993 forward, I went home and I sat in my kitchen for a long time. She has always understood that this case lives in the house with us, in a way that is real and that does not fully go away just because we are not at the office.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Eight women. Thirty years. I thought about every year on that timeline and what it contained. Cases I had worked. Cases Carol had worked. Promotions. Department reshuffles. ## Extended Act: Inside the House

[NARRATOR] The search of the house in Massapequa Park takes twelve days. The forensic team enters at seven in the morning on the day of the arrest and does not complete their work until nearly two weeks later. In those twelve days, every square foot of the house is documented, every item of potential evidentiary value is photographed in place before being touched, every surface that can hold a print or a trace is processed.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The house is fourteen hundred square feet of living space. The forensic team generates over four thousand pages of documentation from those fourteen hundred square feet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This is not because the house contains obvious evidence of the crimes in the way that a careless criminal's house might. It is because Roland Voss built his hidden life with the same meticulous, patient attention to detail that he brought to his professional work. Understanding what he built required understanding the architecture of the concealment as completely as the concealment itself.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The basement modifications are the most significant structural finding. The basement of a standard Cape Cod house in suburban Long Island is a utilitarian space - utilities, storage, maybe a finished rec room in houses where someone has invested in the improvement. Voss's basement is different. Not dramatically different in appearance - it would not look unusual to a casual observer. The modifications are in the details. The specific placement of certain structural elements. The access panels behind fixed shelving that are not reflected in any permit filing. The section of floor that is not original.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The forensic team spends three days on the basement. What they find there is subject to ongoing proceedings that limit what I can describe specifically. What I can tell you is that what they find is significant and that it is consistent with someone who used that space deliberately and who took extensive measures to ensure that what happened there was not visible.

[REESE] I reviewed the basement evidence logs every evening during the twelve days of the search. Each evening the picture got more complete and more specific. By day five I had a clear enough understanding of what was in there that I had to put the report down and walk around the block before I could continue reading it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The digital evidence is extensive and takes weeks to fully analyze. Among the most significant categories: financial records that document years of prepaid SIM card purchases, always for cash, never at the same retail location twice. The purchase pattern spans over a decade and matches, in timing, the periods of victim contact documented in the cell tower records. This is the methodical record of someone who understood that prepaid SIM cards leave a trace if purchased carelessly and who spent a decade purchasing them with specific measures to minimize that trace.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is the same approach he brought to everything. Patient. Systematic. Applied with the professional discipline of an architect who understands that the details are where buildings succeed or fail.

[DR. PARK] When I first reviewed the full digital evidence archive, the thing that struck me most was the consistency. The same operational approach applied to every aspect of the hidden life, year after year, without significant deviation. SIM card rotation. Cash transactions. Access timing that avoided the periods of highest observation. The pattern in the data looks less like criminal behavior and more like professional process management. He was managing his hidden life the way he managed his projects. With systems. With documentation. With regular review and adjustment.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The monitoring of the investigation is the clearest expression of this. For years - from the first news coverage through the day of the task force formation - he tracked the investigation with the systematic attention of an analyst. Reading everything. Re-reading the things that were most informative about the investigation's direction. Adjusting his behavior when the coverage suggested intensification. Relaxing when the coverage suggested things were quiet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He was an excellent reader of the investigation's public posture. He correctly identified several periods of intensification and correctly identified several periods of genuine stagnation. What he could not read - what no amount of public coverage would have told him - was the eleven-page memo Carol Marsh was writing in her private hours, and the thread book Danny Reese was filling with thin observations that would eventually mean something, and Dr. Nina Park's annual evidence audit, and Donna Kelsey's testimony in Albany.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The invisible parts of the investigation. He could not monitor what he did not know existed.

## Extended Act: The Architecture of a Double Life

[NARRATOR] I have spent a considerable amount of time over the past three years trying to understand how Roland Voss built his double life and how he maintained it for as long as he did. Not in a morally curious way - I am not interested in humanizing him or finding the sympathetic frame for what he did. I am interested in the mechanics. In understanding the specific cognitive and behavioral architecture that made this possible, because understanding it is necessary for understanding why he was so difficult to find.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The forensic psychologists I consulted for this series use various clinical frameworks to describe him. The one that appears most consistently across multiple expert opinions is extreme compartmentalization - a cognitive structure in which different areas of life are treated as literally separate realities, with no contamination between them. The professional self and the domestic self and the hidden self exist in separate compartments. Each compartment has its own logic, its own emotional register, its own rules. They do not bleed into each other.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This is different from hypocrisy, which involves knowing that what you are doing contradicts what you claim to believe. In the compartmentalized structure, the compartments do not reference each other. The Roland Voss who coached youth soccer on fall Saturdays was not performing the role of normal man while concealing his awareness of what he also was. In that compartment, at that moment, he was a youth soccer coach. Completely.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The practical behavioral consequence of this is that nothing leaked. Nothing showed at the seams. The colleagues who described him as serene were not being fooled by a performance. They were seeing the professional compartment operating exactly as designed. The neighbors who found him considerate were not being deceived. The considerate neighbor compartment was real.

[REESE] When I try to explain this to people - colleagues, family, anyone - I usually get the same reaction: that can't be right, that's too complete, there must have been something that showed. And I understand the instinct behind that reaction. We want there to be a tell. We want the monster to have been visible somewhere. Because if he wasn't visible, that means monsters can be invisible, and that is genuinely terrifying.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] But he was invisible. The psychological profile we built after the arrest is consistent with someone for whom the compartmentalization was so complete that the emotional processing of the hidden life did not intrude on the other compartments. He was not carrying guilt in a way that produced behavioral symptoms that could be observed externally. If anything, the opposite - the extended period of impunity had produced a confidence that manifested as the serenity his colleagues described.

[NARRATOR] The physical architecture of the house supports this. The modifications to the basement, the concealed storage, the access panels - these are not improvised solutions to a problem of concealment. They are planned, professional, deliberate. They are the work of an architect who applied his professional skills to the specific problem of maintaining the separation between his compartments in physical space as well as cognitive space.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He built his own concealment. He designed it. He executed it with the same methodical competence he brought to his professional projects. The house that looked like any other house on that block was, to someone who knew how to look, a building with a specific hidden architecture.

[MARSH] That is one of the things that I find most distinctively disturbing about this case. That he used his professional knowledge - the knowledge he was paid to have and apply and develop over twenty-one years at a legitimate firm - to build the infrastructure of what he did at home. The skills were the same. The approach was the same. The context was the only difference.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Graves, the defense attorney, challenges the genealogical evidence in a motion filed three weeks after the arraignment. The challenge is serious - not frivolous, not a delay tactic. The constitutional questions it raises about genetic privacy and the consent framework of commercial ancestry databases are real and significant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The question at the heart of the challenge is this: when millions of people submit their DNA to commercial ancestry services, they consent to having their genetic information analyzed for the purpose they signed up for. They do not consent to having it used as a resource for law enforcement purposes. Forensic genealogy uses the overlap between those millions of profiles and an unknown contributor's partial profile to build a family tree that points at the contributor. In doing so, it reaches into the private genetic information of people who never agreed to be part of a law enforcement database.

[GRAVES] This is a genuinely important constitutional question and I believe courts will continue to grapple with it long after the Voss case is resolved. The ruling in this case found the evidence admissible, citing the warrant process and the precedent from other jurisdictions. I disagree with that conclusion. I think the consent framework question has not been fully resolved. But I want to be clear that my disagreement is about legal principle, not about what the evidence showed once it was admitted.

[TULLY] We anticipated this challenge from before the arrest. The warrant applications for the genealogical analysis were written specifically to address the constitutional questions. They were reviewed by a federal magistrate who scrutinized them carefully. The ruling that the evidence is admissible did not come easily or quickly - it came after a rigorous Frye hearing that lasted months and that required us to present the scientific validity of the methodology in exhaustive detail.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] The question of whether forensic genealogy requires a broader statutory framework is a legitimate policy question that legislatures should address. The question of whether it was properly used in this specific case, with this specific warrant process, in this specific legal framework, was answered by the court. The answer was yes.

[NARRATOR] The ruling in the Voss case has been cited in subsequent forensic genealogy proceedings in four states. It establishes, in the jurisdictions that have followed it, a framework for the use of the methodology that requires warrant approval and specific documentation of the investigative necessity. Whether that framework is ultimately sufficient is a question that is still working its way through the courts.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is a question worth following. Because the technology that identified Roland Voss is not going away. It will become more powerful. The databases it relies on will grow. The questions it raises about genetic privacy and consent will become more pressing, not less. The Voss case is not the end of this legal conversation. It is close to the beginning.

[MUSIC STING]

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I can also tell you that I sat in my car for a long time after each of those calls before I drove home.
