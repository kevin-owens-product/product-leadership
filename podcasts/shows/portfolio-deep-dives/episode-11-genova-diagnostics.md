### The settlement and why compliance comes first

**NORA:** You mentioned the company's history makes the rules impossible to ignore. Let's talk about that.

**JAMES:** Yes. In April 2020, the US Department of Justice announced that Genova had agreed to pay up to forty-three million dollars to resolve allegations about claims for medically unnecessary tests and issues under the Stark law. The structure was about seventeen million dollars paid, with up to twenty-six million more contingent. And Genova entered a five-year Corporate Integrity Agreement with the Office of Inspector General at the Department of Health and Human Services.

**NORA:** Explain those terms for us.

**JAMES:** As general background: the Stark law is a federal law that restricts doctors from referring patients for certain services to entities they have a financial relationship with, in government health programmes. Medically unnecessary testing refers to billing government programmes for tests that weren't needed. And a Corporate Integrity Agreement is a formal compliance programme a company agrees to as part of a settlement, with obligations like training, reviews and reporting, for a set period. I'm describing these terms in general; the specifics of Genova's case are in the government's documents.

**NORA:** Five years from 2020 would take you to around 2025.

**JAMES:** On that simple arithmetic, yes, but we didn't confirm its current status, so I won't say it has ended. And either way, the lesson stays. For a lab, how tests are ordered, billed and described is a serious compliance matter.

**NORA:** Why does that matter for AI?

**JAMES:** Because one of the most obvious AI uses in a lab like this is helping clinicians interpret results and decide what to do next, which can include suggesting further tests. If an AI tool were to nudge clinicians towards ordering more tests, that would raise exactly the kind of questions the settlement was about. So any AI here has to be designed with compliance at the centre, not bolted on.

**NORA:** So compliance isn't a side note. It's the design brief.

**JAMES:** That's my view. A lab with this history that builds AI carefully, with clear rules, can actually make that a strength. A lab that rushes it could revive old risks.

**NORA:** Does the settlement still shape the business today?

**JAMES:** We can't say from public sources how. ### What functional testing is

**NORA:** Let's go into the product. What is functional testing, and how is it different from what my regular doctor orders?

**JAMES:** In general terms, routine lab tests, the ones a regular check-up includes, focus on standard measures used to diagnose and monitor common conditions. Functional or specialty testing tends to look at broader panels of markers, aiming to describe how body systems are working, for example digestion, nutrient status or immune responses. It's often ordered by functional medicine and integrative practitioners. And as we said, how much weight to put on some of these tests is debated in medicine, and we're not taking a position.

**NORA:** What are Genova's best-known tests?

**JAMES:** From the company's product lines, two that come up a lot are GI Effects, a digestive and stool panel, and NutrEval, a nutritional assessment. There's also Microbiomix, which looks at the gut microbiome, and Metabolomix Plus. And now from Cell Science Systems, Alcat and CICA, which relate to immune and food-sensitivity testing. Genova's site also highlights whole genome sequencing, or WGS, which reads a person's full genetic code, with what it calls AI-empowered pathway insights.

**NORA:** That's a lot of product names.

**JAMES:** It is. And that's one of the challenges we'll come to. Overlapping product lines from acquisitions can make it harder for clinicians to know what to order, and harder for the company to present results consistently. That's an inference, but a common pattern.

**NORA:** Who pays for these tests?

**JAMES:** The research suggests that insurance coverage is weak for much of this kind of testing, so cash payment by patients matters a lot. That's an inference from the channel and from how the company prices consumer collection. And the 2020 settlement shows that where government reimbursement is involved, the exposure is real.

**NORA:** Talk me through the actual process. How does a test happen?

**JAMES:** Roughly, and generally: a clinician orders a test. The patient provides a sample, which might be collected at home with a kit, at a draw site, or by a mobile phlebotomist who comes to them. The sample is shipped to the lab. The lab runs the analysis, checks quality and produces a report, which goes to the clinician, who interprets it and discusses it with the patient. The report is where all those markers show up.

**NORA:** What does quality mean in a lab like this?

**JAMES:** In general terms, accuracy and consistency. The lab has to show that its tests measure what they claim, that results are reproducible, and that samples aren't mixed up or degraded. CLIA certification involves standards for personnel, quality control and proficiency testing, where labs are checked against known samples. That's background on the framework, not a statement about Genova's specific results.

**NORA:** And turnaround?

**JAMES:** Turnaround time matters a lot to practitioners and patients. A complex stool panel or genetic test takes longer than a routine blood test, but the faster and more predictable the turnaround, the better the experience. Shipping, logistics and lab scheduling all affect it.

**NORA:** And now there are two labs, Asheville and Florida.

**JAMES:** Right, plus the London presence. Running multiple labs means coordinating where each test is done, keeping quality consistent and presenting results in one format. Integrating Cell Science Systems' tests under the Genova brand means the practitioner should see one coherent experience, even if the sample goes to a different building.

**NORA:** And the mobile phlebotomist part is new.

**JAMES:** That's the Getlabs partnership. Getlabs sends trained phlebotomists, people who draw blood, to patients' homes. In October 2026 it expanded its partnership with Genova to cover more tests: Alcat, IgG food antibodies, a celiac panel and genetic tests related to digestive and celiac conditions. Getlabs covers thirty-five states, and charges a ninety dollar collection fee at booking, separate from the price of the test. Genova's own page says coverage is strongest in metro areas and expanding.

**NORA:** Let's do the reality check. Where is Genova with AI?

**JAMES:** Here's what we found. One phrase on the homepage: AI-empowered WGS pathway insights for truly personalised care. That's linked to its whole genome sequencing offer. But the page doesn't explain what the AI does, what data it uses, or how it was validated.

**NORA:** And beyond that?

**JAMES:** Nothing public. No AI hires, partnerships, product launches or statements in 2024 to 2026 news. The 2026 announcements about the chief commercial officer and Cell Science Systems make no AI claim. We didn't see a visible technology or product leader in public sources either.

**NORA:** So, in plain terms?

**JAMES:** An AI claim on the homepage, without a described feature. And a real data asset underneath: more than three hundred biomarkers and genomics products, with decades of results. But no visible AI programme.

**NORA:** What might "pathway insights" from whole genome sequencing actually mean?

**JAMES:** I'll talk generally, because the company doesn't explain. Whole genome sequencing produces an enormous amount of data per person, far more than anyone can read by hand. ### Opportunities

**NORA:** Opportunities. Where would you push?

**JAMES:** First and biggest, AI-assisted report interpretation for practitioners. Panels like GI Effects and NutrEval return many markers per patient. An AI layer that drafts a clear summary of the results, highlights what's most notable, and links to the relevant clinical education, could save practitioners time and help them get more value from each test. The practitioner always makes the decisions.

**NORA:** You'd want to be careful about suggesting next steps, given the history.

**JAMES:** Exactly. In my view, an interpretation assistant should explain results, not sell tests. It shouldn't recommend additional testing in a way that could look like generating orders. Any reference to further testing should follow clinical guidance, be reviewed by compliance, and be the clinician's call. That's a design rule, not an afterthought.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Order-to-result automation. Getlabs handles scheduling, collection and handoff, and results then flow through Genova's normal workflow. Automating intake, sample tracking and result release would cut cost per test and speed up turnaround. That's an inference, but it's where labs in general find efficiency.

**NORA:** Give me a concrete example of the automation idea.

**JAMES:** Take intake. When samples arrive, they need to be matched to the right order and patient, checked for completeness and logged. Errors there, a missing form, a mislabelled tube, cause delays or rejected samples. Software that reads requisition forms, checks them against orders and flags problems before the sample reaches the bench could reduce those errors. Then sample tracking, so everyone knows where each sample is. And result release, where routine checks could be automated with humans reviewing exceptions.

**NORA:** That's not glamorous.

**JAMES:** It's not, but it's where labs make or lose money, in general. Every rejected sample or delayed result costs money and goodwill. And unlike interpretation, it doesn't touch clinical decisions, so it's lower risk. A good place to build AI skills.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Cross-selling within the expanded menu. The Cell Science Systems immune and food-sensitivity tests can be offered alongside GI Effects, which the brand integration supports. Done appropriately, so that clinicians can order what's clinically relevant from one place.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Pricing and packaging. Clearer bundles and a clear structure for consumer collection fees. The ninety dollar collection fee separate from the test price is clear in one sense, but patients may find it confusing to combine. Making the total cost easy to understand is good for trust.

**NORA:** And commercial?

**JAMES:** The new chief commercial officer's stated goal is to expand commercial capabilities. An obvious step, in my view, is building sales analytics: understanding which practices order what, where growth is coming from and where clinicians need more education. That's data and analytics rather than generative AI, but it's valuable. Plus international expansion, since the site already has a UK and international selector, and more acquisitions, following the Metametrix and Cell Science Systems playbook.

**NORA:** You mentioned international. How real is that?

**JAMES:** It's an inference from the site's UK and international selector and the London presence. In my view, it's a later step, after the US channels and the Cell Science Systems integration are working well. **NORA:** And acquisitions?

**JAMES:** The playbook is visible: buy a lab, add its tests to the menu, put them under the Genova brand. More tuck-ins are plausible. The discipline, in my view, should be to integrate each one properly before the next, given the compliance stakes and the existing product sprawl.

**NORA:** How would you sequence?

### The best AI bet and the first 100 days

**NORA:** One AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** AI-drafted interpretation summaries for practitioners on the big panels, starting with GI Effects and NutrEval, with compliance controls built in from day one. The value lever is practitioner time per case and the usefulness of the test. Why: the panels are data-heavy, the value lies in interpretation, and Genova already sells through practitioners.

**NORA:** Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: governance first. Bring compliance, the lab director and clinical education into the project from the start. Write down what the assistant may and may not do: it summarises and explains results in the context of the report; it doesn't diagnose, doesn't recommend treatment and doesn't push additional testing. Check the regulatory position, including whether any part could be treated as medical device software. Decide which data can be used and how patient privacy is protected.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Build and test internally. Use historic, appropriately de-identified reports to generate draft summaries. Have Genova's clinical education team review every one. Measure accuracy: does the summary reflect the report correctly, does it overstate anything, does it miss anything important? Fix the problems. And design the output so every statement points back to the specific result it's describing.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** A small pilot with willing practitioners, clearly labelled as a draft aid. Ask them: did it save you time, was it accurate, did it help you explain results to patients? Track any concerns. At day one hundred, decide on a wider rollout, with the compliance team signing off. And publish an AI policy for clinicians and patients explaining what the tool does and doesn't do.

**NORA:** How would you know it's working?

**JAMES:** I'd track four kinds of measure. Accuracy: in clinical review, how often did the summary misstate or omit something important? That should be very rare before any rollout. Usefulness: practitioners' ratings and how often they open the summary. Time: practitioners' estimate of time saved per report, and the volume of interpretation questions coming to the support team. And compliance: a regular review confirming the tool hasn't influenced ordering in ways it shouldn't, for example by comparing ordering patterns before and after.

**NORA:** That last one is unusual. Measuring that the AI didn't increase sales.

**JAMES:** It is unusual, and in this case I think it's exactly right. Given the history, being able to show that the tool improves understanding without driving inappropriate ordering would be a powerful proof point, for regulators, for practitioners and for any future buyer.

**NORA:** And what would make you stop?

**JAMES:** Any pattern of serious errors, or any sign that practitioners treat the summary as a substitute for reading the report. In that case, pause, fix the design, perhaps make the summary shorter or more clearly tied to the report, and only then continue.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** A clinical leader and a product or technology leader, jointly, with compliance having a clear veto. We didn't see a public technology leader, so this may need a hire. And the chief executive should sponsor it, because, given the company's history, the board will care.

### A clinician's report with AI, a hypothetical

**NORA:** Let's replay Dr Patel's moment with the assistant. Still hypothetical.

**JAMES:** Sure. Laura's results come in. Dr Patel opens the report on the portal. Alongside the usual pages of markers, there's a one-page draft summary, clearly labelled as an AI-generated aid for the clinician, to be reviewed before use.

**NORA:** What does it say?

**JAMES:** In this imagined version, it groups the findings by area: digestion, inflammation, microbiome, food reactions. For each, it says in plain clinical language which results are outside the reference range and by how much, with a link to the exact line in the report. It notes which findings are borderline. And it links to Genova's own educational material on what each marker measures and its limitations.

**NORA:** Does it say what to do?

**JAMES:** No. That's the deliberate design. It doesn't diagnose, doesn't prescribe and doesn't recommend more tests. It explains. Dr Patel reads it in three minutes, checks two of the flagged results in the full report, and has a clear picture to combine with what she knows about Laura.

**NORA:** And for Laura?

**JAMES:** Possibly, a patient-friendly version that Dr Patel can choose to share, explaining in plain language what each section of the report measures. Again, no diagnosis, no advice, just explanation, with Dr Patel deciding whether to use it.

**NORA:** What's the business result?

**JAMES:** If it works, practitioners spend less time decoding reports and more time with patients. They feel more confident using the test. Patients understand their results better. And because the assistant is designed not to drive extra orders, the compliance team can be comfortable with it. Over time, that's the kind of thing that makes a practitioner stay with a lab.

**NORA:** And for Genova internally?

**JAMES:** The same technology can help the clinical support team, who answer practitioners' questions about results. Drafting responses, finding the relevant education material, spotting common questions. That's a lower-risk internal use that builds experience.

**NORA:** And the genomics side, where the homepage already mentions AI?

**JAMES:** That's a natural place to apply the same principles. If the whole genome sequencing insights use AI, Genova should explain clearly how, what's been validated and what the limits are. Clarity there would make the AI claim far more credible.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Genova?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, let your history shape your AI design. A company that has been through a major compliance settlement knows exactly where its risks are. That knowledge should be written into the AI rules from day one. The same goes for any business with a sensitive past: build the guardrails where you know the cliffs are.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, the value is often in the interpretation, not the data. Genova produces a lot of data per test. The customer's real problem is making sense of it. Many businesses that generate data, from labs to analytics firms, can add the most value by helping customers understand it.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, be specific about AI claims. "AI-empowered" on a homepage is easy to write. In regulated sectors especially, a precise description of what the AI does, how it's checked and what it doesn't do is far more credible, and far safer.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, convenience is a strategy. The Getlabs partnership shows that how a customer gets a service, a phlebotomist at your door, can matter as much as the service itself. Look for the points of friction in your customers' journey and remove them, whether or not AI is involved.

**NORA:** I'd add one: product sprawl is a hidden tax.

**JAMES:** Good one. Every acquisition that adds products also adds complexity for customers and staff. Simplifying the menu, or at least how it's presented, can be as valuable as adding to it.

**NORA:** Any lesson about hiring?

**JAMES:** One. Genova's recent visible hire is commercial. That makes sense for growth. But if a company wants credible AI, someone senior has to own technology and data, and be visible. Hiring a commercial leader without a matching technology leader can leave the sales team promising things the product can't yet deliver. That's a general pattern, not a claim about Genova's internal team, which we simply can't see.

**NORA:** So balance the hires.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Growth, technology and compliance all need a seat at the top table, especially in healthcare.

**NORA:** And for the private equity world?

**JAMES:** In a very long hold, there's a temptation to focus only on the exit. But buyers will look at the foundations: compliance, data, technology. In healthcare especially, a carefully governed AI capability could be a real asset in the story, while a vague claim could be a question mark.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks. Give me the list.

**JAMES:** One, AI disruption to the revenue model. If consumer platforms or AI interpretation tools deliver similar insight from cheaper, standard blood panels, the premium for specialty testing could erode. Two, reimbursement and regulatory risk, which the 2020 settlement made very concrete. Three, scale competitors, Quest and Labcorp, adding functional panels and out-spending on consumer channels. Four, dependence on consumer platform partners like Getlabs, which may also serve competitors. And five, an AI tool that goes wrong, whether by misstating results or by appearing to drive unnecessary orders.

**NORA:** What about patient data privacy?

**JAMES:** It's another important risk. A lab holds very sensitive health information, including, with whole genome sequencing, genetic data. Any AI work has to keep that data protected under HIPAA and related rules, use it only for permitted purposes, and be clear with patients and clinicians about how it's used. Using historical data to train or test AI tools needs particular care around de-identification and consent.

**NORA:** And the partners?

**JAMES:** Right. Data shared with collection partners or technology vendors must be governed too. In general, the more partners in the chain, the more places data can leak. Strong contracts, minimal data sharing and regular reviews are the basics.

**NORA:** Going back to your fifth risk, an AI tool that goes wrong. That seems especially serious here.

**JAMES:** It is, given the history. Which is why I've been so insistent that compliance must have a veto. In healthcare, one bad headline about AI can undo years of trust.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** The exit story after thirteen years. A buyer will want to see a clean compliance record, a well-integrated test menu, growing practitioner and consumer channels, and a credible plan for technology and AI.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, how the Cell Science Systems tests are integrated into the Genova brand and menu. Second, what the new chief commercial officer does to expand reach, including any new partnerships. Third, whether Genova describes its AI more specifically, especially around whole genome sequencing or report interpretation. And fourth, any sign of an ownership change, given the long hold.

**NORA:** And the Getlabs coverage?

**JAMES:** Yes. Whether at-home collection extends beyond metro areas, and to more of Genova's tests. That would tell you how serious the consumer convenience push is.
