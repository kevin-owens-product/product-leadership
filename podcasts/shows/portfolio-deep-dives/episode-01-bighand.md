### Who BigHand is

**NORA:** Let's start with the company itself. Where did BigHand come from?

**JAMES:** BigHand started life in the mid nineteen-nineties as a voice and dictation company. Its own site says it was founded in 1996, and the private equity firm's page says 1995, so call it the mid-nineties. The original product was digital dictation for lawyers. If you've ever seen a partner dictating a letter for an assistant to type up, BigHand built the digital version of that workflow.

**NORA:** Which sounds a long way from AI pricing.

**JAMES:** It does, but the thread is actually consistent. Dictation is about work moving from a lawyer to someone who supports them. Once you're managing that handoff, you naturally get into task management, delegation, who's busy and who isn't. And once you know who is doing what, you're sitting on data about time and effort. Pricing is the next step along that chain.

**NORA:** So what does the product line look like today?

**JAMES:** BigHand lists around sixteen solutions, grouped into what it calls people, document and financial productivity. On the people side there's workflow and delegation, resource management, the tools that help a firm figure out which associate or support person should take on which task. On the document side there's document creation and automation. And on the financial side there's matter pricing and budgeting, time and billing analytics, and business intelligence for partners and finance teams.

**NORA:** Sixteen products is a lot.

**JAMES:** It is, and we'll come back to that, because it's both a strength and a weakness. Breadth means BigHand touches a lot of the firm. But many of those products came in through acquisitions, and stitching them into one coherent experience is real work.

I'd treat those as company claims, and we've seen slightly different percentages on different pages of its own site. But the direction is clear. This is a vendor with deep penetration into large, established law firms on both sides of the Atlantic.

**NORA:** Where is it based?

**JAMES:** Headquartered in London, with offices in Chicago, Seattle and Sydney. And the leadership team, according to its 2026 announcements, includes Sam Toulson as chief executive and Rob Stote as chief product officer. Stote led the Ayora deal, which tells you that the product organisation is driving the AI strategy.

**NORA:** And size?

**JAMES:** That's one of the gaps. Revenue isn't public. An older section of its website said more than three hundred and fifty employees, but that dates from around 2022, so it's stale. A buyer in a future sale will want to see AI that has actually shipped and is producing revenue, not just a strategy deck.

### The problem BigHand solves

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer problem. What's actually hard about running a law firm?

**JAMES:** More than outsiders think. A large law firm is essentially thousands of highly paid professionals selling their time, with a huge support operation behind them. The partners own the business, but most of them are lawyers first and managers second. And the economics depend on a few things that are surprisingly hard to measure. How much work is actually being done. How long things really take. Whether a matter is profitable. Whether the right person is doing the right task.

**NORA:** Give me an example.

**JAMES:** Take delegation. A partner needs a document drafted, a set of court papers prepared and a research memo written. In a lot of firms, that work gets handed out by email or by walking down the corridor. There's no central view of who's overloaded and who's free. Junior lawyers can be swamped while others are underused, and secretarial and support teams get tasks from ten directions at once. BigHand's workflow tools put structure around that. Tasks get logged, routed, tracked and reported.

**NORA:** And the pricing side?

**JAMES:** Pricing is the bigger prize. Clients have pushed back on open-ended hourly billing for years. General counsel want certainty. They ask for fixed fees, capped fees, phased budgets. Collectively these are called alternative fee arrangements, or AFAs. And when a firm agrees a fixed fee, the economic risk moves from the client to the firm. If the work takes longer than expected, the firm eats the cost.

**NORA:** So the firm needs to know how long similar work took in the past.

**JAMES:** Exactly. And here's the surprising part. Many firms don't know that very well. BigHand's own 2023 research found that only thirty-nine percent of firms could give clients matter-level time information. The data exists in time entries, but it's messy. Narratives are inconsistent, tasks aren't coded properly, matters are opened under the wrong categories.

**NORA:** Garbage in, garbage out.

**JAMES:** That's literally how Ayora pitched itself. Its argument was that firms' historic matter data is too messy for AI to use directly, so the first step is cleaning and enriching it. Only then can you build a reliable pricing model on top.

**NORA:** So BigHand's customer problem has two layers. Run the work better day to day, and price the work better up front.

**JAMES:** That's a good summary. And the two layers feed each other. Better workflow data makes better pricing possible. Better pricing makes the workflow data more valuable.

### Inside the product

**NORA:** Let's go a bit deeper on the product. Walk me through how a firm might actually use BigHand.

**JAMES:** Picture a mid-sized commercial law firm. A partner lands a new deal for a client. On the workflow side, the partner or their assistant creates tasks in BigHand: draft this, review that, prepare these schedules. Those tasks get routed to the right team, whether that's a document production unit, a secretarial pool or a junior lawyer, based on capacity and skills. Everyone can see what's waiting, what's late and what's done.

**NORA:** And the support teams see the queue rather than a pile of emails.

**JAMES:** Right. And in November 2025 BigHand added something that's a nice illustration of practical AI. It's called AI Email Routing. Support teams often have a shared inbox where requests arrive in free text. "Can someone format this agreement by three o'clock." AI Email Routing reads those emails, works out what's being asked, creates the task and routes it to the right person. BigHand made it available to cloud customers without an extra licence fee.

**NORA:** That's a very unglamorous use of AI.

**JAMES:** And I love it for that reason. It saves time on something people do hundreds of times a day, the data is right there in the inbox, and the risk is low because a human still does the actual work. That's the profile of a good first AI feature.

**NORA:** What about pricing?

**JAMES:** On the pricing side, a partner preparing a fee proposal would open BigHand's matter pricing and budgeting tool. It helps them build a budget by phase and task, using historic matters as a reference. Back in 2023, BigHand added AI-enabled timecard analysis to that product, which looks at the free-text narratives on time entries and classifies the work, so you can see what actually happened on past matters even if the coding was poor.

**NORA:** And Ayora goes further.

**JAMES:** Ayora adds a data enrichment layer that cleans and structures historic matter data, plus what it calls an AI Pricing Agent that helps produce a price or budget. In May 2026 the two companies announced a partnership to integrate it into BigHand's Matter Pricing and Budgeting. In August BigHand bought Ayora outright. At the time of the acquisition, the integrated capability was described as coming "later this year", so as of our recording, we haven't seen evidence it's fully in customers' hands.

**NORA:** So the AI pricing story is partly shipped and partly still to come.

**JAMES:** That's the honest picture. Timecard analysis from 2023 and email routing from 2025 are shipped. The agentic pricing assistant is the big next step, and it's the one that matters most for the investment case.

**NORA:** Then there's business intelligence.

**JAMES:** Yes. The Iridium acquisition brought BI tools designed for law firms, so partners and finance teams can see utilisation, realisation, work in progress, how matters are tracking. In BigHand's 2026 framing, the shift is from backward-looking reporting to decisions made before and during a matter. Not just telling you that a matter overran, but warning you while there's still time to do something.

### Why vertical software holds on in legal

**NORA:** Before we get to competitors, I want to ask a broader question. In a world where general AI assistants can draft documents and summarise emails, why does a specialised legal software company still matter?

**JAMES:** It's the question every vertical software company is being asked right now. My answer for legal has three parts. The first is workflow specificity. Law firms have very particular ways of working. Conflict checks, matter opening, delegation rules between partners and support teams, time recording in six-minute units, billing guidelines that differ by client. A general assistant doesn't know any of that out of the box. Specialist software encodes it.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** The second is the system of record. BigHand isn't just a tool people use, it's where the tasks, the time and the pricing history are stored. Whoever holds the record of how work was done is in the best position to apply AI to it. General assistants are brilliant at language, but they need data to reason about, and in a law firm that data lives in systems like BigHand's.

**NORA:** And third?

**JAMES:** The third is trust and accountability. When a law firm signs a contract with a specialist vendor, it gets a supplier that understands legal confidentiality obligations, has been through the firm's security review, and can be held to account. That matters a lot in a profession where a data mistake can end careers.

**NORA:** So the general assistant becomes a front end, and the specialist system becomes the brain with the memory.

**JAMES:** That's a nice way to put it. In fact, I'd expect the smart move for BigHand over time is to make its data and AI available inside the tools lawyers already use. If a partner lives in Outlook and Word, the pricing agent should be reachable from there. You don't fight the general assistant, you plug into it, and you bring the firm-specific data and logic it lacks.

**NORA:** Is there a risk in that? Making yourself invisible?

**JAMES:** There is, which is why the brand and the value need to be clear. But I think the bigger risk is the opposite: insisting that lawyers come to your application when the rest of their day is somewhere else. Adoption follows convenience.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** Let's talk market. How big is legal practice management software?

**JAMES:** I'm going to be careful, because we didn't find a reliable, current market size figure for BigHand's specific niche, and I don't want to quote a number we can't stand behind. What we can say is that legal technology overall has been one of the hottest areas for AI investment over the past couple of years, with a lot of venture money going into AI tools for legal research, drafting and review.

**NORA:** Who does BigHand compete with?

**JAMES:** It depends on the product line, which is part of the challenge of a sixteen-product suite. In broad practice management and financial systems, the big names in the industry are firms like Intapp, Aderant and Thomson Reuters with its Elite products, and Clio in the smaller-firm market. That's from general industry knowledge rather than anything BigHand says about itself. In document automation there are many specialists, some of which have been acquired by bigger platforms. And in pricing specifically, there's a wave of AI-native startups trying to do exactly what Ayora does.

**NORA:** And the horizontal threat.

**JAMES:** The horizontal threat is general-purpose AI. If every lawyer has Microsoft Copilot or a similar assistant in their email and documents, some of what BigHand does at the edges, triaging emails, drafting standard documents, could start to feel like a feature of the office suite rather than a separate product.

**NORA:** So what's BigHand's defence?

**JAMES:** Two things, in my view. First, depth in the specific workflows of law firms. A general assistant doesn't know your firm's delegation rules, your resourcing model or your pricing policies. Second, and more important, data. BigHand has years of time, task and matter data inside thousands of firms. That's very hard for a horizontal tool to replicate, and it's exactly what an AI pricing model needs.

**NORA:** Assuming firms let it be used that way.

**JAMES:** That's an important caveat. Law firms are very protective of their data, and for good reason. Any AI built on it has to respect confidentiality, client obligations and each firm's control over its own information. But within a single firm's own data, the opportunity is very real.

### The billing-model shift

**NORA:** I want to spend some time on the big industry shift, because I think it's the key to this whole episode. How is AI changing the way law firms charge?

**JAMES:** It's genuinely unsettled. BigHand published a survey in September 2026 on pricing and budgeting trends. It was vendor-sponsored and we didn't see the sample size, so take it as directional. But the findings are fascinating. About twenty-nine percent of firms said they had changed how they price AI-augmented work. Fifty-three percent said clients would pay the same fee where AI improves outcomes. Twenty-six percent expected a premium, and seventeen percent expected clients to pay less.

**NORA:** So there's no consensus at all.

**JAMES:** None. And it gets more interesting. Thirty percent of firms said they keep AI efficiency gains internally, meaning they don't pass the savings on. Twenty-five percent offer discounts where clients permit AI. And when asked about barriers to discussing AI with clients, thirty-five percent cited partner discomfort and thirty-one percent regulatory concerns.

**NORA:** That's a market in the middle of a negotiation with itself.

**JAMES:** That's well put. And here's why it matters for BigHand. If billing stays purely hourly and AI reduces the hours, firms lose revenue unless they raise rates or change the model. If they move to fixed fees, they need very good data on what work really costs. Either way, the firms that understand their own matter economics will win, and the ones that don't will either leave money on the table or take on fixed fees that lose money.

**NORA:** So the shift actually increases demand for BigHand's pricing products.

**JAMES:** In the short to medium term, I think so. When the old pricing model is breaking, tools that help you build the new one become more valuable. That's the thesis behind the Ayora deal.

**NORA:** But there's a flip side.

**JAMES:** There is. A lot of BigHand's analytics value comes from timecards. If fixed fees and subscriptions eventually replace hourly billing for big chunks of work, lawyers may stop recording time in such detail. Then the time-based data asset becomes less rich over time. So BigHand needs to evolve from time data to work data, what was actually done, by whom, with what effort, whether or not it's billed by the hour.

### AI at BigHand today

**NORA:** Let's take stock of where BigHand actually is with AI right now. Strip out the marketing.

**JAMES:** Okay. Shipped and in customers' hands, as far as public evidence goes: AI-enabled timecard analysis in the pricing product, from 2023, and AI Email Routing for cloud customers, from November 2025. Bought and being integrated: Ayora's data enrichment and pricing agent, acquired August 2026. Stated intent: extending AI into resource management and business intelligence.

**NORA:** And who's leading it?

**JAMES:** The chief product officer, Rob Stote, is the visible face of the AI strategy. There are also signs of a dedicated AI and data products role, but we couldn't confirm the details from public sources. What we didn't find is a public AI governance page or a published approach to responsible AI, which for a company selling into law firms will become increasingly important.

**NORA:** Why law firms in particular?

**JAMES:** Because law firms will ask hard questions. Where does our data go? Is it used to train models for other firms? Can we explain how a price was generated if a client asks? Is the vendor certified for information security? Large firms run serious vendor security reviews. A clear, published AI governance stance can actually be a sales advantage.

**NORA:** So what's your overall read?

**JAMES:** BigHand is one of the more AI-advanced companies we'll cover in this series. It has real shipped features, a clear thesis and a deal to back it up. The gap is between the story and the revenue. The pricing agent needs to ship, work reliably on real firm data, and become something customers pay for.

### People and change inside the firm

**NORA:** We've talked a lot about software and data. Let's talk about people, because law firms are famously hard places to change.

**JAMES:** They are. A law firm is a partnership. The partners are the owners, they're senior, they're busy, and they've been successful doing things their way. You can't mandate a new pricing tool the way a corporation might roll out a new system. You have to persuade.

**NORA:** So who are the people BigHand has to win over?

**JAMES:** I'd think about four groups. Partners, who make pricing decisions and own client relationships. Pricing and finance teams, who are often the internal champions for better data. Resource managers and support teams, who live in the workflow tools every day. And the associates and junior lawyers, whose time is being allocated and recorded.

**NORA:** What does each of them care about?

**JAMES:** Partners care about winning work and not losing money on it, and about not looking foolish in front of a client. If an AI tool helps them give a confident, defensible price faster, they'll use it. If it produces a number they can't explain, they won't. Pricing teams care about consistency and data quality. They're often the ones who know how messy the time data really is, so enrichment speaks directly to their pain.

**NORA:** And the support teams?

**JAMES:** They care about workload and fairness. AI routing that sends them clearer, better-organised tasks is a win. AI that's perceived as monitoring them is a risk. How the tools are introduced really matters.

**NORA:** And associates?

**JAMES:** Associates care about getting good work, developing their careers and not being buried. Smarter resource allocation can genuinely help them, if it's designed with them in mind. There's also a bigger question in the profession right now about how junior lawyers learn if AI does a lot of the routine work they used to cut their teeth on. That's not BigHand's problem to solve alone, but resource management tools will be part of the answer.

**NORA:** So adoption is a change management project, not just a feature release.

**JAMES:** Absolutely. The vendors that win in legal invest in customer success, training and champion programmes inside firms. BigHand's annual conference, which in 2026 included sessions on matter pricing with Ayora, is part of that. It's where firms share how they're using the tools, and peers convincing peers is very powerful in this market.

### Data, trust and governance

**NORA:** You mentioned governance earlier. Let's dig in, because I think it's going to be one of the deciding factors in legal AI.

**JAMES:** I agree. Law firms hold some of the most sensitive information in the economy: deal terms before they're public, litigation strategy, personal data. They have strict duties of confidentiality to clients, and many clients put conditions on how their information can be used, including with AI.

**NORA:** So what does a firm want to know before it switches on an AI pricing agent?

**JAMES:** At least five things. Where the data is processed and stored. Whether the firm's data is used to train models that other firms benefit from. Which AI models are used underneath and under what terms. How outputs can be explained and audited. And what security certifications the vendor holds. On that last point, we couldn't independently confirm BigHand's certifications from public sources, so that's something a buyer would check directly.

**NORA:** And the training question seems especially important.

**JAMES:** It's the big one. The most valuable model in theory would learn across every firm's data. But firms will almost certainly insist that their data only improves their own pricing, not their competitors'. So the realistic design is firm-specific models or firm-specific enrichment on top of shared general capability. Any cross-firm benchmarking would need explicit consent and strong anonymisation.

**NORA:** Is there an upside to getting this right?

**JAMES:** A big one. A vendor that publishes a clear, strict AI and data policy, and can show it in security reviews, shortens sales cycles. In a market where firms are nervous, trust is a competitive advantage. I'd encourage BigHand to make its AI governance as visible as its AI features.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about the challenges. What keeps BigHand's leadership up at night, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name four. The first is integration and product sprawl. Sixteen products from at least seven acquisitions is a lot of different codebases, data models and user experiences. Every new AI feature is harder to deliver across a fragmented suite. The second is cloud migration. BigHand has said it is moving its portfolio to the cloud, which means there's still a legacy on-premise base. AI features like email routing are cloud-first, so the slower customers move, the slower the AI story reaches them.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Proving Ayora. At the time of the deal, Ayora hadn't published named customers or metrics. That's normal for a small startup, but it means BigHand is buying a capability and a team, not a proven revenue line. The integration has to work in real firms with their real, messy data.

**NORA:** And fourth.

**JAMES:** Pricing BigHand's own AI. It's a bit meta. BigHand's customers are struggling to price AI-augmented work, and BigHand itself has to decide how to price AI features. Email routing was bundled for free for cloud customers, which is a sensible adoption play. But at some point, AI has to show up as revenue, whether through higher subscription tiers, premium pricing modules or new products.

**NORA:** You could say BigHand has to solve the same problem it's helping its customers solve.

**JAMES:** That's exactly right. How do you charge for the value AI creates, when AI is also reducing effort?

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Okay, let's move to opportunities. If you were on BigHand's board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** The first and biggest is the obvious one: get the AI pricing agent live inside Matter Pricing and Budgeting, and sell it across the installed base. About four thousand two hundred customers, many of them large firms under pressure on AFAs. Even a modest share of them upgrading to an AI pricing tier would be meaningful.

**NORA:** How would you prioritise customers?

**JAMES:** I'd start with firms that already use BigHand's pricing product and already do a lot of fixed-fee work. They feel the pain, they have the data, and they can measure the result. The metric I'd care about is pricing accuracy. How close were budgets to actual costs before and after the AI. If BigHand can show firms that their fixed fees became more profitable, that's a very easy story to sell to managing partners.

**NORA:** What else?

**JAMES:** Second, agentic resource management. BigHand has said AI will extend to resource management. Imagine a system that looks at incoming work, skills, availability and deadlines, and proposes who should do what, with the resource manager approving. That saves resource managers hours, and it should improve utilisation and associate experience.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Business intelligence that actually advises. Instead of a dashboard that shows a partner their matters, an assistant that says: these three matters are trending over budget, here's why, here's what similar matters did. That's the "decisions before and during the matter" idea made concrete.

**NORA:** And beyond the product?

**JAMES:** A fourth opportunity is benchmarking. BigHand runs an annual pricing and budgeting survey. With permission and proper anonymisation, aggregated insight across firms could become a paid benchmark product. Firms love to know how they compare to peers. I'd be cautious here, because data rights in law firms are sensitive. But it's worth exploring.

**NORA:** And North America?

**JAMES:** Yes, BigHand has stated a goal of growing in North America, and it already has offices in Chicago and Seattle. The AFA trend is strong in the US, and American firms are huge buyers of legal technology. An AI pricing product could be a strong wedge there.

**NORA:** And M&A?

**JAMES:** More bolt-ons are likely. BigHand has said it will make acquisitions where they make sense for customers. Natural targets would be more AI pricing or analytics capability, or document automation. But I'd say the priority should be integrating what it already owns before buying more.

### The best AI bet

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI use case to bet the next eighteen months on, what is it?

**JAMES:** AI data enrichment plus a pricing agent inside Matter Pricing and Budgeting. It's the use case where BigHand has the strongest right to win. The data is already in its products. The Ayora team and technology are in-house. The market shift to AFAs creates demand. And the value is measurable in pricing accuracy and matter profitability.

**NORA:** How would you run it?

**JAMES:** I'd pick five to ten design partner firms with good historic data and real fixed-fee volume. Run the enrichment on their past matters, then use the agent to propose budgets for new matters alongside the partner's own estimate. Compare the two over a quarter. Where the agent was closer, show it. Where it was wrong, learn why. Only then roll it out more widely, with a clear price.

**NORA:** That's the shadow mode idea again.

**JAMES:** It is. In legal, it's essential. Partners will only trust an AI price if they've seen it be right on their own work. And they'll want to see the reasoning: these past matters, these phases, these adjustments. Explainability is not optional when you're putting a number in front of a general counsel.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** Agentic resource management. It saves time for a specific, identifiable role, resource managers, and it improves something partners care about, which is getting the right people on the right work.

### A fixed-fee proposal, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make the pricing idea really concrete. Walk me through one fixed-fee proposal, today and with the AI pricing agent. An invented example, not a real firm.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's say a corporate partner at a large firm, call her Helen, is asked by a long-standing client for a fixed fee to run the legal work on a mid-sized acquisition. The client's general counsel wants one number by Friday, broken down by phase: due diligence, the purchase agreement, disclosure, completion.

**NORA:** And how does Helen do that today?

**JAMES:** In a lot of firms, she does it from memory and a spreadsheet. She thinks about two or three similar deals she remembers. She asks finance to pull the time recorded on them. Finance sends back a report, but the time entries were coded inconsistently, so it's hard to tell how much went on diligence versus drafting. One of the deals had a messy negotiation that blew the budget, another was unusually smooth. Helen makes a judgment, adds a buffer for risk, and sends a number.

**NORA:** And maybe the number is right.

**JAMES:** Maybe. If it's too high, the client may go elsewhere or push back hard. If it's too low, the firm writes off time at the end and the matter loses money. And because the analysis is so painful, nobody goes back afterwards to learn systematically from the result.

**NORA:** Now replay it with the agent.

**JAMES:** Helen opens the pricing tool and describes the matter: acquisition, this sector, roughly this deal size, these jurisdictions, this client. The enrichment layer has already gone through years of the firm's historic time entries and classified them consistently into phases and tasks, even where the original coding was poor. The agent finds the most similar past matters, not just the two Helen remembers but perhaps twenty, and shows her the distribution: typical hours by phase, the range, and the factors that drove the expensive ones.

**NORA:** So she sees the spread, not just an average.

**JAMES:** Exactly, and that's crucial for a fixed fee. The agent proposes a budget by phase with a suggested price, and explains it. These matters, these phases, this adjustment for the extra jurisdiction. Helen can change any assumption. She might know something the data doesn't, like this client's habit of reopening negotiated points. She adjusts, and the agent recalculates.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** Then the matter runs, and the same system tracks actual effort against the budget phase by phase. If diligence is running hot in week two, Helen gets a warning while there's still time to talk to the client about scope. At the end, the result flows back into the data, so the next estimate is better.

**NORA:** That's a learning loop.

**JAMES:** It's the whole point. Pricing stops being a one-off guess and becomes a system that gets more accurate with every matter. And notice the AI isn't replacing Helen's judgment. It's giving her a much better starting point and an early warning system.

**NORA:** What does that mean financially?

**JAMES:** For the firm, potentially a lot. Write-offs on fixed fees come straight out of profit. If better budgets reduce overruns even a little across hundreds of matters, the money adds up. That's the value story BigHand needs to prove with design partners, with real numbers rather than our hypothetical.

### Lessons for other software businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other software companies, especially those owned by private equity, learn from BigHand?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. The first is that your data may be your AI strategy. BigHand didn't need to invent a new market. It looked at the data its products already collected and asked what AI could do with it. Many vertical software companies sit on similar data and haven't asked that question.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, start with boring AI. Email routing isn't flashy, but it ships, it's useful, and it gets customers comfortable with AI inside the product. That builds trust for the bigger, riskier features like pricing.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, buy when time matters. BigHand partnered with Ayora first, in May, and then bought it in August. That's a sensible sequence. Test the fit through a partnership, then acquire when you're convinced. Buying a small specialist can be much faster than building from scratch, especially when the market is moving quickly.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, integration is where acquisitions succeed or fail. Buying the capability is the easy part. Getting it inside the core product, working on customers' real data, with a clean experience and a clear price, is the hard part. Anyone who has integrated acquired software knows this. The companies that do it well set a clear owner, a short timeline and measurable milestones.

**NORA:** If you were running a hundred-day plan for the Ayora integration, what would be in it?

**JAMES:** First thirty days: pick the design partner firms, agree the success measures, and map exactly how Ayora's enrichment connects to BigHand's pricing data model. Days thirty to sixty: run enrichment on design partners' historic data and have partners review the results, because if the classification is wrong, nothing built on top will be right. Days sixty to a hundred: put the pricing agent in front of a handful of partners on live proposals, in parallel with their normal process, and measure the gap. At day one hundred, decide the general availability date and the pricing model based on evidence.

**NORA:** And who owns it?

**JAMES:** One person, with authority across both teams. Split ownership is the classic way integrations drift.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, commoditisation by general AI tools. If Microsoft and others build good-enough legal workflows into the office suite, BigHand's lighter products come under pressure. Two, billing model change eroding the timecard data advantage over time. Three, clients expecting lower fees. If seventeen percent of firms expect AI to reduce what clients pay, and that number grows, firms' revenue gets squeezed, and that can squeeze their software budgets. Four, execution: integrating Ayora and migrating to the cloud while running a sixteen-product suite.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** Timing. After six years, a sale process at some point is natural. The AI pricing story needs to show traction in revenue to support the valuation a buyer would pay. So speed of execution over the next twelve to eighteen months matters a lot.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, an announcement that the Ayora pricing agent is generally available inside Matter Pricing, ideally with named customers or results. Second, how BigHand prices it: bundled, premium tier, or separate product. Third, whether BigHand publishes an AI governance and data use policy for law firm customers. That would be a sign of maturity.
