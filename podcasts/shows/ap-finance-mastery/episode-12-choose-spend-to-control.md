### THE CANONICAL SPEND MODEL

**KEVIN:** Calder's first task is to define the grain. If we analyze only invoice headers, a single invoice for maintenance services, replacement parts, freight, and tax becomes one category based on the supplier name. That is fast and wrong for action. The canonical model keeps document and line grain, then connects both to supplier, legal entity, category, contract, purchase order, receipt, cost owner, accounting treatment, and payment.

**MORGAN:** Line-level detail matters when the buying decision occurs at the line. But do not promise perfect line classification everywhere. Some invoices arrive with poor descriptions. Some service invoices genuinely cover bundled outcomes. The model records classification confidence and the evidence used. Low-confidence spend remains visible and enters a remediation queue. It does not disappear into “other” and it does not receive a precise savings rate.

**KEVIN:** We separate external spend from intercompany movement before opportunity analysis. Intercompany charges matter to allocation and the consolidated view, but sourcing the same legal group from itself does not create external price leverage. Taxes, statutory fees, payroll, and other nonaddressable flows receive explicit treatment rather than being dropped without lineage.

**MORGAN:** Then distinguish sourceable from controllable. A category can be externally sourced but not controllable within the program horizon because a plant certification, customer specification, regulated component, termination right, or capacity constraint prevents change. Conversely, demand may be controllable even when the supplier is not. Calder may not replace the approved safety-testing provider, but it may reduce unnecessary testing variation or consolidate scheduling.

**KEVIN:** Spend also needs a time contract. Annualized run rate, trailing twelve months, current-year actual, committed future spend, and one-time project spend answer different questions. If a commodity price spiked last quarter, extrapolating that quarter can manufacture opportunity. If a category is seasonal, comparing unaligned months can do the same.

**MORGAN:** Currency needs its own bridge. Calder operates across sixteen countries. Transaction currency, functional currency, and reporting currency can make apparent price variance that is really exchange movement. Preserve local amount, rate, rate date, and translated amount. A sourcing manager can influence supplier price and demand. They do not get credit because reporting currency strengthened.

**KEVIN:** Finally, every record preserves lineage. The dashboard can aggregate, but an analyst must be able to trace a heatmap cell back to the invoice line, supplier cluster, contract, purchase order, and ledger entry that produced it. If the trace stops at an exported spreadsheet, the model is a presentation layer, not governed spend intelligence.

### FROM RAW LABELS TO TRUSTED CLASSIFICATION

**MORGAN:** Calder uses a layered classification process because no single method deserves every decision. Start with deterministic evidence: purchase-order category, catalog item, contract family, account, cost center, supplier commodity, and tax treatment. Deterministic does not mean correct. It means the rule and its input can be inspected. Conflicts between those fields are valuable signals, not reasons to choose whichever source appears first.

**KEVIN:** Then add learned classification for the ambiguous population. Invoice descriptions, line text, supplier history, buyer behavior, and prior corrections can recommend a category. But we evaluate at the level the decision requires. A model can achieve a strong overall score by being excellent on high-volume easy classes while failing a small, high-value or regulated category. The evaluation set must preserve value, risk, geography, language, new suppliers, and rare categories.

**MORGAN:** Corrections need governance too. If an AP user changes a code to make an invoice pass, that is not automatically a high-quality training label. The correction may reflect a workaround, a local accounting convention, or a misunderstanding. Calder distinguishes user action from validated label. Category stewardship reviews recurring disagreement and changes the rule, taxonomy, buying path, or training evidence at the cause.

**KEVIN:** Confidence should control use, not merely color a dashboard. High-confidence classified lines can enter category totals automatically. Medium-confidence lines can enter with a visible range and sampling. Low-confidence lines remain in the unresolved population and can block a sourcing target if they are material. No analyst may improve trusted coverage by lowering the threshold without recording the change.

**MORGAN:** Sampling protects against a second failure. Once eighty-eight percent appears trusted, teams will focus only on the remaining twelve. Calder also samples the trusted population for silent error. Measure both precision—how often assigned categories are correct—and coverage—how much spend receives an acceptable assignment. A ninety-six-percent coverage target at Month Eighteen means nothing if precision erodes.

**KEVIN:** We also retain taxonomy versions. If facilities software moves from general IT to plant operations, historic analysis should explain the remapping instead of restating the past invisibly. Every opportunity record names the taxonomy version it used. That lets finance reproduce why the category baseline changed and prevents a reclassification from masquerading as spend reduction.

**MORGAN:** Finally, classification is not the outcome. Its value comes from a better decision: a renewal surfaced, a demand pattern challenged, a fragmented supplier relationship joined, a contract leak identified, or a cash exposure forecast. A classified line with no plausible action may still help reporting, but it does not automatically deserve transformation capital.

### ENTITY RESOLUTION: WHEN ONE SUPPLIER BECOMES NINETEEN

**MORGAN:** The most dangerous defect is not category classification. It is identity. Calder's maintenance supplier Atlas Industrial Services appears under nineteen records: Atlas Industrial, Atlas UK, Atlas Services GmbH, two acquired legal entities, several abbreviations, and three misspellings. Some are valid legal and remittance distinctions. Some are duplicates. The first dashboard treats all nineteen as unrelated suppliers.

**KEVIN:** That fragmentation understates concentration, hides total relationship value, splits contract compliance, and makes tail spend look larger. But merging them blindly is just as dangerous. Two records may share a trading name while representing different legal entities, tax obligations, bank accounts, sanctions checks, or contracts.

**MORGAN:** Exactly. We need a supplier cluster and a legal-entity identity, not one flat cleanup. The parent relationship supports negotiation and concentration analysis. The legal supplier record supports contracting, tax, payment, and liability. A remittance site supports operational routing. The model can connect them without pretending they are the same object.

**KEVIN:** Calder builds an entity-resolution evaluation set before trusting automation. It includes known duplicates, legitimate subsidiaries, similar names that must remain separate, historic acquisitions, shared bank details with valid reasons, changed tax identifiers, and adversarial lookalikes. Each match proposal gets evidence, confidence, and a review rule based on consequence.

**MORGAN:** Precision and recall have economic meanings here. Aggressive matching may find more duplicates but incorrectly merge distinct suppliers. Conservative matching avoids false merges but leaves fragmentation. For category analysis, a provisional cluster may be acceptable. For bank-data activation, a provisional identity is absolutely not acceptable. The threshold follows the action.

**KEVIN:** This is an important AI-operator habit. There is no universal “good enough” entity-resolution score. The acceptable error depends on whether the output groups spend for research, recommends a sourcing action, changes a supplier master, or directs cash. Evaluation must represent the action boundary.

### THE CATEGORY HEATMAP

**MORGAN:** Once the model can trust category and supplier clusters, Calder scores opportunities across eight dimensions. Addressability asks what portion can actually change. Price and demand variance ask whether value comes from rate, quantity, specification, or consumption. Contract leakage asks whether negotiated terms fail in buying or payment. Supplier risk asks whether intervention increases resilience or threatens it.

**KEVIN:** Implementation capacity asks whether Calder has an owner, data, change bandwidth, and operational path. Confidence asks how much of the value is supported by transaction evidence rather than benchmark assumptions. Time to proof asks whether the first observable result arrives inside the program's stage gate.

**MORGAN:** Do not combine those dimensions into one magical score without preserving the components. A weighted score helps rank. It does not eliminate judgment. High gross opportunity with low confidence and eighteen-month implementation may be a research theme. Moderate opportunity with clear leakage, a willing owner, and ninety-day proof may deserve capital now.

**KEVIN:** Calder's heatmap identifies three candidate families. First, industrial consumables: large spend, many suppliers, meaningful price variance, and moderate switching effort. Second, contingent maintenance labor: lower price transparency, strong demand variance, local relationships, and real capacity risk. Third, tail software subscriptions: fragmented spend, duplicate tools, auto-renewals, and high contract leakage, but smaller total value.

**MORGAN:** The largest category is metals. It represents far more spend than all three. It also follows indexed pricing, customer specifications, long qualification cycles, and supply commitments that Calder cannot safely redesign within eighteen months. Putting it first would create a giant opportunity slide and a weak implementation path.

**KEVIN:** The tail is tempting because supplier count is dramatic. But supplier count is not value. Thousands of low-value transactions may be expensive to manage, yet attempting to source every tail item can consume more procurement effort than it returns. Tail strategy may require channels, catalogs, cards, or aggregation—not thousands of miniature negotiations.

**MORGAN:** The heatmap therefore makes two questions visible: where is economic value, and which mechanism can capture it? Sourcing can change price and terms. Procurement design can change channel and compliance. Product rationalization can change demand. Supplier consolidation can reduce administration or risk. AP controls can stop leakage. Those mechanisms need different owners and evidence.

### THE VALUE FUNNEL: IDENTIFIED IS NOT REALIZED

**KEVIN:** Calder gives every opportunity a stage. Identified means evidence suggests a mechanism and rough addressable base. Qualified means an owner has validated scope, feasibility, dependencies, and cost. Negotiated means a supplier or internal decision has created agreed commercial value. Implemented means the new price, demand rule, buying channel, or process is active for the governed population.

**MORGAN:** Then come validated run rate and realized result. A validated run rate means the implemented action has enough stable evidence to estimate the forward recurring effect under finance's rules. Realized means the effect appears in the measured financial period, net of volume, mix, currency, implementation cost, leakage, and overlap. Cash timing can still differ from P and L timing.

**KEVIN:** Consider a software renewal. The old contract costs one million dollars. Calder negotiates eight hundred thousand. That is two hundred thousand negotiated. If half the users migrate late and Calder pays a three-month extension on the old tool, the first-year implemented economics are lower. If another team buys replacement functionality, demand has moved. Finance validates only the net recurring change supported by contracts, invoices, and the ledger.

**MORGAN:** Or take industrial consumables. A five-percent unit-price reduction on the approved basket is negotiated value. If volume rises because production increases, total spend may go up while price performance improves. Do not tell the board the program failed because spend rose, and do not claim five percent of the higher volume if the agreement covers only part of it. Bridge baseline quantity, price, mix, currency, and scope explicitly.

**KEVIN:** The funnel also records negative value. Switching cost, dual running, inventory write-off, supplier exit fees, implementation labor, and service disruption belong beside gross benefit. A category strategy with three million gross and two point five million cost is not a three-million initiative. Time matters because an eighteen-month program can identify a durable opportunity whose cash arrives after the mandate.

**MORGAN:** Confidence narrows as evidence improves. Identified opportunity may use a range. Qualified opportunity needs a base case. Negotiated value uses actual terms but still carries implementation risk. Validated run rate requires operational and financial proof. The pipeline should shrink without embarrassment. A disciplined funnel destroys weak value before capital does.

**KEVIN:** Ownership changes by stage but accountability stays explicit. Analytics owns detection quality, category leadership owns the commercial mechanism, the business owns demand, implementation owns adoption, and finance validates the result. No opportunity advances because the transformation office changes its status alone.

**MORGAN:** That is how Calder can show eleven point six million dollars of opportunity and zero benefit without sounding evasive. It is saying exactly what it knows, what it does not know, and what evidence will change the classification.

### CONFLICTING EVIDENCE: THE BENCHMARK SAVINGS RATE

**KEVIN:** A consulting benchmark suggests industrial consumables could save seven percent. Applied to Calder's spend, that creates three point eight million dollars of modeled opportunity. The category manager says the benchmark ignores service levels, emergency availability, plant-specific items, and supplier-owned inventory. Finance asks whether we are being too conservative.

**MORGAN:** Treat the benchmark as a hypothesis generator, not a baseline. Rebuild the category from units, specifications, delivered price, rebates, freight, inventory ownership, stockouts, emergency orders, and service. If two suppliers charge different prices for products that are not operationally equivalent, the gap is not automatically savings.

**KEVIN:** Tail software shows the opposite conflict. The raw spend is smaller, but invoice descriptions and cost centers reveal overlapping functionality, inactive seats, and automatic renewals. Procurement can influence renewal timing, but business owners must decide whether demand disappears. Canceling a tool that a team quietly replaces with another budget creates no durable value.

**MORGAN:** Contingent maintenance labor has a third pattern. Rate cards vary, but the larger opportunity may be demand planning and emergency work. A lower hourly rate with slower response can increase plant downtime. Total cost includes the operational consequence, not just the invoice.

**KEVIN:** So the opportunity backlog needs a mechanism sentence. “Consolidate duplicate software and remove inactive demand before renewal.” “Standardize equivalent consumable specifications and competitively source the approved basket.” “Reduce emergency maintenance demand through planning before negotiating labor rates.” If the mechanism cannot be written, the percentage is decoration.

### DECISION GATE: SIZE, TAIL, OR CONTROLLABILITY

**MORGAN:** Choice one: attack the largest categories. The case is materiality. A one-percent improvement on a large category can exceed a ten-percent improvement on a small one. Senior attention and supplier leverage concentrate. Calder would begin with metals and the largest industrial inputs.

**KEVIN:** The risk is confusing spend size with change capacity. Certification, indexation, supply continuity, and customer requirements make much of that spend unavailable inside the program. We could spend six months validating why the headline cannot move.

**MORGAN:** Choice two: attack tail spend. The case is fragmentation. Thousands of suppliers, low contract coverage, high administrative cost, uncontrolled renewals, and poor visibility create obvious process waste. Calder would aggregate the tail and reduce supplier count.

**KEVIN:** The risk is a supplier-count vanity metric. Some tail suppliers are locally critical. Many transactions are too small to justify a sourcing event. Without channel design, demand management, and identity cleanup, consolidation can simply move spend to a different uncontrolled path.

**MORGAN:** Choice three: rank the spend with the clearest controllable value. Use addressability, price and demand variance, contract leakage, supplier risk, implementation capacity, confidence, and time to proof. Fund a mixed backlog rather than one ideological category strategy.

**KEVIN:** The risk is underweighting transformative opportunities because near-term proof looks safer. We need a separate research lane for large, slower categories so the portfolio does not become a collection of easy but immaterial wins.

**MORGAN:** Next, create an entity-resolution evaluation set. **MORGAN:** Fourth, create one backlog row per mechanism, not per dashboard cell. State the owner, gross hypothesis, addressable base, action, cost, timing, confidence, dependency, operational proof, finance proof, and next gate. **KEVIN:** Finally, reconcile the backlog to the CFO value tree from Episode Eleven. One opportunity can influence price, demand, process cost, cash, risk, or supplier resilience. It may affect several, but the same dollar enters the financial bridge once. Write the nonfinancial outcomes separately so they remain visible without becoming invented savings.
