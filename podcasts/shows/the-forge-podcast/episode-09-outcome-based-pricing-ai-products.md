### SEGMENT 1: Why Seat-Based Pricing Breaks in AI-Native Products (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's start at the foundation. Seat-based pricing has been the workhorse of enterprise SaaS for twenty years. It's simple, it's predictable, finance teams love it, customers understand it. So why does it start to fall apart when AI enters the product?

**KEVIN:** The core issue is that seat-based pricing assumes a roughly linear relationship between the number of users and the value a customer extracts from the product. That assumption held pretty well in traditional SaaS. If you had a CRM with fifty salespeople using it, you got roughly fifty units of value. If you added another fifty seats, you got roughly another fifty units of value. The math was proportional, and everyone was comfortable with it. But AI-native products break that proportionality in a fundamental way. When you introduce automation, intelligence, and autonomous task execution, a single user can generate the output that used to require twenty or fifty users. The value is decoupled from the headcount. And that creates a painful misalignment: either the customer is dramatically overpaying relative to their user count, or the vendor is dramatically undercharging relative to the value being delivered.

**ALEX:** Can you give a concrete example of what that looks like in practice?

**KEVIN:** Sure. Think about an AI-native customer support platform. In the old world, you had thirty support agents using a ticketing tool, so you sold thirty seats. Each agent handled maybe forty tickets a day. Now you deploy an AI system that autonomously resolves seventy percent of incoming tickets without any human involvement. Your customer goes from thirty agents to maybe eight, because the AI is handling most of the volume. If you're still pricing per seat, you just lost twenty-two seats of revenue, even though your product is delivering massively more value to the customer. Their resolution times are faster, their CSAT scores are up, their cost per ticket is down. You made them more successful, and you got punished for it financially. That is the fundamental structural problem. The better your AI works, the fewer humans are needed, and the fewer seats get purchased. Your commercial model penalizes your own product's success.

**ALEX:** That's a perverse incentive if I've ever heard one. So the vendor is actually incentivized to keep the product just mediocre enough to require human users?

**KEVIN:** Exactly, and that's not a theoretical concern. I've seen product teams internally resist automation features because they knew it would cannibalize seat revenue. There was a mid-market HR tech company in one of our portfolios where the product team had built an incredible AI-driven onboarding workflow that could reduce the number of HR coordinators a client needed by about sixty percent. The sales team killed it. They refused to ship it because their compensation was tied to seat expansion, and this feature would have contracted their largest accounts. The AI capability sat on a shelf for nine months until we restructured the pricing model. That's what happens when your pricing architecture is misaligned with your product's value delivery. You get internal dysfunction that directly hurts the customer.

**ALEX:** And I imagine this problem only intensifies as AI capabilities improve over time. It's not a one-time adjustment.

**KEVIN:** That's the part people miss. This isn't a static problem you solve once. As your AI models improve, as your automation coverage expands, the gap between seat count and value delivered gets wider every quarter. If you're a PE firm doing diligence on an AI-native platform that's still on pure seat pricing, that widening gap is a ticking time bomb. Either the company will have to reprice and take a short-term revenue hit, or their best customers will figure out they're overpaying and start pushing back on renewals. Neither outcome is pleasant, but the first one is at least manageable if you plan for it. The second one comes with churn, and churn is much harder to recover from. So the question isn't whether to move away from seat pricing, it's when and how to do it in a way that protects the business during the transition.

### SEGMENT 2: The Outcome Pricing Ladder (12 minutes)

**ALEX:** So if seat pricing is broken, what replaces it? I think there's a temptation to jump straight to pure outcome pricing, where you say something like "pay us ten percent of the revenue we generate for you." But I suspect that's too aggressive for most companies as a starting point. How do you think about the progression?

**KEVIN:** You're right that jumping straight to pure outcome pricing is usually a mistake, and I'll explain why in a moment. What I've found works best is what I call the Outcome Pricing Ladder. It has four levels, and each level corresponds to a different degree of maturity in your product, your measurement infrastructure, and your customer relationships. The beauty of the ladder is that it gives you a structured migration path. You don't have to bet the company on a single pricing model switch. You can move customers up the ladder as trust and capability develop. Let me walk through each level.

**ALEX:** Let's do it. What's Level 1?

**KEVIN:** Level 1 is Capability Access Pricing. This is where most AI-native products should start, and it's not that different from what people are already doing, but with a subtle reframe. Instead of pricing per user seat, you're pricing for access to a set of AI capabilities. Think of it as a platform fee plus tiered access to specific AI functions. For example, the base tier might include AI-assisted search and basic recommendations, the mid tier adds autonomous workflow execution, and the top tier includes custom model training on the customer's data. The price scales with the capability tier, not the number of humans using it. This is the easiest transition from seat-based pricing because customers understand capability tiers. They're already used to "Standard, Professional, Enterprise" packaging. You're just removing the per-seat multiplier and replacing it with a capability-based structure. It doesn't fully capture value, but it stops the bleeding from the seat cannibalization problem.

**ALEX:** That makes sense as a first step. It removes the perverse incentive without requiring you to actually measure outcomes. What's Level 2?

**KEVIN:** Level 2 is Usage-Linked Pricing, and this is where you start connecting price to the actual work the AI is doing. Instead of just paying for access to capabilities, the customer pays based on the volume of automated tasks the system executes. This could be the number of support tickets resolved, the number of invoices processed, the number of documents analyzed, the number of leads scored, whatever unit of work your AI performs. The key distinction from pure outcome pricing is that you're measuring activity, not results. You're counting the tasks completed, not whether those tasks achieved a specific business outcome. That's an important distinction because measuring activity is straightforward. You can log every task your system executes with high fidelity. There's no ambiguity about whether a ticket was processed or a document was analyzed. It happened or it didn't. Usage-linked pricing works well when the customer already implicitly understands that each unit of automated work has value. If they were previously paying a human twelve dollars to process an invoice, and your AI does it for two dollars, the value proposition is clear at the unit level even without measuring downstream outcomes.

**ALEX:** And then Level 3 moves into actual outcomes?

**KEVIN:** Level 3 is Performance-Linked Pricing, and this is where it gets both exciting and tricky. At this level, the price is tied to validated performance metrics that both parties agree represent meaningful outcomes. Notice I said validated and agreed-upon. Those qualifiers matter enormously. A performance-linked pricing model might say: we charge a base fee, plus a performance component based on the accuracy rate of automated decisions, or the reduction in average handling time, or the improvement in first-call resolution rate. The metrics have to be specific, measurable, attributable to the AI system, and agreed upon before the contract starts. This is where most companies stumble, because measuring performance sounds simple until you try to do it in a live enterprise environment. Did the resolution rate improve because of the AI, or because the customer also hired three new senior agents and revamped their knowledge base? Attribution gets messy fast, and if you don't have a clear measurement methodology baked into the contract, you end up in disputes that destroy the customer relationship.

**ALEX:** So the trust and measurement infrastructure really has to be in place before you even attempt Level 3. What about Level 4?

**KEVIN:** Level 4 is Shared-Value Pricing, and this is the summit of the ladder. At this level, the vendor's revenue is directly tied to the customer's economic outcomes. You might take a percentage of cost savings, a percentage of revenue generated, or a share of the margin improvement that the AI system enables. This is the most aligned model because both parties benefit from the same outcome. If the AI system drives a million dollars in incremental revenue for the customer, the vendor captures, say, fifteen percent of that. The incentives are perfectly aligned. But and this is a critical caveat, Level 4 only works in relationships where there is deep trust, transparent data sharing, and a track record of reliable measurement. I've seen maybe a handful of companies successfully operate at Level 4 at scale, and they all had years of relationship history with those customers before they got there. This is not where you start. Companies that try to lead with shared-value pricing, particularly startups looking to differentiate, usually end up in contract disputes within eighteen months because the measurement framework wasn't mature enough to support the commercial model.

**ALEX:** So the ladder is really a maturity model. You progress up it as your capabilities, your measurement systems, and your customer trust all develop together. Is there a typical timeline for moving through the levels?

**KEVIN:** It varies by market and customer sophistication, but as a rough guide, most companies spend twelve to eighteen months at Level 1, another twelve months at Level 2, and then selectively move their highest-trust customers to Level 3 over the following year. Level 4 is aspirational for most companies and typically only applies to their top ten or twenty percent of accounts where the relationship and data transparency support it. The critical mistake is trying to skip levels. Every level builds the commercial muscle, the measurement infrastructure, and the customer trust needed for the next level. If you jump from Level 1 to Level 3 because a board member read an article about outcome pricing, you're going to have a very painful twelve months.

### SEGMENT 3: Prerequisites Before Pricing Outcomes (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** You've mentioned measurement and trust several times now, so let's dig into what has to be true before a company can credibly price on outcomes. What are the prerequisites?

**KEVIN:** There are three foundational prerequisites, and I'm going to be blunt: most companies underestimate all three of them. The first is reliable measurement infrastructure. The second is a trust framework between vendor and customer. The third is baseline data. Let me take them one at a time, because each one is deeper than it sounds.

**ALEX:** Start with measurement.

**KEVIN:** Reliable measurement means you can accurately, consistently, and transparently track the metrics that your pricing depends on. That sounds obvious, but think about what it actually requires. You need instrumentation in your product that captures every relevant event. You need a data pipeline that aggregates those events into the metrics your pricing model references. You need that pipeline to be auditable, meaning the customer can verify the numbers independently or through a mutually agreed third party. And you need the whole system to be resilient. If your telemetry drops events during a system outage, and that outage happens to coincide with a high-volume period, you could be systematically underreporting usage or performance in a way that affects your revenue. I worked with a document processing company that moved to usage-based pricing and then discovered that their event tracking had a twelve percent data loss rate during peak processing hours. They were literally leaving money on the table because their measurement infrastructure couldn't keep up with their product's throughput. They had to invest three engineering quarters in rebuilding their telemetry pipeline before they could trust their own pricing data.

**ALEX:** That's a great example. What about the trust framework? That seems squishier and harder to operationalize.

**KEVIN:** It's squishier in concept but very concrete in practice. A trust framework for outcome pricing means you and the customer have explicitly agreed on three things: what gets measured, how it gets measured, and what happens when there's a disagreement. Let me illustrate why this matters with a real scenario. Say you're an AI-powered sales enablement tool, and you've priced on the number of qualified leads your system generates. Sounds clear enough. But what does "qualified" mean? Is it a lead that matches certain firmographic criteria? A lead that the customer's sales team accepted into their pipeline? A lead that resulted in a first meeting? Each definition produces a different number, sometimes wildly different. If you and the customer haven't explicitly agreed on the definition, the measurement methodology, and the data source before the contract starts, you're going to have a dispute at the first quarterly review. The trust framework is the document that preemptively resolves those disputes. It's like a prenup for your pricing model. Nobody wants to write it, everyone thinks they won't need it, and it saves you enormous pain when things get complicated.

**ALEX:** And baseline data is the third prerequisite. Why is that so important?

**KEVIN:** Because outcome pricing inherently involves measuring improvement, and you cannot measure improvement without knowing where you started. If you're pricing on reduction in customer support handling time, you need to know what the handling time was before your product was deployed. If you're pricing on improvement in defect detection rates, you need the pre-deployment defect rate. Without a credible baseline, you're in a "said-she-said" situation where the vendor claims massive improvement and the customer argues the baseline was already better than the vendor assumed. Establishing baselines takes time. You typically need thirty to ninety days of clean pre-deployment data, and the customer has to agree that the baseline measurement methodology is fair. Some customers won't have clean historical data at all, which means you might need to run a measurement period after deployment but before the outcome pricing kicks in. I've seen contracts that include a sixty-day "measurement stabilization" period where the customer pays a flat fee while both sides establish and agree on baselines. That's smart commercial design because it separates the deployment risk from the pricing risk.

**ALEX:** So the implication is that companies need to build all of this before they announce an outcome pricing model. This isn't something you figure out after the press release.

**KEVIN:** Absolutely. And I'd go further. The prerequisite investment is significant enough that it should be explicitly planned and budgeted. You need engineering resources for measurement infrastructure, product resources for the trust framework design, data science resources for baseline methodology, and legal resources for contract templates. I typically tell portfolio companies to budget two to three quarters of dedicated work on prerequisites before they pilot outcome pricing with even a single customer. Companies that skip this step end up in one of two failure modes: either they launch outcome pricing with bad measurement and lose money they should have captured, or they launch it with ambiguous contracts and spend the next year in commercial disputes instead of building product. Neither outcome is acceptable.

### SEGMENT 4: Commercial Contract Design for Outcome Pricing (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about the contract itself. Traditional SaaS contracts are relatively standardized at this point. Everyone knows what a seat license agreement looks like. Outcome pricing contracts are a different animal. What are the key elements that have to be in the contract?

**KEVIN:** Outcome pricing contracts need to address five areas that barely exist in traditional SaaS agreements. The first is the measurement methodology clause. The second is the SLA structure. The third is dispute resolution. The fourth is the adjustment mechanism. And the fifth is the termination and transition provisions. I'll walk through each one because getting any of them wrong can sink the entire commercial relationship.

**ALEX:** Let's start with measurement methodology.

**KEVIN:** The measurement methodology clause is the most important section of the entire contract, and it's the one that most legal teams are least equipped to write. This clause needs to specify exactly what data sources are used, how metrics are calculated, how frequently they're reported, and who has access to the underlying data. You need to be specific to the point of including formulas. If you're pricing on "ticket resolution rate," the contract should literally define the formula: tickets autonomously resolved divided by total tickets received, excluding tickets in categories X, Y, and Z, measured over rolling thirty-day periods using data from the vendor's telemetry system and reconciled monthly against the customer's CRM records. That level of specificity feels excessive when you're drafting the contract, but it's exactly the level of detail you need when a customer calls six months later and says your reported metric doesn't match their internal numbers. I've been in those calls, and the only thing that resolves them cleanly is pointing to the contract and saying "here is the agreed formula, here is the agreed data source, here is the reconciliation process." Without that, you're negotiating in real time with an unhappy customer, and you'll usually end up giving concessions to preserve the relationship.

**ALEX:** What about the SLA structure? How does that differ from traditional SaaS SLAs?

**KEVIN:** Traditional SaaS SLAs are mostly about uptime: the system will be available 99.9 percent of the time, and if it's not, here are the service credits. Outcome pricing SLAs need to be much more nuanced because you're making commitments about performance, not just availability. You might have an SLA that guarantees a minimum accuracy rate for automated decisions, a maximum error rate for processed transactions, or a minimum improvement percentage over the agreed baseline. Each of these SLAs needs a clearly defined measurement window, a clearly defined remedy if the SLA is missed, and a clearly defined escalation path. The remedy structure is particularly important. In traditional SaaS, if you miss your uptime SLA, you issue a service credit, typically a percentage of that month's fee. In outcome pricing, the remedy needs to be proportional to the business impact. If your AI system's accuracy drops below the SLA threshold and the customer has to redirect human resources to handle the fallback, the remedy should account for that operational disruption, not just the subscription fee. Some of the best outcome pricing contracts I've seen include tiered remedies: minor SLA misses trigger service credits, sustained SLA misses trigger fee reductions, and severe SLA misses trigger the right to exit the outcome pricing model and revert to a capability-access price.

**ALEX:** Dispute resolution seems like it would be critical here. How do you structure that?

**KEVIN:** You need a multi-tier dispute resolution process that starts informal and escalates only when necessary. The first tier is typically a joint operational review where the technical teams from both sides examine the data together and try to reconcile any discrepancies. Most disputes get resolved here because they're usually caused by data quality issues, not bad faith. The second tier is executive escalation, where a designated executive from each side reviews the dispute and has authority to make a binding commercial decision. The third tier is independent arbitration, and ideally you've pre-agreed on the arbitration body and process in the contract. The key design principle is speed. Disputes in outcome pricing contracts need to be resolved within thirty to forty-five days, not the six-to-twelve months that traditional commercial arbitration takes. If a pricing dispute lingers for months, the commercial relationship deteriorates even if the dispute is eventually resolved in your favor. I've seen contracts include a "provisional payment" clause where the customer pays an agreed interim amount during the dispute resolution period, which prevents cash flow disruption while the dispute is being worked. That's a sophisticated but very practical provision.

**ALEX:** You also mentioned adjustment mechanisms and termination provisions. Can you cover those briefly?

**KEVIN:** The adjustment mechanism addresses what happens when the underlying conditions change in ways that neither party anticipated. Maybe the customer's business volume doubles because of an acquisition, or maybe a new regulation changes the definition of a compliant transaction. The adjustment mechanism gives both parties a structured way to revisit the pricing parameters without renegotiating the entire contract. Typically this involves annual or semi-annual pricing reviews where both sides can propose adjustments based on predefined trigger events. For termination and transition, the contract needs to address what happens when the relationship ends, particularly how you unwind an outcome pricing model. If the customer wants to leave, what happens to data, what are the notice periods, and crucially, what pricing model applies during the transition period? I've seen ugly situations where a customer gave notice on an outcome pricing contract and the vendor tried to revert them to a seat-based price for the wind-down period that was three times what they'd been paying. That's a relationship-destroying move that also creates legal risk. The transition pricing should be fair and predefined in the original contract.

### SEGMENT 5: Migration Strategies from Seat-Based to Outcome-Based (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Now let's talk about the migration itself. Most companies considering outcome pricing aren't greenfield startups. They have an existing customer base paying seat licenses, sales teams compensated on seat expansion, and a financial model built around predictable recurring revenue. How do you actually make the switch without cratering the business?

**KEVIN:** This is where most pricing transformations fail, not because the new model is wrong, but because the migration was botched. I'm going to describe three approaches I've seen work, in order of increasing aggressiveness, and then talk about the communication strategy that makes or breaks all of them.

**ALEX:** Let's hear them.

**KEVIN:** The first approach is what I call the New Logo strategy. You keep all existing customers on their current seat-based contracts and only offer outcome pricing to new customers. This is the lowest-risk approach because it doesn't disrupt any existing revenue. Your current customer base continues on their existing contracts, which renew normally. New customers come in on the new pricing model. Over time, as renewals come up, you offer existing customers the option to migrate. The downside is that it's slow. If your new logo growth rate is twenty percent annually, it could take four or five years before outcome pricing represents a majority of your revenue. But for companies with large installed bases and PE owners who are sensitive to short-term revenue disruption, this is often the right choice.

**ALEX:** What's the second approach?

**KEVIN:** The second approach is the Cohort Migration, where you deliberately migrate segments of your existing customer base in planned waves. You start with a pilot cohort of five to ten customers who you've identified as ideal candidates, typically customers who trust you, who have clean data, and who are already extracting high value from your AI features. You migrate them to the new pricing model, learn from the experience, refine the contract and measurement infrastructure, and then move the next cohort. Each wave gets smoother because you're incorporating lessons from the previous one. The key to making this work is being genuinely selective about your pilot cohort. You want customers where you're highly confident the outcome pricing will result in equal or higher revenue than their current seat pricing, because the last thing you need is your pilot customers paying less and then telling the rest of your customer base that the new model is cheaper. I've seen that happen, and it turns your migration into a stampede where every customer demands the new pricing because they think it's a discount.

**ALEX:** And the aggressive approach?

**KEVIN:** The third approach is a Hard Cutover, where you announce a date by which all customers will transition to the new model. This is the fastest path, but it's high-risk. You need extraordinary confidence in your measurement infrastructure, your contract design, and your customer relationships. The only time I've seen a hard cutover work well is when the company had already been running parallel pricing, meaning showing customers what they would have paid under the new model alongside their actual invoice, for at least six months. That parallel run does two things: it pressure-tests your measurement systems with real data, and it gives customers time to understand and get comfortable with the new model before it affects their actual spend. If a hard cutover is done without that preparation, you get a wall of customer complaints on the transition date and your customer success team drowns.

**ALEX:** What about the communication strategy? How do you frame this to customers so they don't panic?

**KEVIN:** The communication has to be centered on alignment of value, not on the mechanics of the pricing model. Customers don't care about your pricing architecture. They care about whether they're going to pay more, whether the price will be predictable enough for their budget planning, and whether the new model is fair. The message should be: "We're aligning our pricing with the value our product delivers to you, so that when our AI creates more value for your business, both of us benefit, and when it doesn't, you're protected." You should also provide modeling tools. Give every customer a calculator or a dashboard that shows what their cost would have looked like under the new model over the past six months, using their actual data. Nothing defuses pricing anxiety like concrete numbers. Customers who can see that the new model would have cost them roughly the same, or less, as their current model are dramatically more likely to embrace the transition. And you need to offer pricing protection for the first contract period. A guarantee that says "your annual cost under the new model will not exceed 110 percent of your current annual spend" removes the fear of a cost explosion and gives the customer a reason to say yes.

**ALEX:** That pricing protection idea is clever. You're essentially capping the downside for the customer during the transition.

**KEVIN:** Exactly, and it also caps your upside temporarily, but that's a worthwhile trade. If your product is delivering genuine value, you'll capture that upside in the second contract period when the protection rolls off and the customer has a year of positive experience with the model. The short-term revenue cap is an investment in the long-term commercial relationship. PE firms sometimes push back on this because it limits near-term revenue expansion, but the alternative, which is customer churn driven by pricing anxiety, is far more expensive.

### SEGMENT 6: Impact on Revenue Recognition and Financial Modeling (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's shift to the finance side. Revenue recognition under ASC 606 is already complicated for SaaS companies. How does outcome pricing make it more complex, and what does that mean for the financial model?

**KEVIN:** This is one of the most underappreciated challenges of outcome pricing, and I've seen it blindside CFOs who were fully on board with the pricing strategy but hadn't thought through the accounting implications. Under seat-based pricing, revenue recognition is straightforward: you have a fixed contract value, you recognize it ratably over the contract period, and your revenue is predictable. Under outcome pricing, you have variable consideration, which is ASC 606's term for revenue that depends on future events. Variable consideration requires you to estimate the transaction price at contract inception using either the expected value method or the most likely amount method, and you can only recognize revenue to the extent that it's probable there won't be a significant reversal. In practical terms, this means your recognized revenue in the early months of an outcome pricing contract might be lower than the actual billings because your auditor requires you to constrain the variable portion until you have enough history to estimate it reliably.

**ALEX:** So there's a potential mismatch between cash flow and recognized revenue?

**KEVIN:** Exactly, and that mismatch can be significant. I worked with a company that transitioned a major customer to performance-linked pricing. The first quarter, they billed four hundred thousand dollars based on the actual performance metrics, but their auditor only let them recognize two hundred and eighty thousand because the variable component didn't meet the constraint threshold yet. They had a hundred and twenty thousand dollars sitting in deferred revenue that they'd collected but couldn't recognize. For a growth-stage company being evaluated on ARR multiples, that's a material hit to the headline metric, even though the underlying economics were excellent. The solution is to design your contracts with a structure that makes revenue recognition cleaner. The most common approach is a base-plus-variable structure where sixty to seventy percent of the contract value is a fixed base fee and the remaining thirty to forty percent is the outcome-linked variable component. The base fee is recognized ratably like traditional SaaS, and the variable component is recognized as performance obligations are satisfied. This gives you a stable revenue floor while still capturing the upside from outcome delivery.

**ALEX:** What does this mean for financial modeling and forecasting? PE firms love predictable revenue, and outcome pricing introduces variability by definition.

**KEVIN:** It changes forecasting from a deterministic exercise to a probabilistic one. Instead of saying "we have a hundred million in ARR and our net retention is 115 percent, so next year we'll have a hundred and fifteen million," you're saying "we have seventy million in base fees plus a variable component that we estimate will produce thirty to fifty million depending on product performance and customer volume." That range makes PE firms nervous, and understandably so. The mitigation is portfolio-level thinking. While any individual outcome pricing contract has variability, a portfolio of fifty or a hundred such contracts tends toward predictable aggregate outcomes, the same way an insurance book becomes predictable at scale even though individual claims are unpredictable. I encourage CFOs to model outcome pricing revenue using statistical distributions rather than point estimates, and to present the board with confidence intervals rather than single numbers. A presentation that says "we expect variable revenue of forty million, with a ninety percent confidence interval of thirty-five to forty-six million" is more honest and ultimately more credible than a presentation that claims false precision.

**ALEX:** That's a mature way to handle it. It also seems like it requires a much more sophisticated finance function than most mid-market software companies have.

**KEVIN:** It does, and that's why the finance team upgrade needs to happen in parallel with the pricing model transition. If you're asking a two-person FP&A team that's been forecasting seat licenses for five years to suddenly model variable revenue with confidence intervals, you're setting them up to fail. This is an area where PE operating partners should be proactive about investing in finance capabilities alongside the product and commercial changes. The cost of a senior revenue operations hire or a fractional CFO with consumption pricing experience is trivial compared to the cost of a botched financial model that misleads the board or an investor.

### SEGMENT 7: Real-World Examples and PE Due Diligence (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's bring this to life with some examples. You've worked with a lot of portfolio companies on pricing transitions. Without naming names where you can't, what are some examples of companies that got this right and companies that got it wrong?

**KEVIN:** Let me share three examples that I think illustrate the key lessons. The first is a success story. There was a mid-market accounts payable automation company, roughly forty million in ARR, PE-backed. They were on a traditional seat-based model where customers paid per user in the finance department who had access to the platform. Their AI capabilities had gotten good enough that a single AP clerk could process the work that previously required a team of five. Their net retention was declining because customers were rightfully consolidating seats. The company implemented a phased migration over eighteen months. They started by repackaging their tiers around capability access, which is Level 1 on the ladder. Six months later, they introduced a usage component based on the number of invoices processed autonomously, which moved them to Level 2. They offered every existing customer a six-month parallel billing period where customers could see both pricing models side by side. Eighty percent of their customer base voluntarily migrated within twelve months because the usage-based model was actually slightly cheaper for most customers at current volumes, but it gave the vendor massive expansion upside as AI processing volumes grew. Their net retention went from 104 percent under the old model to 128 percent under the new model within two years, because as customers processed more invoices through the AI, revenue grew without any seat expansion.

**ALEX:** That's a clean execution. What about a failure case?

**KEVIN:** The second example is a cautionary tale. An AI-powered sales intelligence platform, roughly twenty-five million ARR, tried to jump straight from seat pricing to Level 3 performance-linked pricing. They offered customers a pricing model where the fee was tied to the number of qualified opportunities the AI identified that converted to pipeline. The concept was sound. The execution was terrible. They didn't have reliable attribution. Their system would flag a lead, but then a salesperson would also work that lead through traditional outreach, and when it converted, both the AI and the human channel claimed credit. They didn't have a baseline measurement period, so they were arguing with customers about what the conversion rate would have been without the AI. And their contracts didn't have a dispute resolution mechanism beyond "let's get on a call and talk about it." Within nine months, their three largest customers were in active billing disputes, their customer success team was spending seventy percent of their time on pricing arguments instead of product adoption, and one customer escalated the dispute to legal. They ended up reverting to seat pricing with a usage kicker and lost about eight months of productive customer relationship time. The pricing concept would have worked if they'd spent twelve months building the prerequisites first.

**ALEX:** And the third example?

**KEVIN:** The third example is interesting because it shows how PE firms can drive pricing transformation as a value creation lever. A PE firm acquired a document intelligence company that was entirely on per-seat pricing. During diligence, the PE firm identified that the company's AI extraction accuracy was in the top decile of the market but their pricing didn't reflect it at all. As part of the hundred-day plan, the PE firm brought in a pricing consultant and a revenue operations leader. They rebuilt the measurement infrastructure, designed a Level 2 usage-based model with a pathway to Level 3, restructured the sales comp plan to reward expansion through usage growth rather than seat addition, and ran a six-month pilot with a dozen customers. The results were striking. Average contract value for pilot customers increased thirty-five percent because the customers were processing far more documents through the AI than the seat count would have suggested. The company's implied price per unit of value actually decreased for customers, meaning customers were paying more total but getting even more value per dollar, which is the ideal outcome. That pricing transformation was the single largest driver of EBITDA growth in the PE firm's hold period and added meaningfully to the exit multiple.

**ALEX:** That's a powerful illustration of pricing as a value creation lever. How do PE firms typically evaluate pricing model transitions during due diligence? What are they looking for?

**KEVIN:** PE firms evaluating AI-native companies should be asking five questions about pricing during diligence. First, is the current pricing model aligned with the product's value delivery mechanism? If there's a visible gap between how the product creates value and how the company charges for it, that's either a risk or an opportunity depending on whether the company has a credible plan to close the gap. Second, what measurement infrastructure exists? Can the company reliably track usage and outcomes, or would that require significant investment? Third, what percentage of revenue is at risk if customers optimize their seat count? This is the cannibalization analysis, and it's critical for AI-native companies. If thirty percent of seat revenue is at risk because the AI reduces the need for human users, that needs to be reflected in the valuation model. Fourth, is there evidence of pricing power? Do customers renew at rate increases, or is there margin pressure at renewal? AI-native products with strong outcome delivery should have pricing power, and if they don't, the pricing model is probably the constraint. Fifth, what is the company's pricing roadmap, and is it credible? A company that says "we're moving to outcome pricing next year" but hasn't invested in measurement infrastructure or contract design is not credible. A company that's in month six of a phased migration with pilot data is very credible. The difference between those two scenarios can be worth multiple turns of EBITDA in a transaction.

**ALEX:** That's a thorough framework for diligence. Before we wrap up, any final thoughts on where this all goes? Is outcome pricing the inevitable end state for all AI-native software?

**KEVIN:** I believe that some form of outcome-aligned pricing will become the dominant model for AI-native products over the next five to seven years. The economic logic is too strong to resist. Customers will increasingly demand to pay for value delivered rather than for access granted, and vendors who resist that shift will lose to competitors who embrace it. But the transition will be uneven. Companies that invest in the prerequisites, build the ladder, and execute phased migrations will capture enormous value. Companies that chase the concept without building the foundation will damage their businesses. And PE firms that understand how to evaluate and drive pricing transformation will find it to be one of the most powerful value creation levers available in AI-native software portfolios. The key takeaway for anyone listening is this: outcome pricing is a commercial architecture problem, not a pricing page problem. Treat it with the same rigor you'd apply to rebuilding your product architecture or your data infrastructure, because it requires that level of investment and that level of care to get right.
