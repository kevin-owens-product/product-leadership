# DEADWATER
## Episode 2: "Ocean Parkway"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] Before Roland Voss was an architect, he was a beach kid.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He grew up on the south shore of Long Island in the 1970s and 80s, in a modest house in a modest neighborhood where summers meant the ocean and winters meant watching the shore road empty out. He knew every access point. Every parking lot. Every trail through the dunes. He knew where the surveillance cameras were and, more importantly, where they weren't.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He worked a summer job at the beach complex on the barrier island when he was in his early twenties - ticketing, parking enforcement, light maintenance. He drove the service roads that ran behind the dunes when the park was closed. He knew which stretches of the marsh grass grew tallest, and where.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This is not speculation. This is in the court record. And it is the first thing you need to understand about Roland Voss: he was not random. He was never random. Everything he did was the product of specific knowledge, applied with patient deliberation over decades.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. You're listening to Deadwater.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In the weeks following the discovery of the bodies, investigators begin a painstaking process of mapping the case geographically. Every victim. Every known last location. Every cell tower ping. Every credit card transaction.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What emerges is a pattern.

[REESE] The stretch of road where the bodies were found is about four miles long, east to west. The victims were not clustered - they were spaced. Deliberately spaced. The placement shows intimate knowledge of the terrain and clear advance planning. You don't stumble onto this location. You scout it. You return to it. You learn it the way you learn your own neighborhood.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] And when we started tracking back the victims' last known locations - where they were picked up, which cell towers their phones last pinged - we found something that stopped us cold. All of them, every single one, had their last contact originating within a roughly twenty-mile radius of the dump site. He wasn't driving long distances with the bodies. He was local. He was living here, or close enough that it made no difference.

[NARRATOR] Detective Danny Reese spent the first three months of the investigation doing what he calls "threading the map" - building a geographic and temporal picture of the killings that could be overlaid with population data, employment records, and property ownership in the target area.

[REESE] It's slow work. It's the kind of work that doesn't look impressive in a briefing room but it's the foundation of everything else. If you know where someone operates, you can start to narrow down who they are. Geography is identity, in cases like this.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The earliest murder investigators can tie to the same killer, based on physical evidence and method, dates to 1993. A partial set of remains found in a wooded area in a neighboring county, previously unsolved, matches the DNA profile and binding method of the later Gilgo victims. The woman is eventually identified as Sandra - thirty-one years old, a mother of two. She has been unidentified for seventeen years.

[MARSH] When we identified Sandra and understood that she was the earliest confirmed victim, we had to reset our entire picture of the timeline. We were looking at almost two decades of killings from the same person. That is not someone who made a terrible choice once. That is someone who built this into his life. Who maintained it alongside everything else he was doing. Who went home after and had dinner and slept and went to work the next morning.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] That understanding changes how you investigate. It tells you you're looking for someone with extraordinary self-control. With compartmentalization that most people can't even comprehend. And it tells you he almost certainly presents as completely normal. Because he has had to, for twenty years.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey, whose sister Renee is identified as one of the victims, begins attending every public briefing the SSPD holds in the months after the discovery. She drives down from upstate New York, sometimes twice a week. She sits in the front row and she takes notes.

[DONNA] People kept telling me to go home. That there was nothing I could do. That I was just putting myself through more pain. And I understood where they were coming from. But I couldn't leave. Renee was down there. The answers were down there. And I had already spent sixteen months being told to wait and look what waiting had gotten us.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] At the briefings they'd give us almost nothing. Which I understood - it was an active investigation, they couldn't show their hand. But at one briefing, early on, I noticed something. A detective in the back of the room - not Carol, a different one - was looking at a map on his laptop. And I could see just enough of it to know it was a map of the highway corridor with points marked on it. And the points were not all where we'd been told the bodies were found. There were points further east. Out toward where the highway bends south.

[NARRATOR] Donna doesn't say anything at the briefing. But afterward she drives east on the highway and she stops at the point she thinks she saw marked on the map.

[DONNA] It's a parking area. Empty in December, completely deserted. I just sat there in my car and I looked at the marsh and I thought, what did you see, Renee? At the end. What were you looking at?

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She is right that there are more markers. Investigators later confirm that the search radius was quietly extended to include additional areas east of the initial discovery site. Some of those areas will yield further evidence in the years ahead.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey is not a detective. She has no law enforcement training. But she has three things that turn out to be invaluable over the thirteen years of this investigation: she is tireless, she is precise, and she refuses to let anyone forget.

[DONNA] I became a pain in the ass. I make no apology for that. My sister deserved someone who would be a pain in the ass on her behalf.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] By spring of the first year, investigators have identified a critical piece of evidence that they do not publicize. A male hair, found on one of the victims. Intact enough for DNA analysis. Not a match to any profile in the national database. But preserved. Filed. Waiting.

[REESE] In those early years, we didn't know what that hair would eventually mean. We knew it was significant. We knew it could, under the right circumstances, be compared to a suspect sample and give us a definitive answer. But you need a suspect first. You need something to compare it to. And we didn't have that yet.

[NARRATOR] Dr. Nina Park is a forensic DNA specialist who joins the investigation three years in, recruited specifically for her expertise in what was then an emerging field: forensic genealogy - using partial DNA to trace family trees and identify suspects whose profiles don't exist in any criminal database.

[DR. PARK] When I first reviewed the hair evidence, I was struck by how well-preserved it was. Whoever was responsible for the initial processing and storage had done exceptional work. We had enough genetic material to potentially run a genealogical analysis, but the technology to do that effectively was still being developed. I told the investigation team: this evidence is our best lead. Protect it. When the science catches up, we will have something.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] The science caught up about six years later. And the evidence was exactly where we'd left it.

[NARRATOR] This is one of the things about cold cases that people outside of investigative work often don't understand. Patience is not passive. Patience is active and strategic and sometimes it is the most important investigative decision you make - knowing what to preserve, what to wait for, and what to keep pressing on in the meantime.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh presses on.

[MARSH] I ran thirty-seven interviews in the first year. Worked every tip the line generated that had any substance to it. Ran background checks on registered sex offenders in the target area. Ran checks on men who'd been employed along the barrier island complex going back twenty years. I was building a list. A very long list of people who knew that area well enough to use it the way this killer did.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Roland Voss was not on that list yet. He should have been. Looking back, there are records that should have connected him to that beach complex, to that service road, to that whole geography. But we were pulling from the wrong databases and asking the wrong questions. You don't always know which questions are wrong until you find out, years later, what the right ones were.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Meanwhile, the public investigation is generating noise. A lot of it. Tips continue to come in for months after the initial discovery - thousands of them, from well-meaning people and from the not-so-well-meaning. An anonymous caller claims to know the killer personally. The tip is traced to a phone booth in Queens and leads nowhere. A man in New Jersey contacts the department claiming his neighbor matches the profile. The neighbor turns out to be a retired gym teacher with a spotless record.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A local journalist - not me, a different reporter working the story in those early years - publishes a story claiming investigators have a strong suspect. They don't. The story damages the investigation by sending the actual killer into a period of heightened caution that investigators will later describe as a "behavioral freeze." He does not kill again for several years. At the time, investigators don't know that. They think they may have scared him off entirely. They haven't.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss reads that story. He is sitting in his office in Manhattan - a mid-size architecture firm where he has worked for years, a reliable, meticulous professional with a reputation for handling difficult projects with patience and precision. His colleagues will later describe him as "quiet" and "private" and "always at his desk." He reads the story, and then he goes back to work.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is not scared. He is careful. There is a difference.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Three years in, the investigation hits a wall.

[REESE] It happens in cold cases. It happened here. The tips slow down. The resources get reallocated. The department is running twenty-three other active homicide investigations. The Gilgo case - we called it Deadwater internally, after the stretch of water behind the barrier island - goes into what I'd call a maintenance phase. A skeleton crew keeps it moving, but "moving" is generous. We're standing still and calling it movement.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I want to be clear: nobody gave up. That's not what happened. The file was never closed. The evidence was never stored and forgotten. But the intensity dropped. And intensity matters in this kind of work. Intensity is what generates the friction that produces leads.

[MARSH] I kept my own file. Separate from the official one. Notes, theories, connections I wanted to track that didn't fit neatly into the case management system. My husband used to call it my obsession binder. He wasn't entirely wrong.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey keeps calling. Keeps attending every briefing she can get into. Starts connecting with other victims' families - a loose network of people united by loss and by the grinding frustration of not having answers. She starts a blog. She testifies before a county committee pushing for more resources on the case. She drives to Albany and sits in a waiting room for three hours to speak to a state legislator's aide for twelve minutes.

[DONNA] People kept asking me, aren't you exhausted? And yes, I was exhausted. I was absolutely exhausted. But the alternative was stopping. And stopping meant Renee was just gone. Just a file somewhere that no one was actively looking at. I couldn't do that. I would not do that.

[NARRATOR] She does not stop.

[PAUSE]
