### The regulation machine

**NORA:** Let's talk about the problem cleversoft solves. Why is regulation such a big burden for financial firms?

**JAMES:** Because there's so much of it, and it keeps changing. A European insurer, for example, has to report its capital position to its regulator under Solvency II, which is the European framework for how much capital insurers must hold against their risks. It has to apply the IFRS 17 accounting standard for insurance contracts. It may have to report on sustainability under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, CSRD. And under DORA, the Digital Operational Resilience Act, it has to show it can withstand technology disruptions and cyber attacks.

**NORA:** That's four acronyms already.

**JAMES:** And that's just the insurer. An asset manager deals with the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation, SFDR, which governs how funds describe their sustainability credentials. A firm selling investment products to retail customers deals with PRIIPs, which requires short, standardised key information documents. Investment advice falls under MiFID II, the main European rules for investment services. Switzerland has its own equivalent, called FIDLEG, and Germany has its own securities trading law. Pension funds have IORP, a European directive for occupational pension schemes. And everyone deals with anti-money laundering rules.

**NORA:** Let's take one of those as an example. DORA. What does it actually require?

**JAMES:** As general background, DORA is a European Union regulation that has applied since January 2025. It requires financial firms to manage technology risk properly: to have frameworks for identifying and handling information technology risks, to report major incidents to regulators, to test their resilience, and to manage the risks from their technology suppliers. That last part is interesting, because it means banks and insurers have to scrutinise vendors like cleversoft more closely.

**NORA:** So regulation creates demand for cleversoft, and also raises the bar for cleversoft as a supplier.

**JAMES:** Exactly. A vendor selling into European financial institutions now has to be able to show its own resilience, security and incident handling. In our view that favours vendors with mature processes and can be hard for very small specialists, which may be one more reason the market is consolidating.

**NORA:** And sustainability reporting? I've heard that's been in flux.

**JAMES:** It has, as general background. The European Union introduced extensive sustainability reporting requirements, and there has since been political debate about simplifying them and changing timelines. For vendors, that kind of flux is double-edged. Uncertainty can make customers delay purchases. But every change, including a simplification, still has to be implemented in the software, which is work customers pay for.

**NORA:** And all of these are in cleversoft's product set?

**JAMES:** According to its product listings, yes, across its four domains. That breadth is a big part of the pitch. One vendor that can cover several of these regimes, rather than a different specialist for each.

**NORA:** Why can't firms just do it themselves?

**JAMES:** Some large firms do build parts in-house. But it's expensive and never-ending. Every regulation comes with detailed technical specifications: which data points, which formats, which validation rules. Regulators update them regularly. If you build in-house, you need a team that reads every update, works out what it means, changes the calculations, tests them and gets them live before the deadline. For a mid-sized insurer or bank, it's usually cheaper to buy software from a vendor who does that once for many clients.

**NORA:** So the vendor is really selling a promise to keep up.

**JAMES:** That's it exactly. cleversoft calls it its compliance commitment. The company says it implements legal changes in its software and proactively informs customers. And the founder put it this way, in translation: "We don't simply sell software, but show our customers how they can fully implement new regulation."

**NORA:** That sounds more like a service than a product.

**JAMES:** It's a blend, and in my view that's the heart of the business. The software is the delivery vehicle. The real value is the regulatory expertise built into it, and the ongoing work of keeping it current. That's what customers pay for year after year.

### Inside the product

**NORA:** Let's go through the four domains. Start with supervisory reporting.

**JAMES:** Supervisory reporting is the regular, formal reporting that financial firms send to their regulators. For insurers under Solvency II, that's a set of detailed templates on capital, assets, liabilities and risks. cleversoft's software takes the firm's data, performs the calculations, produces the reports in the required format, runs the validation checks the regulator expects, and helps submit them. FS Assist, the August 2026 deal, adds a Solvency II product called SII Assist, which the deal announcement says is used in the United Kingdom and eighteen countries of the European Economic Area, by about three hundred and fifty European insurers.

**NORA:** Then financial crime.

**JAMES:** Financial crime prevention covers anti-money laundering, sanctions screening and fraud. The software monitors transactions for suspicious patterns, screens customers and payments against sanctions lists, and helps investigators manage cases. A product page says its financial crime model constantly improves by learning from suspicious behaviour, though there's no technical detail. And at Sibos 2025, the big annual banking conference, cleversoft and Fineksus presented a joint anti-money laundering and financial crime platform with what they called integrated AI for fraud prevention and real-time compliance.

**NORA:** Third, digital advisory.

**JAMES:** Digital advisory and compliance is software that helps banks and wealth managers give investment advice in a way that follows the rules. Checking that a product suits a customer, recording the advice, producing the required disclosures, handling sustainability preferences. That's where tetralog and FAIT fit, both of them advisory software companies.

**NORA:** And fourth, financial messaging.

**JAMES:** That's mainly Fineksus, the SWIFT messaging work. Creating, validating and routing payment messages, and linking them to financial crime screening.

**NORA:** Is there a common platform underneath?

**JAMES:** The company describes a proprietary platform called eFrame, with a cloud architecture, and the founder emphasises cloud delivery. How much of each acquired product actually runs on that common platform today, we couldn't tell from public sources. That's an important question for integration and for AI, and we'll come back to it.

**NORA:** How do customers find cleversoft?

**JAMES:** The company has said referrals are a major channel. In a market like regulatory software, compliance officers and reporting heads talk to their peers at other firms. A recommendation from someone who has survived a reporting deadline with a vendor carries a lot of weight.

### A new rule, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Take the insurer from the cold open. Walk me through what happens when a regulation changes. An invented example.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's call the insurer Rheinland Mutual, a mid-sized German insurer, and the head of reporting Katrin. The European insurance regulator publishes an update to the Solvency II reporting templates. Some data points are added, some definitions change, and a few validation rules are tightened. It takes effect for reports in about a year.

**NORA:** What does Katrin do if she has no vendor?

**JAMES:** She assembles a small project. Someone reads the update in full and writes a summary. Someone compares old and new templates line by line. The actuaries work out which calculations change. IT works out which source systems hold the new data, and whether that data is even captured today. Then they build, test, and hope it works on the first live submission. It takes months, and it pulls her best people away from their day jobs.

**NORA:** And with cleversoft?

**JAMES:** With a vendor like cleversoft, a lot of that happens centrally, once, for all customers. cleversoft's regulatory team reads the update, works out what changes, updates the templates, calculations and validation rules in the software, tests them, and ships a new release. It also sends customers a note explaining what's changed and what they need to do on their side, for example supply a new data field.

**NORA:** So Katrin's job becomes much smaller.

**JAMES:** Much smaller. She checks the note, makes sure her data team can provide the new fields, runs a test submission on the new release, and reviews the results. Instead of a six-month project, it's a few weeks of focused work. That's the value of the compliance commitment.

**NORA:** And where's the time going at cleversoft?

**JAMES:** In that central regulatory and content team. Reading the regulation, interpreting it, mapping it to the software, building and testing the changes, writing the customer guidance. That's skilled, specialist labour, and it's repeated for every regulation, every update, every jurisdiction. It's exactly the work the founder says AI is now helping with.

**NORA:** Which brings us to AI. But first, the market.

**NORA:** And the reverse? Fineksus products coming into Europe?

**JAMES:** That's the other half of the logic, and the joint platform at Sibos 2025 suggests the two teams were working on exactly that. Whether European banks buy it at scale is one of the things we'd want to see evidence of. That's our view of the strategy, rather than anything the company has spelled out.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** How big is the RegTech market?

**JAMES:** We didn't find a reliable figure, so I won't quote one. What we can say is that the demand drivers are strong: a steady stream of new European regulation, tough enforcement on anti-money laundering, and hard reporting deadlines that firms can't miss.

**NORA:** Who does cleversoft compete with?

**JAMES:** A data provider, CB Insights, lists several alternatives. We didn't verify their ownership, so I'll describe them only briefly. Regnology does regulatory reporting and data solutions for financial services, and is probably the closest head-to-head competitor in supervisory reporting. LPA, based in Frankfurt and founded in 1999, is described as a RegTech company and is probably the closest German peer. Corlytics, from Dublin, does regulatory intelligence and automated policy compliance, which is closest to cleversoft's regulatory change pitch.

**NORA:** Others?

**JAMES:** FundApps, which offers compliance monitoring and reporting software as a service. Kaizen Reporting, based in London, founded in 2013, in compliance and regulatory reporting. Continuity Control, a compliance platform. And H3M Analytics, which describes itself as an AI-driven financial compliance vendor, and which could be an example of an AI-native challenger, though we didn't look into it further.

**NORA:** How does cleversoft differ?

**JAMES:** Mainly breadth. Most competitors focus on one or two domains. cleversoft's pitch is coverage across reporting, financial crime, advisory and messaging, from one vendor, with the regulatory expertise built in. The risk with breadth is that each individual product has to be as good as a specialist's, or customers will pick the specialist.

**NORA:** How sticky is this kind of software? Once a bank picks a vendor, does it stay?

**JAMES:** Generally, quite sticky, in our understanding of the category. Regulatory reporting software is wired into the firm's data, its calendar of deadlines and its audit trail. Switching vendors means re-mapping data, re-testing every report and taking the risk of getting a submission wrong during the change. Few heads of reporting want that risk unless they're unhappy. That's why renewals and cross-selling tend to matter more than winning brand-new logos.

**NORA:** Which makes the integration even more important.

**JAMES:** It does. A customer who is happy with one product is the easiest person to sell a second one to, but only if the second product works smoothly with the first. If buying another cleversoft product feels like buying from a different vendor, the cross-sell advantage shrinks.

**NORA:** And the big software suites?

**JAMES:** In my view, larger vendors with deep pockets can out-invest a private equity-backed roll-up on AI. That's an inference, but it's a real consideration. Whoever builds the best AI for reading and implementing regulation could gain a big advantage.

**NORA:** You mentioned regulators themselves are starting to ask about AI.

**JAMES:** Yes. A 2026 blog from fina2, which is another vendor in supervisory reporting, so treat it as a vendor view, argues that supervisors now expect AI governance, model lineage and audit trails from firms using AI in reporting. Model lineage means being able to show which model, which version and which data produced a given number. If that's right, it's a demand driver for vendors who can build those controls in.

### Where cleversoft is with AI

**NORA:** Let's take stock. Where is cleversoft with AI?

**JAMES:** There are three pieces of public evidence. First, the joint anti-money laundering platform with Fineksus at Sibos 2025, presented with integrated AI for fraud prevention and real-time compliance. Second, the product page saying its financial crime model constantly learns from suspicious behaviour. Third, the founder's comments in October 2026.

**NORA:** What did he say?

**JAMES:** In the Wirtschaftsforum profile, he said AI speeds up product development and helps analyse regulatory texts, and that it's also used for technical translation. And he was clear that humans remain decisive. AI supports the work, but people make the calls.

**NORA:** That's a sensible position for a compliance company.

**JAMES:** Very sensible. If you get a regulatory report wrong, the consequences land on your customer, and then on you. So "AI assists, humans decide" is the right stance, especially at this stage.

**NORA:** What's missing?

**JAMES:** We didn't find AI partnerships, AI hires or an AI budget. We didn't find technical detail behind the AI features. And the Sibos platform is a launch claim. We couldn't confirm it's being sold and used at scale. We also didn't find AI-specific governance published, or security certifications, although, as always, that doesn't mean they don't exist.

**NORA:** What could go wrong with AI reading regulation?

**JAMES:** The obvious risk is that the model misreads or invents something. Large language models can produce confident text that is subtly wrong. In regulatory work, a missed definition change or a misread threshold could lead to a wrong report. That's why the human review, the tests, and the traceability back to the exact paragraph of the regulation all matter. An AI suggestion that can't be traced to its source shouldn't make it into a release.

**NORA:** And there's the European AI Act.

**JAMES:** As general background, yes. The EU AI Act sets obligations depending on how risky an AI use is, and some uses in financial services, such as credit scoring, are treated as high risk. Using AI internally to help experts read regulation is likely to be a lower-risk use, but cleversoft's customers will still want to know how AI features in products they buy are governed. Publishing a clear AI policy would help, in our view.

**NORA:** So how would you characterise it?

**JAMES:** In my view, cleversoft is somewhere in the early middle. It has a named AI feature in a core product, in financial crime, and it says it uses AI internally on its most important process, regulatory analysis. But the public evidence is thin, and there's no visible AI leadership. It's further along than some companies we've covered, and less far than its opportunity suggests it should be.

**NORA:** Why does the internal use matter so much?

**JAMES:** Because of where cleversoft's costs and value sit. Reading regulation and turning it into software is the company's core work. If AI can make that work significantly faster and more reliable, cleversoft can ship updates sooner, support more regulations with the same team, and improve margins. That's not a side project. It's the engine of the business.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's list the challenges. What keeps leadership up at night, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, integration load. Four acquisitions in about twenty-six months, each with its own technology stack and customers, two of them still closing as of our last information. Combining products, data models and teams across Germany, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Austria, Turkey and Britain is a lot.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, the AI threat to its own revenue model. Customers pay partly for cleversoft's expertise in reading and implementing regulation. If AI makes that much cheaper for everyone, including competitors and even general-purpose AI vendors, the value of that expertise could be squeezed. That's our inference.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, data discipline. The public numbers on staff and customers vary a lot from source to source. That may be just a communications issue, but in our view it can also suggest that the company's internal metrics need tightening, which matters a lot for integration and for a future sale.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, leadership depth. The founder is chief executive and the main public voice. There's a new chief financial officer from late 2025, a new chief revenue officer from 2024, and no visible technology or AI leader. For a company with an AI-heavy future, that's a gap worth filling.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, regulatory cycles. Demand is driven by deadlines. If regulators delay rules, or if the political mood shifts toward simplification, spending can slow. That's our inference, and it cuts both ways, because simplification itself creates change work.

**NORA:** Is there a cultural challenge in a roll-up like this?

**JAMES:** Definitely. Acquired companies often have proud, specialist teams. A Solvency II expert in Britain, an anti-money laundering engineer in Istanbul and an advisory software developer in Vienna all see the world differently. Getting them to share a platform, a roadmap and an AI approach requires leadership that respects their expertise while insisting on common standards.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's flip to opportunities. If you were on the board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** First, cross-selling into the acquired bases. Reporting and compliance products into FS Assist's three hundred and fifty insurers. Anti-money laundering from Fineksus into the bank base. That's the company's own stated plan for FS Assist.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, productise AI-assisted regulatory change. Today, the compliance commitment is part of what customers get. cleversoft could also sell a rules-to-controls capability as a priced module. A tool that reads a new regulation, identifies what changes for that specific customer, maps it to their data and processes, and produces a draft implementation plan.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, geographic expansion. The founder has said the United States is expected to receive more focus. The Middle East and Turkey are already reachable through Fineksus and the Dubai and Kuwait offices.

**NORA:** The United States is a very different regulatory world.

**JAMES:** Very different, and I'd be cautious. American rules for insurers and banks differ a lot from European ones, and the market has strong incumbents. The most transferable piece is probably financial crime, where the problems are similar worldwide. That's our view.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, delivery margin. The Sofia office is a substantial site. Moving more build, testing and regulatory content work there, supported by AI, could save real money. Fifth, the new regulation pipeline. The founder sees opportunities in new rules on private retirement provision and in digital, compliant advice. And sixth, continued bolt-on acquisitions.

**NORA:** And AI in financial crime?

**JAMES:** That's a big one too. As general background, anti-money laundering systems are notorious for false positives. Investigators spend most of their time clearing alerts that turn out to be nothing. AI that reduces false alarms and helps investigators triage faster is now a standard pitch across the industry. cleversoft has a foothold there with the Sibos platform, and it should push to show measurable results.

**NORA:** Is there a data opportunity across all those institutions?

**JAMES:** Possibly, with great care. A vendor that processes regulatory reports for many insurers sees patterns: which validation rules most often fail, which templates cause the most trouble, how long firms take to adopt new requirements. Used in aggregated, anonymised form, and only where contracts and law allow, that could help cleversoft warn customers about common errors before they submit. I'd be very cautious about anything beyond that, because this is confidential supervisory data, and customers would rightly object to it being used loosely. That's our suggestion, not anything the company has proposed.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** One AI bet for the next eighteen months. What is it?

**JAMES:** AI-assisted regulatory change. Turning new rules into tested calculations, reporting templates and control steps, with humans signing off. It's the company's own core process, the founder already says AI is helping with it, and it has a clear labour cost and a clear speed benefit. If it works internally, it can also become a product.

**NORA:** Let's replay the Rheinland Mutual story with that in place.

**JAMES:** Sure. The regulator publishes its three-hundred-page update. Within hours, the AI system at cleversoft has read it, compared it to the previous version, and produced a structured list of changes: new data points, changed definitions, new validation rules, effective dates. For each change, it suggests which templates, calculations and code components are affected, and drafts the changes.

**NORA:** And then the humans step in.

**JAMES:** Right. cleversoft's regulatory experts review the list. They correct the system where it misread something, and they make the interpretation calls, which are often genuinely ambiguous. Engineers review the drafted code changes and the tests. And the customer note is drafted automatically and checked by an expert. What used to take weeks of reading and mapping takes days.

**NORA:** And for Katrin?

**JAMES:** She gets the release sooner, with a clearer explanation, and, if cleversoft productises it, a tailored view: here's exactly what changes for Rheinland Mutual, given your products and your data. She has more time to prepare and fewer surprises.

**NORA:** How would you run it? Give me a hundred days.

**JAMES:** First thirty days: pick the domain and set the rules. Choose one regulatory area with frequent updates and a strong internal team, Solvency II reporting is a good candidate, especially with FS Assist joining. Map how the team handles an update today, step by step, and how long each step takes. And set governance from day one: which models are used, how outputs are checked, how every AI suggestion is logged and traceable. If regulators expect model lineage from customers, cleversoft should model it itself.

**NORA:** Then?

**JAMES:** Days thirty to sixty: test on history. Take several past regulatory updates where the final implementation is known. Run the AI on them. How many changes did it find? How many did it miss? How good were the drafted mappings? This builds the evidence and shows the experts where to trust it and where not to.

**NORA:** And the last stretch?

**JAMES:** Days sixty to a hundred: run it live, in parallel. On the next real update, run the AI-assisted process alongside the normal one, with the experts comparing. Measure time saved, errors caught and missed. At day one hundred, decide whether to make it the standard internal process for that domain, and which domain comes next. And start shaping the customer-facing version.

**NORA:** And who owns it?

**JAMES:** This is where the leadership gap matters. Ideally a senior product or technology leader with authority across the regulatory content team and engineering. If there isn't one, hiring one would be my first move. And a respected regulatory expert as the champion, because the experts have to trust the tool.

**NORA:** How would the regulatory experts feel about this? Isn't it their job being automated?

**JAMES:** It's a fair worry, and leadership has to address it directly. My view is that in a company growing by around twenty percent a year by its own account and buying more businesses, the realistic outcome isn't fewer experts. It's each expert covering more regulations, faster, with less time spent on reading and copying and more on interpretation and customer guidance. That's a better job. But people need to hear that clearly, and see it in how the programme is run.

**NORA:** And how would you price the customer version?

**JAMES:** I'd start by including faster, clearer updates in the existing subscription, to protect renewals and show value. Then add a premium module for the tailored analysis, the "here's exactly what changes for you" view, priced per regulatory domain. That turns an internal efficiency into new revenue without making customers pay more for what they already get.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** AI triage in the anti-money laundering platform. Let me give a quick example. An invented bank in Luxembourg has investigators facing a queue of alerts every morning. Today they open each one, look up the customer, review the transactions and write a note. With AI triage, each alert arrives with a short summary, the relevant history and a suggested risk level, with the reasons. The investigators work the high-risk ones first and clear obvious false positives much faster. The bank's measures are time per alert and the share of real cases caught. If cleversoft can show those numbers improving at real customers, that's a powerful sales story.

**NORA:** Before we move on, let's do a second walk-through, on the advisory side, since that's where tetralog and FAIT fit. Another invented example.

**JAMES:** Sure. Picture a private bank in Vienna. An adviser, call him Lukas, is meeting a client who wants to invest an inheritance. Under European investment rules, Lukas has to assess whether the products he recommends suit the client: her knowledge and experience, her financial situation, her goals, her tolerance for risk. He also has to ask about her sustainability preferences. And he has to document all of it and give her the required disclosures.

**NORA:** That sounds like a lot of paperwork for one conversation.

**JAMES:** It is, and in the past a lot of it was literally paperwork. Advisory compliance software turns it into a guided digital process. Lukas works through the questions with the client on a tablet, the system checks which products are suitable, flags any that aren't, records the reasoning, and produces the disclosure documents and the suitability report automatically.

**NORA:** And where could AI fit in?

**JAMES:** In a few places, in our view. Summarising the conversation into the required record, which Lukas then checks. Explaining in plain language why a product does or doesn't fit the client's profile. Checking that product documents and the advice record are consistent. And, on the vendor side, updating the rules engine quickly when the advice regulations change. In every case, the adviser remains responsible for the advice, and the regulator will expect that.

**NORA:** So the same theme. AI does the drafting and the checking, the human does the judgment.

**JAMES:** Exactly. And the more regulatory domains cleversoft covers, the more places that pattern applies, which is an argument for building the AI capability once, as a shared platform, rather than separately in each product.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from cleversoft?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, if your product is expertise wrapped in software, AI hits both. It can make your expertise cheaper to deliver, and it can make it cheaper for competitors to copy. The answer is to use it on your own core process first, before someone else does.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, a buy-and-build is only as good as its integration. Buying four companies is the easy part. Making them feel like one company to customers, and run on shared data, is where the value is created. And AI makes this even more important, because AI works best on clean, connected data.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, get your numbers straight. Inconsistent public figures on staff and customers may be harmless, but buyers, investors and customers notice. Clear, consistent metrics are a sign of an organisation in control, and they're the baseline for proving any AI benefit.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, "humans decide" is a strategy, not just a safety line. In high-stakes domains, customers want AI that makes experts faster and better, not AI that replaces them. Building products that make the human checkpoint easy, fast and well documented is a competitive advantage.

**NORA:** I'll add one from the outside. In compliance, trust compounds.

**JAMES:** That's well said. Every deadline you help a customer hit builds trust, and that trust drives referrals, which the company says is a major channel. AI that helps hit deadlines more reliably builds on that.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI disrupting the revenue model by cutting the cost of reading and implementing rules, weakening the pricing power of the compliance commitment. Two, larger vendors such as Regnology out-investing it on AI. Three, deal execution, with FAIT and FS Assist not confirmed closed and several integrations running in parallel. Four, demand cyclicality, if regulation slows. And five, founder dependence.

**NORA:** Anything else on the list?

**JAMES:** One more, in my view. Security. A vendor handling supervisory reports, transaction data and customer screening for financial institutions is an attractive target for attackers. Under DORA, a serious incident at a vendor becomes a serious incident for its customers too. We didn't find published certifications, which doesn't mean they're absent, but strong, visible security is part of what customers will increasingly demand.

**NORA:** And for the owner?

**JAMES:** At about three and a half years in, the owner will want to see the acquisitions integrated, cross-selling working and a clear AI story, in time for a future sale. The next eighteen months should show measurable progress on all three.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch for?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, confirmation that FAIT and FS Assist have closed, and early signs of cross-selling into FS Assist's insurers. Second, any named AI product or module, especially for regulatory change. Third, a technology or AI leadership hire. And fourth, any evidence from customers that the AI in the financial crime platform reduces false positives.

**NORA:** And a bonus?

**JAMES:** Any move into the United States. That would be a big strategic step, and it would tell you a lot about the company's ambition.
