### The Award That Looked Too Good

**MORGAN:** Six weeks after Calder created one governed supplier identity, a sourcing analyst put a recommendation on Kevin's desk. The proposed supplier was eleven percent cheaper than the incumbent. On the face of it, the award was worth nearly two million dollars a year. The capacity file told a different story. The bidder could support only two of Calder's four regions at launch, had one qualified distribution center, and wanted Calder to fund conversion stock. The proposal was not false. The savings number was incomplete.

**KEVIN:** Which is exactly the trap. A bid can be valid and the conclusion can still be wrong. Season One taught me to understand the buying process. Here I have to decide whether a lower price is value after switching cost, service scope, demand, resilience, and implementation are priced.

**MORGAN:** And the incumbent was not simply expensive. Its unit price included emergency delivery, vending-machine replenishment at twelve plants, and engineering support that Calder had never represented as separate services. Procurement compared a stripped-down challenger price with a bundled incumbent price. The spreadsheet called that competition. Finance called it an uncontrolled scope change.

**KEVIN:** So the first consequence from the supplier episode is uncomfortable. Better identity did not tell us whom to award. It made the missing evidence visible. We can now prove that the bidder exists, owns the stated bank account, holds required certificates, and passed onboarding. None of that proves it can deliver the business outcome.

**MORGAN:** Correct. Supplier truth is an eligibility condition, not an award decision. At Month Four, Calder had spent a cumulative one point three million dollars. Fully validated active suppliers had risen from sixty-one to seventy-eight percent. Trusted spend classification, established in the prior data work, stood at eighty-eight percent. The opportunity backlog contained eleven point six million dollars, but that was still a pipeline, not booked savings. Only one hundred thousand dollars of recurring benefit had survived finance validation, largely from supplier-service capacity that had actually moved.

**KEVIN:** That is our six-metric CFO pack: one point three million spent, seventy-eight percent validated suppliers, eighty-eight percent trusted classification, eleven point six million of identified opportunity, one hundred thousand of validated recurring benefit, and zero permission to call the rest value.

**MORGAN:** Good. Now source something that can survive reality.

### Price Is Only One Value Mechanism

**KEVIN:** The board question sounds simple: is value created by negotiating price, redesigning demand, changing the supply base, or doing nothing?

**MORGAN:** The answer can be any of those. Price is the easiest mechanism to display and often the least durable one to isolate. If Calder buys an unnecessary specification at a lower unit rate, procurement has negotiated waste. If it consolidates demand but creates a single point of failure, it has bought fragility. If it changes a supplier and spends the savings on qualification, inventory, downtime, and retraining, it has moved cost rather than removed it.

**KEVIN:** Then demand comes first. What are we buying, why, at what service level, in what quantity, and with what consequence if supply fails?

**MORGAN:** Yes. Start with four value mechanisms. First, eliminate demand that does not need to exist. Second, standardize or substitute demand so more suppliers can compete. Third, change commercial terms or price. Fourth, change the supply model: the number of suppliers, geographic allocation, inventory ownership, service boundary, or route to market. A fifth choice is deliberately to do nothing because switching cost or resilience risk overwhelms the available benefit.

**KEVIN:** Doing nothing has to be a real option, not a negotiating pose.

**MORGAN:** Exactly. A disciplined no-change decision can be more valuable than a bad event. Calder's evidence showed hundreds of categories, but only two had the combination we needed: trusted demand data, meaningful variance, credible competition, manageable implementation, and an owner willing to change behavior. Those were maintenance, repair, and operating consumables, which we will call MRO, and calibration plus field-maintenance services.

**KEVIN:** That is not quite the Month Two shortlist. We funded industrial consumables and tail-software renewals for evidence, while contingent maintenance remained in discovery. Why did the second wave change?

**MORGAN:** Because the stage gate was allowed to work. Industrial consumables matured into the MRO wave. Tail software could not yet prove usage ownership, cancellation rights, and accountable adoption across entities, so it stayed in discovery without a sourcing event or savings claim. Calibration and field-maintenance data improved: Calder normalized service units, found named site owners, and proved credible competition. It crossed the gate. A backlog that never changes after evidence is a target list, not a decision system.

**KEVIN:** MRO carried thirty-eight million dollars of annual addressable demand across plants. Calibration and field maintenance carried twenty-six million. Sixty-four million in the two waves. Why not start with a larger category?

**MORGAN:** Because spend size is not value readiness. A larger metals category had indexed contracts, customer-linked specifications, and capacity commitments that could not be changed within the program window. A large logistics category had poor lane data. The opportunity might be real, but Calder could not prove or implement it fast enough. We are optimizing confidence-adjusted, time-bound value, not hunting the largest number.

### The Three Credible Paths

**MORGAN:** Kevin had three choices. Option one was a broad sourcing event across the top twenty categories. It promised visible momentum, wide supplier tension, and a large negotiated number. It also exceeded category-owner capacity, relied on immature specifications, and would generate awards the business could not implement.

**KEVIN:** Option two was incumbent renegotiation. Faster, lower switching risk, and easier on operations. But it would preserve fragmented demand and let the current suppliers define the scope. We might get a concession while leaving the structural cost untouched.

**MORGAN:** Option three was two evidenced category waves. Narrower headline, stronger data, explicit total-cost and resilience tests, and enough implementation capacity to convert awards into buying channels. It would disappoint anyone measuring procurement by event count or negotiated savings.

**KEVIN:** All three are defensible. Broad events can work when specifications and implementation capacity are ready. Incumbent renegotiation can be rational when continuity matters. Two waves sacrifice apparent scale for proof.

**MORGAN:** Before you hear Calder's call, pause. Choose one. Broad event, incumbent renegotiation, or two evidenced waves. State the value mechanism you expect, the evidence that could falsify it, and the person who must change behavior after award.

[PAUSE]

**KEVIN:** I choose two evidenced waves. The board did not ask for the largest sourcing pipeline. It asked for finance-validated recurring benefit. I want categories where demand, competition, switching cost, supplier resilience, and implementation capacity can all be measured.

**MORGAN:** That became the canonical call. Two categories, each with a category strategy before an event, and no savings claim without an implementation route.

### Build the Category Strategy Before the Event

**KEVIN:** Let's construct the first artifact. A category strategy is not a market summary. It is an explicit theory of how Calder will obtain value.

**MORGAN:** For MRO, the evidence showed twenty-seven thousand active item descriptions, duplicated stock-keeping units, inconsistent brands, emergency orders, and local suppliers serving only one plant. Thirty-eight million dollars was addressable, but not every dollar was equally contestable. Safety-critical items, original-equipment warranties, and remote-plant emergency stock needed separate treatment.

**KEVIN:** We segmented the demand into four pools. Standard consumables suitable for a national catalog. Technical equivalents requiring engineering validation. Emergency stock requiring local service. And protected items that stayed with approved original-equipment suppliers. That removed the fantasy of one universal price event.

**MORGAN:** It also exposed demand reduction. Calder was buying twelve glove specifications where five would meet the approved safety requirements. It ordered small quantities because plants planned independently. It paid expedited freight on predictable replenishment. Those were not supplier price problems. They were specification, planning, and channel problems.

**KEVIN:** The MRO strategy therefore combined specification rationalization, aggregated volume, a primary and secondary supply allocation, defined emergency coverage, and a catalog implementation. Price competition mattered, but only inside that design.

**MORGAN:** Calibration and field maintenance required a different theory. Twenty-six million dollars covered scheduled calibration, break-fix visits, compliance documentation, travel, parts, and response commitments. The incumbent's price looked high because service components were bundled. Challenger bids looked low because travel, night work, recertification, and transition support were exclusions.

**KEVIN:** So we normalized the service unit. Not just hourly rate. We priced planned visit, emergency callout, travel zone, required certificate, first-time fix, response-time tier, parts markup, and the cost of equipment downtime. We also separated sites where a supplier needed regulatory authorization from sites with open competition.

**MORGAN:** That is the category-strategy discipline: define the demand, segment it by risk and contestability, identify the value mechanisms, state the implementation route, and write the reasons not to source. Only then decide what event, if any, deserves to run.

### Total Cost or Spreadsheet Fiction

**KEVIN:** The second artifact was the total-cost model. How did we stop it becoming a convenient collection of assumptions designed to favor the incumbent?

**MORGAN:** We assigned every material cost to an evidence owner and a confidence level. Purchase price came from bids. Consumption came from twelve months of trusted invoice and PO lines. Freight came from paid charges, not contract promises. Transition labor came from named site tasks. Qualification cost came from engineering estimates with approved hours. Inventory impact came from agreed stocking policies. Downtime exposure used operating ranges, not a single heroic number.

**KEVIN:** And we did not monetize every risk. Some risks became award constraints. A supplier without qualified capacity for a critical region could not buy its way through the model with a lower price.

**MORGAN:** Precisely. Expected cost is useful when probability and impact can be defended. A hard control is better when failure is intolerable or evidence is weak. The low MRO bidder offered an eleven-percent unit-price reduction. Once conversion stock, duplicated inventory during transition, lost local replenishment, expedited freight, and Calder's implementation labor were added, the first-year advantage fell below four percent.

**KEVIN:** Then the capacity constraint mattered. It could not serve two regions at launch. A full award would create dependence before the supplier had proven delivery.

**MORGAN:** The incumbent's apparent premium also narrowed when vending replenishment and emergency delivery were priced separately. Calder discovered it was paying for some service it valued and some service it did not use. The answer was neither accept the incumbent bundle nor select the cheapest stripped bid. The answer was to design the service explicitly.

**KEVIN:** For calibration, the lowest labor rate produced the highest modeled cost at several sites because travel and repeat visits were excluded and first-time-fix performance was weak. The incumbent remained more expensive at low-risk sites but competitive at regulated sites where transition and downtime were material.

**MORGAN:** A total-cost model should change the award, not merely decorate it. If the winner is identical with or without the model, challenge whether the model contains decision-useful factors or just procurement ceremony.

**KEVIN:** There is another test: distinguish a transfer from enterprise value. If Calder negotiates a lower supplier price by accepting longer lead times, the price variance may look favorable while safety stock and downtime exposure rise elsewhere. If Calder demands one hundred and twenty day terms from a small critical supplier, reported working capital may improve while resilience deteriorates. The category model has to show whose economics changed and where the consequence landed.

**MORGAN:** Right. We labeled every lever as demand removed, unit price changed, specification changed, service unbundled, payment timing changed, process cost changed, or risk changed. That stopped one mechanism being counted twice. A reduced order frequency might lower freight and internal handling, but it could raise inventory. A consolidated supplier base might create volume leverage, but concentration risk needed an explicit response. A rebate was not recognized as benefit until the contractual threshold was probable and the accounting treatment was agreed.

**KEVIN:** We also separated recurring from temporary effects. Selling obsolete stock, recovering a historical overcharge, and avoiding one transition fee can create cash, but they are not the annual run rate the board asked us to defend.

**MORGAN:** This classification made negotiation better. The team could trade across variables instead of demanding a theatrical price cut. Calder could offer a more reliable forecast or consolidated volume in exchange for price, preserve faster payment for a vulnerable supplier, or pay separately for emergency coverage rather than hide it in every unit. Total cost became a design tool, not an after-the-fact justification.

### Design the Event to Answer a Question

**KEVIN:** The event brief became artifact three. What had to be in it?

**MORGAN:** One page before attachments. The decision to be made. In-scope demand and exclusions. Baseline and confidence. Required service and resilience. Commercial structure. Supplier instructions. Evaluation method. Approval authority. Implementation owner. Benefit definitions. And the conditions under which Calder would make no award.

**KEVIN:** The MRO event used multiple lots rather than a winner-take-all design. National catalog supply, regional emergency coverage, and selected technical-item groups. Suppliers could bid one or several lots, but they had to disclose capacity, subcontracting, implementation lead time, and service assumptions.

**MORGAN:** The service event used scenario pricing. A normal planned visit, an emergency response, a remote-site call, a failed first visit, and a transition month. That made exclusions visible. It also asked suppliers to demonstrate how completion evidence would flow into Calder's service-entry and invoice-matching process.

**KEVIN:** That last part connects sourcing to the operating system. An award is not ready if the supplier cannot support the evidence needed downstream.

**MORGAN:** Exactly. Medius's current Sourcing materials describe demand capture, RFX events, auctions, quick quotes, supplier questionnaires, automatic scoring and ranking, and an audit trail behind award decisions. Those are useful mechanics. The operator lesson is that the software can structure and preserve the decision, but it cannot decide Calder's risk appetite or invent a credible category strategy.

**KEVIN:** Nor does automatic scoring make the source evidence comparable. If one supplier includes emergency service and another excludes it, ranking their entered prices faster gives us a faster wrong answer.

**MORGAN:** That is why the event brief owns the semantics and the platform owns the controlled execution. Technology should reduce coordination cost and preserve evidence. Accountability for the commercial question remains human.

### Weight the Bid Without Hiding Judgment

**MORGAN:** Calder's fourth artifact was a weighted bid model. Price carried thirty points. Modeled total cost carried twenty. Service performance carried fifteen. Proven capacity carried fifteen. Resilience carried ten. Implementation readiness carried ten. One hundred points in total.

**KEVIN:** Why separate price from total cost? Isn't price already inside total cost?

**MORGAN:** Because the committee wanted price transparency without letting it dominate. The raw price score showed the commercial offer. The total-cost score showed Calder's consequence. We tested for double counting and documented it. If freight was in total cost, it was not also rewarded under service. If capacity constrained eligibility, it did not receive a second hidden penalty in price.

**KEVIN:** We also set thresholds. A bidder needed a minimum capacity and compliance score before its commercial score could win. That prevented a low price from averaging away a control failure.

**MORGAN:** And we ran sensitivity. If changing one reasonable weight moved the award, the decision was fragile and needed executive judgment. The MRO challenger won standard consumables in two regions but did not receive the national award. The incumbent retained protected items and one critical region under an unbundled service schedule. A second supplier received a smaller resilience lot contingent on successful qualification.

**KEVIN:** For calibration, the incumbent retained regulated and high-downtime sites. A challenger won standard sites after a staged pilot. A separate specialist won a narrow technical lot. No one supplier received all twenty-six million dollars.

**MORGAN:** The sourcing team initially disliked the split because the headline discount was lower than a full transfer. Operations disliked it because multiple awards required governance. Finance supported it because implementation cost, concentration, and service value were explicit.

**KEVIN:** Weighted models do not remove judgment. They force judgment into inspectable assumptions. The award memo must still say why the decision is right, what could make it wrong, and who owns the next action.

### The Award Memo That Finance Can Read

**KEVIN:** Artifact five was the award memo. Let me try the structure. Decision, scope, supplier allocation, commercial value, total-cost result, risks, mitigations, implementation plan, benefit treatment, approval, and stop conditions.

**MORGAN:** Add rejected alternatives. A future reviewer should be able to see why the lowest bidder did not receive the whole award and why Calder did not simply renew the incumbent. That protects the organization from both hindsight and supplier pressure.

**KEVIN:** The two waves produced three point four million dollars of negotiated annualized value. That number combined price changes, demand reduction, avoided services, and revised commercial terms relative to the governed baseline.

**MORGAN:** Stop there. Negotiated value means the signed or awarded commercial position compared with an approved baseline. It is not yet recurring benefit. Calder approved two point five million dollars for implementation because some lots required qualification, catalog configuration, service-entry design, site transition, or later start dates. By Month Six, only three hundred thousand dollars of new recurring benefit from the waves had passed finance review.

**KEVIN:** Added to the prior one hundred thousand, cumulative finance-validated recurring benefit became four hundred thousand dollars. We did not book the remaining three million negotiated. We did not book the full two point five million approved for implementation. We booked what had reached compliant transactions and could be evidenced against baseline.

**MORGAN:** Exactly. This is where transformation claims usually inflate. Negotiated value is a commercial event. Implemented value means the new price, demand rule, supplier, or service model is available in operations. Realized value means actual transactions changed. Finance-validated recurring benefit means the change is normalized, attributable, repeatable, and accepted under the benefit policy.

**KEVIN:** So the award memo contains a benefits schedule, not a victory number. Each lever has a baseline, formula, transaction source, owner, start date, lag, leakage test, and finance reviewer.

**MORGAN:** And a stop condition. For the MRO challenger, Calder would pause regional expansion if fill rate fell below the agreed floor, emergency orders exceeded tolerance, qualification failed, or first-year total cost crossed the approved incumbent comparison. For calibration, Calder would stop migration if certificate quality, response time, or first-time fix breached the pilot threshold.

### The Decision Memo

**MORGAN:** Here is Calder's Month Six decision memo. Choice: run two evidenced category waves with segmented awards, rather than a broad event or an incumbent-only negotiation. Owner: the chief procurement officer, with named MRO and field-service category owners and finance as benefit validator.

**KEVIN:** Spend: one point one million dollars of additional program investment since Month Four, taking cumulative program spend to two point four million. That funds category data preparation, supplier events, technical evaluation, negotiation, implementation design, and the first buying-channel work. It does not change the seven point five million program cap.

**MORGAN:** Target: three point four million of negotiated annualized value from the two waves, two point five million approved for implementation subject to gates, and only evidenced recurring benefit entering finance validation. Guardrail: no award may trade away critical capacity, required certification, service continuity, or auditable transaction evidence for price.

**KEVIN:** Stop condition: pause a supplier allocation if qualification, capacity, service, total-cost, or implementation evidence crosses its threshold. Reopen the award rather than defend a bad decision because procurement announced savings.

**MORGAN:** That is a decision memo. Choice, owner, spend, target, guardrail, stop condition. Short enough to operate. Specific enough to audit.

### Update the Two Ledgers

**KEVIN:** Program ledger first. Month Six. Cumulative spend: two point four million dollars. We remain inside the eighteen-month cap. The work now spans data and definitions, supplier identity, and the first two sourcing waves.

**MORGAN:** Benefits ledger second. Cumulative finance-validated recurring benefit: four hundred thousand dollars. One hundred thousand carried from supplier-service capacity and three hundred thousand from early realized sourcing and demand changes. Three point four million was negotiated in the two waves, but the unimplemented or unvalidated balance remains outside recurring benefit.

**KEVIN:** Operating movement is deliberately modest. We have not changed the final sourceable-spend-under-contract metric yet in the progression ledger. We have created awards and early implementation, not proof that employees will buy through them.

**MORGAN:** The opportunity backlog also remains separate. The original eleven point six million pipeline is being tested and consumed, not converted wholesale into a savings ledger. Some opportunities will die. A dead opportunity is a learning outcome, not a hidden miss.

**KEVIN:** And the board can now see three values without confusion: the opportunity being investigated, the value negotiated, and the recurring benefit validated. Next episode adds implemented and realized as separate states in the waterfall.

**MORGAN:** Good. A CFO pack becomes trustworthy when it makes premature claims difficult.

### Operator Drill: Build an Award That Can Fail Safely

**MORGAN:** Your operator drill produces five durable artifacts for one real category. Do not choose the category with the cleanest presentation. Choose one where price, demand, service, and switching risk genuinely conflict.

**KEVIN:** First, write the category strategy on one page. State addressable demand, exclusions, risk segments, value mechanisms, market structure, implementation capacity, and the explicit no-action case.

**MORGAN:** Second, build a total-cost model. For every material line, record the value, source, owner, confidence range, and whether it is a cost, a constraint, or a risk. Include transition, inventory, service, operational disruption, internal labor, and exit cost where material. Run at least one downside scenario.

**KEVIN:** Third, write the event brief. Define the decision, scope, normalized bid structure, required evidence, evaluation model, timetable, approvers, implementation owner, benefit policy, and no-award conditions.

**MORGAN:** Fourth, create the weighted bid model. Limit it to decision-useful criteria. Test double counting. Set minimum thresholds where a weighted average must not excuse failure. Then change the weights within reasonable ranges and identify whether the proposed award is robust or fragile.

**KEVIN:** Fifth, write the award memo before announcing a winner. Include alternatives rejected, allocation, total-cost conclusion, key assumptions, implementation sequence, benefit states, guardrails, and stop conditions. Ask finance and operations to sign their part of the decision, not merely acknowledge procurement's recommendation.

**MORGAN:** Then perform the uncomfortable test. Remove the supplier names. Can another executive reproduce the award logic from the evidence? If not, you have preference dressed as analysis. Now remove the negotiated savings number. Does the decision still make operational and financial sense? If not, you may be buying a headline.

**KEVIN:** The output is not a slide deck. It is a category strategy, total-cost model, event brief, weighted bid model, and award memo that remain useful when implementation becomes difficult.

### The Value Has Nowhere to Go

**KEVIN:** Calder made defensible awards. It did not yet make them easy to buy. At one plant, the first MRO requisition after award went to a familiar local supplier because the new catalog was not loaded. At another, a technician selected the incumbent's old item code because the approved equivalent was invisible. In field services, a site manager called the incumbent directly because the new service scope had no usable request path.

**MORGAN:** The contracts existed. The transaction behavior did not. Procurement could point to signed terms and call the work implemented. AP would still receive invoices at old prices, without a usable PO or service entry, and route them through exception approval.

**KEVIN:** Which means the next question is not whether Calder negotiated value. It is whether an employee can make the compliant purchase more easily than the bypass.

**MORGAN:** Exactly. Episode Fifteen asks how an awarded term survives into an actual purchase. Calder will try the obvious answer—a mandatory PO policy—and discover that a control can improve the appearance of compliance while leaving price and behavior untouched.
