# DEADWATER
## Episode 7: "Double Life"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] On the morning of Roland Voss's arrest, a team of twelve investigators arrives at his home in Massapequa Park with a search warrant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They will be there for twelve days.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What they find inside that modest red house, on a quiet residential street where the neighbors have known Roland Voss for twenty-five years, will take months to fully catalog. Terabytes of digital data. Physical documents. Items recovered from the walls and floors and concealed spaces that suggest a level of intentional architecture in the house's construction - hidden access points, modified storage - that speaks to preparation. To anticipation. To someone who has thought very carefully, for a very long time, about what he needed to hide and where to hide it.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The public picture of Roland Voss that emerges in the weeks after his arrest is built from three sources: what investigators find in the search, what his colleagues and neighbors say when asked, and what the court documents reveal about the evidence against him. Together, these sources produce a portrait of a person so comprehensively split between his visible and hidden lives that several forensic psychologists I spoke to struggled to find an adequate clinical framework for it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His colleagues describe a meticulous professional. Someone who never missed a deadline, handled difficult clients with what one described as "almost inhuman patience," and took genuine pride in technical precision. He mentored younger architects. He brought in business. He had worked at the firm for over two decades and was, by every professional measure, an asset.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His neighbors describe a man who kept his yard immaculate and helped the woman across the street carry her groceries. Who shoveled the sidewalk in front of his elderly neighbor's house without being asked. Who was quiet but not unfriendly. Who kept to himself but in a way that felt private rather than suspicious.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His wife, speaking through an attorney in a statement released two weeks after the arrest, says she is devastated and had no knowledge of any aspect of the case against her husband.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The investigators believe her. The evidence supports her account. Roland Voss built his hidden life in spaces and times that his wife was not present to observe.

[MARSH] Every serious killing he committed, according to the timeline we've reconstructed, occurred when his wife was out of town. Visiting family. Work trips. Every single one. He was meticulous about that, among everything else. He maintained the illusion of a normal marriage while systematically ensuring that the person closest to him was never in a position to observe any inconsistency.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The daughter is in her twenties. She no longer lives at home. She says, in a single brief statement, that she does not recognize the person described in the charges.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Nobody who knew Roland Voss does.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The digital evidence recovered from the search is, according to the prosecution, extensive and damning. Among the materials recovered from his devices are communications with victims that investigators trace through encrypted and disposable accounts. Search histories that map, in painstaking detail, both the planning of the crimes and the years of monitoring the investigation that followed.

[REESE] He followed the investigation. Obsessively and in detail. He tracked media coverage. He tracked what we were saying publicly. He knew the anniversary pieces, the updates, every press briefing that made the news. There is something deeply unsettling about that - the idea that he was watching us not knowing, for twelve years, just as we were watching for him.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] And there is something else in those searches that the prosecution will address in court. In the years after the initial discovery of the bodies, his search activity shows a period of what I can only describe as behavioral adjustment. He went quiet. He changed patterns. And then, gradually, over years, he relaxed again. The evidence of that relaxation in his digital record is one of the most chilling things I have encountered in this case.

[DR. PARK] The digital trail is, in some ways, a map of his psychology. You can see the fear response in the early years - the behavioral tightening, the cessation of certain activities. And then you can see the confidence returning. The certainty that he was safe. Which tells you something about how he experienced this period. He was not haunted by guilt in any operationally meaningful sense. He was managing risk. And when the perceived risk declined, he resumed.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey drives to Long Island the day after the arrest. She doesn't have a plan. She just drives.

[DONNA] I ended up parking near the barrier island access road. Not at the site itself - I've never been able to go to the actual site. But nearby. And I sat in the car and I looked at the road and I thought about Renee making that same drive. Making it in a car with someone she didn't know she should be afraid of. Making it thinking she was going to do a job and come home.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I don't think I have words for what those twelve days were like, from the arrest to the arraignment. It was not relief, exactly. Relief implies the thing is over. This wasn't over. It was more like... permission. Permission to believe it was going to be resolved. I hadn't let myself believe that in twelve years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Mitchell Graves, the defense attorney, files a motion within three weeks of the arraignment challenging the genealogical DNA evidence. The motion argues that forensic genealogy as applied in this case constitutes an unconstitutional search, because it reaches into the private genetic information of individuals - Voss's relatives - who consented only to commercial ancestry analysis, not to law enforcement use.

[GRAVES] My client is entitled to the strongest defense the law permits. That includes challenging the admissibility of every piece of evidence against him. Forensic genealogy is genuinely new legal territory. The constitutional questions it raises about genetic privacy and the limits of consent are real and substantial. A court should resolve them carefully.

[TULLY] We knew this challenge was coming. We had prepared for it extensively before the arrest. The warrant applications for the genealogical analysis were written specifically to address the constitutional questions, and they had been reviewed and approved by a federal judge. We were confident they would withstand challenge.

[NARRATOR] The genealogical evidence challenge becomes a focal point of the legal proceedings over the following two years. The court ultimately rules the evidence admissible. It is a significant ruling with implications well beyond this case.

[REESE] Whatever you think of the policy questions around forensic genealogy - and there are real policy questions worth discussing - in this case it identified a man who murdered eight women over eighteen years and who would otherwise have continued to evade justice. That is the reality we were working with.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Eight months after the arrest, new charges are filed.

[NARRATOR] The investigation has been ongoing since Voss was taken into custody. Investigators working backward through the timeline, using the DNA evidence and the behavioral data recovered from his devices, build the case for additional victims. The partial remains found in a neighboring county in 1993 - Sandra, thirty-one years old, two daughters. A victim identified only by her DNA profile until forensic genealogy resolves her name in the spring after Voss's arrest. A third victim whose remains were found elsewhere on the island years after the initial discovery and whose case was always treated as potentially connected but never confirmed.

[NARRATOR] The new charges bring the confirmed victim count to eight.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Sandra's daughters are now in their thirties. The older one issues a statement through Donna Kelsey's advocacy organization.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It says: Our mother has been gone for thirty years. We grew up without her and without knowing what happened to her or why. Now we know. Knowing is not what I thought it would be. It does not fill the place she left. But it is something. It is the truth. And the truth matters even when it is hard. Especially when it is hard.

[PAUSE]
