# DEADWATER
## Episode 6: "Pizza Night"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] On a Tuesday evening in January 2023, Roland Voss orders a large pizza from a place near his Manhattan office. He pays cash. He eats most of it at his desk. He puts the box, with two crusts and a napkin inside it, into the office trash.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The trash is collected that night. It goes into a black bag. The bag goes into the dumpster in the alley behind the building.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] At two-seventeen in the morning, a surveillance operative in plain clothes pulls the bag from the dumpster and replaces it with an identical one. The original bag goes into a sealed evidence container in the back of an unmarked van parked on the cross street.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] At seven the following morning, the pizza crusts are in Dr. Nina Park's lab.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The DNA extraction from the pizza crust takes eleven days. Eleven days during which Carol Marsh, by her own account, is almost unbearable to be around.

[MARSH] I am not a patient person by nature. I have developed the appearance of patience over twenty-five years in law enforcement because it is professionally necessary. But on the inside I was vibrating. I knew what that result was going to say. I had known for months. But knowing and proving are the same thing in this work, and until the lab result came back I didn't have the second one.

[REESE] I checked in with Carol twice a day during those eleven days. Not because there was anything new to report. Just to make sure she hadn't done anything impulsive. She hadn't. She was at her desk, working every other angle we had, making sure the case we were building around the DNA was airtight from every other direction. That's who she is. She doesn't stop.

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese is using the eleven days to build the arrest case. If the DNA matches, they will move quickly - Voss has no idea he is under surveillance, but that can change. A wrong tip, a chance encounter, something that spooks him. They cannot afford to wait.

[REESE] An arrest case is not just the evidence. It's the narrative. It's the sequence in which you present the evidence so that the totality of it is undeniable. You have the geographical connection. The vehicle. The cell data and the E-ZPass overlap. The genealogical analysis pointing to him. The parking citation from years earlier. The letter to the editor about the lights. You put all of that together in the right order and you have a story that has only one possible conclusion. Then you add the direct DNA match and it's over.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I had the arrest case written in three days. I spent the other eight days finding every hole in it that a defense attorney might find. Because I knew the defense attorney was going to be very good, and I was not going to hand him anything.

[NARRATOR] DA Frank Tully reviews the arrest case on the afternoon of day eight.

[TULLY] I read it twice. There were two places where I thought the chain of custody documentation needed strengthening and I sent it back with notes. Danny and the team fixed both of them inside twenty-four hours. The case they built was meticulous. It was exactly what you want in an investigation like this. Everything documented. Everything sourced. Every piece of evidence traceable back to a specific decision made by a specific person for a specific reason.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] That's Carol Marsh's fingerprint on it. That methodical, leave-nothing-to-chance approach. She built that culture into the task force from day one.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] On day eleven, Dr. Park calls Carol Marsh at six-forty-three in the morning.

[DR. PARK] I called her the moment I had a conclusive result. I did not wait for business hours. I had been in the lab since four in the morning, running the comparison, and when the result resolved I looked at it for about ten seconds to make sure I was reading it correctly. And then I called her.

[MARSH] My phone rang and I saw it was Nina and I sat up in bed and she said, Carol, it's a match. And I said, how strong? And she said, as strong as I've ever seen. The DNA from the pizza crust matches the DNA from the hair found on the victim. It is the same contributor.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I said thank you and I hung up and I sat on the edge of my bed in the dark for about three minutes. Just sat there. Twenty-two years on the job. Twelve years on this case. Three minutes just sitting with it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Then I called Danny.

[REESE] She called me at six forty-nine and said four words: we have a match. I said: how long do we need? She said: forty-eight hours.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The forty-eight hours before the arrest are the most operationally complex of the entire investigation. The task force needs to ensure that Voss's surveillance coverage is complete and unbroken. They need to position arrest teams without creating any visible change in the normal patterns around his home and office. They need to coordinate with the FBI, the state police, and the county sheriff.

[NARRATOR] And they need to prepare the families.

[MARSH] This is the part that most people don't think about. Before we arrest, we tell the families. Not the details of the evidence - we can't do that before trial. But we tell them: we have someone. We want you to know before it's on the news.

[DONNA] Carol called me on a Thursday morning. She said: Donna, I need you to sit down. I said I was already sitting. She said: we have made a significant development in your sister's case. We will be making an arrest within forty-eight hours. I want you to know before anyone else outside this investigation does.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I just started crying. I couldn't speak. She waited. She didn't rush me. She just waited on the phone until I could say something. And when I could say something, all I said was: who is it? And she said: I can't tell you that yet. But I want you to know that the evidence is strong. This is real. And when it is publicly announced, I want you to know you helped get us here.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I called my mother next. My mother is seventy-eight years old and she has been waiting twelve years for that call. I don't know how to describe what she sounded like. I have never heard a sound like that before. It was like something that had been held too tight for too long finally being allowed to let go.

[NARRATOR] Nine families receive similar calls on that Thursday morning. Each call is made personally by Carol Marsh, working through a list she has kept for twelve years.

[MARSH] I had written every family's name in my notebook on the first day I was assigned this case. Not as an evidence log. Just as a reminder. These are the people I am working for. These are the people who deserve an answer. I kept that notebook current for twelve years. Added names as we identified victims. Never lost it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Making those calls was the hardest and best thing I have ever done professionally. I cried three times. I do not usually cry at work. I was not embarrassed about it.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Friday morning. Five-forty-seven AM.

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss leaves his home in Massapequa Park, as he does every weekday, to walk to the train station. He is carrying a briefcase. He is wearing a gray jacket. He is six foot four and two hundred and forty pounds and he has not had a reason to hurry in twelve years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He gets halfway to the station.

[REESE] We had two plainclothes teams on either side of his route and a marked unit one block east. The arrest team commander made the call at the midpoint - far enough from the house that his family didn't have to watch it from a window, far enough from the train that there were no civilian complications.

[MARSH] I wasn't on the street for the arrest. I was in the command post, two blocks away, listening on radio. I had been asked if I wanted to be there and I said no. The team that did the surveillance and built the case deserved to make that arrest. I didn't need to be the one holding the cuffs. I just needed to hear the word.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] At five-fifty-one in the morning, the arrest commander comes over the radio.

[NARRATOR] "Voss is in custody."

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I exhaled. Twelve years of held breath.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I heard the call from the command post. I was standing next to Carol. I looked at her and she was completely still. And then she said, quietly, like she was saying it to herself: okay. Okay.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That was it. No celebration. No big moment. Just: okay.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss is transported to the seventh precinct. He is processed. He is placed in an interview room.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He asks for his attorney.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His attorney, Mitchell Graves, arrives within the hour. He has already been briefed by Voss via a phone call from the precinct. He walks into the DA's office and asks to see the charges. He reads them. He asks about the evidence. He is given a summary.

[NARRATOR] He says nothing for a long moment.

[GRAVES] What I can tell you - and I'm speaking to you now two years after the arrest, after the legal proceedings have run their course - is that the evidence presented at that summary stage was significant. I have handled serious criminal cases for thirty years. I understood immediately what I was looking at.

[PAUSE]

[GRAVES] My obligation was to my client. My obligation was to ensure that every piece of evidence was properly obtained, properly handled, and properly presented. That's what the adversarial system requires and I believe in the adversarial system. But I will not pretend, sitting here now, that this was a case where the evidence was thin. It was not thin.

[NARRATOR] At his arraignment, Roland Voss enters a plea of not guilty. He is remanded without bail.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is six foot four and two hundred and forty pounds. He walks into that courtroom and he sits down and he is very still.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He has been still like that his whole life. Still and careful and patient. It looked, for a long time, like safety. It turned out to be something else.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: Double Life. As the search of Voss's home reveals the depth of what he built and concealed, the families of his victims begin to find out who he really was - and what they discover is stranger and darker than almost anyone expected.
