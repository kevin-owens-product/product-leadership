### SEGMENT 1: THE FOUR LEVERS OF PE VALUE CREATION (7 minutes)

**KEVIN:** So the starting point is understanding that PE firms are essentially financial engineers. They buy a company, they hold it for typically three to seven years, and then they sell it at a higher price. The entire game is about maximizing the delta between the purchase price and the exit price. And there are really only four levers they can pull to do that. Revenue growth, margin expansion, multiple expansion, and financial engineering. Every single decision a PE firm makes maps back to one of these four levers, and once you understand that framework, a lot of behaviors that seem irrational from a product perspective suddenly make perfect sense.

**ALEX:** Let's take those one at a time. Revenue growth seems like the most obvious one, but I'd imagine it's complicated in a PE context because you're not dealing with a startup that can grow at 200 percent a year just by finding product-market fit.

**KEVIN:** Exactly right. PE firms are typically buying companies that are already doing somewhere between 50 million and a billion dollars in annual recurring revenue. These are mature businesses. They've already found product-market fit. They've already captured a significant portion of their initial addressable market. So growing revenue means you have to be much more deliberate and strategic about it. There are a few standard plays. The first is pricing optimization, which is often the fastest win. A PE firm will come in and discover that the company hasn't raised prices in four years, or that they're giving away premium features for free, or that their pricing scales linearly when it should scale with the value the customer derives. I worked with a company -- let's call them a mid-market ERP vendor -- where the PE firm brought in a pricing consultant during the first 90 days. They found that the company was charging the same per-user fee to a 50-person company as they were to a 5,000-person company. The larger customers were extracting massively more value from the platform but paying essentially the same rate. By restructuring to a tiered, value-based pricing model, they increased ARR by 18 percent within a year without adding a single new customer.

**ALEX:** That's a significant number. And I'd imagine it flows almost entirely to the bottom line since there's no incremental cost to serve.

**KEVIN:** Almost entirely, yes. The only cost is some churn risk, which you have to manage carefully. But pricing optimization is high-leverage because it improves revenue and margin simultaneously. The second major revenue play is what the industry calls "buy and build" or the roll-up strategy. You acquire a platform company, then you bolt on smaller acquisitions that expand your product footprint, give you access to adjacent markets, or bring you into new geographies. I've seen this play out in spend management software, where a firm will acquire an accounts payable automation company, then bolt on a procurement tool, then add expense management, then add a payment network. Each acquisition expands the total addressable market and increases the cross-sell opportunity within the existing customer base. The compounding effect is powerful because you're not just adding revenue arithmetically -- you're creating a platform that's stickier and more valuable to each customer than any individual point solution.

**ALEX:** And what about organic growth? Is that something PE firms invest in, or is it mostly growth through acquisition?

**KEVIN:** This is where the narrative has really shifted over the last five to eight years. The older PE playbook was heavily weighted toward acquisition-driven growth and cost reduction. You'd buy a company, cut costs, bolt on a few acquisitions, and sell it for more. But the best firms today are investing heavily in organic growth because they've learned that acquirers at exit pay a premium for organic revenue growth. If you can show that your platform is growing organically at 15 to 20 percent on top of the inorganic growth from acquisitions, your exit multiple is dramatically higher. So you see PE firms actually increasing R&D spend, hiring product leaders, investing in go-to-market -- things that would have been heretical in the PE world 15 years ago. The third revenue lever is geographic expansion. Many PE-acquired software companies are dominant in one region but have minimal international presence. Expanding from North America into Europe, or from Europe into North America, is a massive growth opportunity. But it requires real product investment. You need localization, you need compliance with local regulatory frameworks, you need market-specific features. In financial software, for example, every country has different invoicing requirements, different tax structures, different data residency laws. A company that nails multi-country compliance can turn those regulatory barriers into a competitive moat.

**ALEX:** So revenue growth is lever number one. What about margin expansion? That feels like where PE gets its reputation.

---

### SEGMENT 2: MARGIN EXPANSION -- BEYOND THE CHAINSAW (8 minutes)

**KEVIN:** You're right that margin expansion is the lever most associated with PE, and it's also the source of most of the horror stories. The crude version is straightforward: you buy a company, you fire 20 percent of the workforce, you offshore the engineering team, you cancel discretionary spending, and EBITDA goes up mechanically. That playbook exists, it still gets deployed, and it's what gives PE a bad name in the tech community. But I want to make a distinction between that approach and what sophisticated operators actually do, because the difference is night and day.

**ALEX:** Walk me through the sophisticated version. What does margin expansion look like when it's done well?

**KEVIN:** The sophisticated version starts with understanding where the company's engineering capacity is actually going. And this is almost always a revelation. When a PE firm acquires a software company that has grown through multiple acquisitions, which is very common, what they typically find is a nightmare of technical complexity. You've got three or four separate codebases, each on a different tech stack. Maybe one product was built in Java, another in .NET, a third is a legacy PHP application. They're running on different cloud providers, or worse, some are still on-premise. You've got separate CI/CD pipelines, separate QA processes, separate on-call rotations. The maintenance burden of all this redundancy is enormous. I've seen companies where 60 to 70 percent of total engineering effort was going to keeping the lights on -- infrastructure maintenance, security patches, compatibility updates, bug fixes in legacy code -- and only 30 percent was going to building anything new.

**ALEX:** So the opportunity isn't about cutting engineers. It's about redirecting what they work on.

**KEVIN:** That's the key insight. The smart margin expansion play is platform consolidation. You invest in migrating to a unified architecture. You move from multiple repositories to a monorepo or at least a well-structured set of services with shared infrastructure. You standardize on a single deployment pipeline. You build shared services for authentication, billing, notifications, analytics -- all the horizontal capabilities that were being duplicated across product lines. The upfront cost is significant. You might spend 12 to 18 months on this consolidation effort. But the payoff is enormous. Once you've reduced the maintenance surface area, you can either do the same work with fewer people -- which improves margins -- or, and this is what the best firms do, you can redirect that freed-up capacity toward building new features and new products that drive revenue growth. So you get margin expansion and revenue growth from the same investment. It's a double win.

**ALEX:** Can you give me a concrete example of what this looks like in practice? Like, what does a technology operating partner actually walk into on day one?

**KEVIN:** Sure. I'll anonymize the details, but this is a real scenario. A PE firm acquires a vertical SaaS company that serves the logistics industry. The company has been around for 15 years, grew mostly through acquiring smaller competitors, and is now doing about 120 million in ARR. On the surface, it looks like a solid business. Strong retention, dominant in its niche, decent growth. But under the hood, it's a mess. There are four separate products, each acquired at different times, each with its own engineering team, its own database schema, its own deployment process. The main product is a monolithic Rails application that was architected in 2011. It works, but making changes to it is painfully slow because the codebase is enormous and poorly tested. There's a second product that was built on a Node.js stack, a third one that's a legacy desktop application still running on customer premises, and a fourth that was a mobile-first product built by a startup they acquired two years ago.

The technology operating partner comes in, does a 60-day assessment, and produces what I'd call a "state of the union" for the technology organization. The findings are always some version of the same story. Engineering velocity is low because of technical debt and architectural fragmentation. The teams can't share code or capabilities across products. There's no unified data layer, so customers who use multiple products can't get a coherent view of their own data. And the cost structure is bloated because you're maintaining four of everything -- four sets of infrastructure, four on-call rotations, four release processes.

**ALEX:** So what's the prescription? What does the operating partner actually recommend?

**KEVIN:** The typical recommendation is a phased consolidation plan. Phase one is infrastructure convergence -- get everything onto the same cloud provider, standardize the CI/CD pipeline, establish shared observability. This is the lowest-risk, highest-immediate-return work because you can do it without touching the application code. Phase two is data layer unification -- build a common data platform that all products feed into and read from. This unlocks cross-product analytics and reporting, which customers love and which supports pricing power. Phase three is application modernization -- start extracting shared services from the monoliths, build a common UI component library, establish a design system. And phase four, which sometimes happens and sometimes doesn't depending on the timeline, is full platform convergence -- actually rebuilding the products on a shared platform.

The critical thing is sequencing this against the hold period. If the PE firm is planning a five-year hold, you have time to do all four phases. If they're targeting a three-year exit, you probably focus on phases one and two, plus selective work in phase three. The operating partner has to be pragmatic about what's achievable and what drives the most value within the timeline. This isn't an academic architecture exercise. Every technical decision maps back to a financial outcome.

**ALEX:** And what kind of margin improvement are we talking about when this is executed well?

**KEVIN:** I've seen companies go from 55 percent gross margins to 75 percent gross margins over a three-year period through a combination of platform consolidation, automation, and cloud migration. On the EBITDA line, moving from 15 percent EBITDA margins to 30 or even 35 percent is a common target. And remember, in PE math, every dollar of EBITDA improvement gets multiplied by the exit multiple. So if you improve EBITDA by 10 million and you're selling at a 15x multiple, you've created 150 million dollars in enterprise value from that margin improvement alone.

### SEGMENT 3: MULTIPLE EXPANSION AND NARRATIVE ENGINEERING (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about the third lever, multiple expansion. This one feels the most abstract to me. How does a PE firm actually control what multiple they sell at?

**KEVIN:** Multiple expansion is fascinating because it's where finance and storytelling intersect. The valuation multiple -- whether it's expressed as a multiple of revenue or EBITDA -- is essentially the market's judgment of the quality and durability of your business. And "quality" is subjective, which means it can be influenced. Let me break down what drives multiples in software. The biggest factor is growth rate. A company growing at 25 percent per year will trade at a significantly higher multiple than one growing at 8 percent. The second factor is retention. Net revenue retention above 110 percent tells acquirers that your existing customers are spending more over time, which means your revenue base is inherently expanding. Third is margin profile -- higher margins signal a more efficient, more scalable business. Fourth is market positioning -- are you in a category that buyers consider attractive? And fifth is TAM, total addressable market. A company with a credible path to a multi-billion-dollar market commands a higher multiple than one that's maxed out its niche.

**ALEX:** So PE firms are essentially trying to improve as many of those factors as possible during the hold period?

**KEVIN:** Yes, but the really clever ones also think about narrative engineering, which is about how you frame the business for potential acquirers. Here's a concrete example. Let's say you buy an invoicing software company. At the time of acquisition, it's positioned as an "AP automation tool." It does one thing well: it takes paper invoices, digitizes them, matches them to purchase orders, and routes them for approval. That's a useful product, but it's a narrow category. The market for AP automation might be two or three billion dollars. During the hold period, through a combination of product development and acquisitions, you expand the product to include procurement, expense management, payment processing, and supplier management. Now you're no longer an "AP automation tool." You're a "spend management platform." The TAM for spend management is 15 to 20 billion dollars. You haven't changed the underlying business that dramatically, but you've repositioned it into a much larger market with a much more compelling growth narrative. That repositioning alone can move your revenue multiple from 6x to 10x or even higher.

**ALEX:** That's a really interesting point because it highlights something that I think product people don't always appreciate -- the market category you choose to compete in has enormous financial implications, independent of the actual product functionality.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And this is where the AI narrative is playing an outsized role right now. If you can credibly position your software company as "AI-native" or "AI-powered," you get a significant multiple premium. I've seen PE firms invest specifically in AI capabilities -- even if those capabilities are incremental improvements to existing functionality -- partly because of the genuine efficiency gains, but partly because it supports a valuation narrative. "We're an AI-powered spend management platform" trades at a different multiple than "We're an AP automation tool with some machine learning." The underlying technology might be similar, but the story is worth billions in enterprise value.

**ALEX:** Is there a risk of that becoming cynical? Like, slapping "AI" on everything just for the multiple premium?

**KEVIN:** Absolutely, and sophisticated buyers see through it. The acquirers doing due diligence on a PE exit are not naive. They're going to bring in technical advisors who will dig into your actual AI capabilities. Do you have proprietary training data? Are you using foundation models with custom fine-tuning, or just calling an API with a generic prompt? Is AI actually improving your unit economics, or is it a cost center disguised as a feature? The companies that benefit most from the AI narrative are the ones where AI genuinely improves the product economics. If your AI-powered invoice processing reduces the need for human reviewers from 30 percent of invoices down to 2 percent, that's a real margin improvement that supports a higher multiple. If you just added a chatbot to your help center and called yourself "AI-native," buyers will see through that in diligence. So yes, narrative matters, but it has to be grounded in substance.

**ALEX:** What about the fourth lever, financial engineering? I feel like that's the one that's most opaque to people outside of finance.

**KEVIN:** Financial engineering is the lever that's most native to PE and least visible to the technology teams. The basic idea is straightforward: PE firms use debt to fund acquisitions. If you buy a company for a billion dollars and you put up 400 million of your own capital and borrow 600 million, you've leveraged your investment. If the company's value increases to 1.5 billion, you've made 500 million on a 400 million investment, which is a 125 percent return. If you'd used all equity, that same increase would only be a 50 percent return. The leverage amplifies your gains. Of course, it also amplifies your losses if things go wrong, which is why over-leveraged PE deals sometimes blow up spectacularly. The other dimension of financial engineering is capital structure optimization -- dividend recapitalizations, tax-efficient structures, working capital management. These are important, but they're almost entirely invisible to the product and technology teams. I mention it mostly for completeness. The first three levers -- revenue growth, margin expansion, and multiple expansion -- are where technology leaders can actually influence outcomes.

---

### SEGMENT 4: THE TECHNOLOGY OPERATING PARTNER (7 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's shift gears and talk about the people who actually execute this playbook. You've mentioned the technology operating partner a few times. For listeners who aren't familiar with the PE world, what is that role and how does it work?

**KEVIN:** The operating partner model is one of the most important innovations in PE over the last 15 years. Historically, PE firms were purely financial shops. You had deal teams -- the people who sourced, evaluated, and executed acquisitions -- and you had the portfolio company management teams who ran the businesses. There wasn't much in between. The deal team would buy the company, install a new CEO if needed, set some financial targets, and check in quarterly. The problem with that model is that deal professionals are great at evaluating businesses and structuring transactions, but they often lack the operational expertise to actually improve the businesses they buy. They know what good looks like from the outside -- strong revenue growth, expanding margins, efficient engineering organizations -- but they don't always know how to get there.

So firms started hiring operating partners, which are essentially seasoned executives who sit at the fund level and work across the portfolio. You'll have a technology operating partner, a go-to-market operating partner, maybe a finance and operations operating partner. These people aren't running any single portfolio company day to day. Instead, they're deployed as advisors and catalysts across the entire portfolio. The technology operating partner might be working with eight or ten portfolio companies simultaneously, spending a day or two a week with each one.

**ALEX:** What does a typical engagement look like? If a PE firm just acquired a software company, what does the technology operating partner actually do in the first 90 days?

**KEVIN:** The first 90 days are the most intensive. The operating partner typically starts with what I call the diagnostic phase. They're evaluating the technology organization across several dimensions. First, they're looking at the team -- who are the key technical leaders, what's the organizational structure, where are the talent gaps? Second, they're assessing the architecture -- what does the tech stack look like, how is the system deployed, what's the technical debt burden, where are the scalability constraints? Third, they're evaluating engineering effectiveness -- what's the deployment frequency, what's the incident rate, how long does it take to ship a feature from conception to production? Fourth, they're looking at the product portfolio -- which products are growing, which are declining, where are the cross-sell opportunities, what's the competitive landscape?

This diagnostic typically produces a document that I've heard called different things at different firms -- a "technology value creation plan," a "100-day plan," a "transformation roadmap." But the core output is the same: here's where we are, here's where we need to be, and here's the sequenced set of initiatives that will get us there, mapped against the financial model and the expected hold period.

**ALEX:** How much authority does the operating partner actually have? Because I can imagine a tension between the operating partner at the fund level and the CTO or VP of Engineering at the portfolio company. Who actually makes the decisions?

**KEVIN:** This is one of the trickiest dynamics in PE-backed companies, and it's handled differently at different firms. Formally, the operating partner is an advisor, not a line manager. They don't have direct authority over the portfolio company's technology team. The CTO or VP of Engineering reports to the CEO, who reports to the board, which the PE firm controls. But in practice, the operating partner has enormous informal authority because they represent the firm that owns the company. When the operating partner makes a recommendation, everyone in the room knows that recommendation carries the weight of the investor. Some firms handle this very well. They hire operating partners who are skilled at building trust, who lead with expertise rather than authority, and who genuinely partner with the portfolio company leadership team. The operating partner brings pattern recognition -- they've seen this situation play out at ten other companies -- and the portfolio company leadership brings context and institutional knowledge. When that partnership works, it's incredibly effective.

**ALEX:** And when it doesn't work?

**KEVIN:** When it doesn't work, it's usually because of one of two failure modes. The first is what I call "the consultant trap." The operating partner produces a beautiful PowerPoint deck with a transformation roadmap, hands it to the portfolio company, and then moves on to the next company. There's no follow-through, no accountability, no sustained engagement. The portfolio company leadership team nods politely, puts the deck in a drawer, and goes back to doing what they were doing before. I've seen this happen more times than I can count. The second failure mode is the opposite extreme -- the operating partner who micromanages. They insert themselves into every technical decision, overrule the CTO on architectural choices, demand specific technology selections that they're personally familiar with regardless of whether they're the right fit. This demoralizes the engineering team, undermines the local leadership, and often leads to bad technical decisions because the operating partner doesn't have enough context about the specific product and customer base to be making those calls.

**ALEX:** So what does the healthy middle ground look like?

**KEVIN:** The healthy version is what I'd describe as "guardrails and enablement." The operating partner sets the strategic direction and the success criteria -- we need to get gross margins from 65 to 75 percent within 24 months, we need to reduce the average time to ship a feature by 40 percent, we need to build a unified data platform that supports cross-product analytics. Those are the guardrails. Then they enable the local leadership team to figure out how to get there. They provide resources, they remove blockers, they connect the portfolio company with experts and vendors, they share playbooks and best practices from other portfolio companies. But they don't dictate the implementation. They hold the "what" and the "why," and they empower the local team to own the "how."

---

### SEGMENT 5: COMMON PE MISTAKES IN TECH COMPANIES (7 minutes)

**ALEX:** We've talked about how PE creates value. Let's flip it and talk about how PE destroys value, because I think that's what a lot of our listeners are most worried about. What are the most common mistakes you see PE firms make when they acquire technology companies?

**KEVIN:** The list is long, but I'll highlight the ones I see most frequently. The first and most damaging mistake is treating engineering as a cost center rather than a value creation engine. This manifests in several ways. The most obvious is aggressive headcount reduction in engineering immediately after acquisition. You buy a company with 200 engineers, you decide that feels like too many, and you cut it to 150 within the first six months. The problem is that those cuts are rarely surgical. You lose institutional knowledge, you break teams that were high-functioning, and you demoralize the engineers who remain. The people who leave first are always the best engineers because they have the most options. So you end up with a smaller team that's also lower quality, and your product velocity collapses. I've seen companies where a 25 percent headcount reduction led to a 60 percent decrease in feature output because of the compounding effects of knowledge loss and morale damage.

**ALEX:** Why does this keep happening if it's so counterproductive? You'd think PE firms would have learned by now.

**KEVIN:** A few reasons. First, the financial models are seductive. If you cut 50 engineers at an average fully loaded cost of 250,000 each, that's 12.5 million in annualized savings that drops straight to EBITDA. At a 15x exit multiple, that looks like 187 million in enterprise value creation. On a spreadsheet, it's a no-brainer. The problem is that the spreadsheet doesn't capture the second and third-order effects -- the features that don't get built, the technical debt that accumulates faster, the competitive position that erodes, the customer satisfaction that declines because bugs aren't getting fixed. Those effects show up 12 to 18 months later, by which time the damage is done.

Second, many PE deal teams simply don't understand software engineering well enough to evaluate engineering productivity. They see 200 engineers and 50 million in revenue and compare that to another portfolio company that has 100 engineers and 50 million in revenue, and they conclude that the first company is overstaffed. But maybe the first company has three separate products on different tech stacks that require more engineers to maintain, or maybe they're investing in a platform migration that will dramatically improve efficiency in 18 months. Without deep technical context, headcount-based comparisons are misleading.

**ALEX:** What's the second big mistake?

**KEVIN:** The second mistake is what I call "the offshore stampede." A PE firm acquires a company with a domestic engineering team and immediately starts moving work to lower-cost geographies -- Eastern Europe, India, Southeast Asia -- with the goal of reducing labor costs. Now, I want to be clear: distributed and global engineering teams can work extremely well. Some of the best engineering organizations in the world are globally distributed. The problem isn't offshoring per se. The problem is doing it too quickly, without the right management structures, communication practices, and cultural integration. I've seen companies try to offshore 50 percent of their engineering in six months. The result is always chaos. The offshore team doesn't have enough context to be productive. The onshore team is spending all their time answering questions and reviewing code instead of building features. Quality drops. Velocity drops. And you've now got a distributed team that's more expensive to manage than the original team, even though the per-person cost is lower.

The right approach is to build distributed capability gradually. Start with a small, senior team in the new location. Invest heavily in onboarding and knowledge transfer. Establish clear ownership boundaries -- don't split features across locations, give each location end-to-end ownership of specific products or services. And give it 12 to 18 months before you evaluate whether it's working. The firms that do this well can genuinely achieve significant cost savings while maintaining or even improving engineering velocity. But it requires patience and investment, which are not always PE's strongest suits.

**ALEX:** What else goes wrong?

**KEVIN:** The third common mistake is underinvesting in product. PE firms sometimes view product management as overhead rather than a strategic function. They'll cut the product team, or they'll never hire senior product leaders, and instead let engineering and sales drive the roadmap. What happens is entirely predictable. Without strong product leadership, the roadmap becomes a collection of customer-specific feature requests driven by the sales team. You end up building bespoke functionality for individual large customers instead of building scalable platform capabilities. Your product becomes increasingly fragmented and difficult to maintain. And your competitive position erodes because you're not investing in the innovations that would differentiate you in the market.

I worked with a company where the PE firm had acquired a market-leading product in its category. Over the course of the hold period, they cut the product team from 12 to 3, shifted engineering to building whatever the top 10 customers asked for, and stopped investing in competitive differentiation. When they went to sell the company three years later, the due diligence revealed that two competitors had leapfrogged them on core functionality. The exit multiple was significantly lower than projected because the acquirers could see that the product had stagnated. The savings from cutting the product team were dwarfed by the multiple compression at exit.

**ALEX:** That's a painful but instructive story. Is there a pattern to when PE gets it right versus when it gets it wrong?

**KEVIN:** The single best predictor of success, in my experience, is whether the PE firm has genuine technology expertise at the partner level. Firms like Vista Equity Partners, which built their entire identity around software operations, tend to make fewer of these mistakes because they have deep institutional knowledge about how technology organizations work. They've made the mistakes before, learned from them, and encoded those lessons into their operating playbooks. The firms that struggle are the generalist PE firms that occasionally buy software companies but don't have dedicated technology operating expertise. They apply the same playbook they'd use for a manufacturing company or a retail chain -- cut costs, improve procurement, optimize the supply chain -- and that playbook simply doesn't translate to software. Software businesses are fundamentally different because the product is created by people writing code, and the quality and velocity of that code is directly tied to the talent, morale, and organizational health of the engineering team. You can't optimize a software company the way you'd optimize a factory.

### SEGMENT 6: THE SHIFT FROM COST-CUTTING TO GROWTH PE (6 minutes)

**ALEX:** You've hinted at a broader evolution in how PE approaches software companies. Can you articulate that shift more explicitly? It sounds like the industry has been on a journey from a cost-focused model to something more growth-oriented.

**KEVIN:** Yes, and it's one of the most significant shifts in PE over the last decade. I'd frame it as three eras. The first era, which dominated from roughly the 1990s through the mid-2000s, was pure financial engineering. PE firms made money primarily through leverage and multiple arbitrage. They'd buy a company with a lot of debt, the market would go up, multiples would expand, and they'd sell for more. The operational improvements were secondary. The second era, which ran from the mid-2000s through the mid-2010s, was the operational efficiency era. This is when the cost-cutting playbook became dominant. PE firms realized that they could create value by genuinely improving operations, but "improving operations" was almost entirely defined as reducing costs. Cut headcount, consolidate offices, renegotiate vendor contracts, offshore what you can. The returns were real but limited, and the approach earned PE its reputation as a destroyer of companies.

**ALEX:** And the third era?

**KEVIN:** The third era, which we're in now, is what I'd call the growth-through-technology era. The leading firms have realized that the biggest returns come not from cutting costs but from accelerating growth and expanding the addressable market. And technology is the primary lever for doing that. This shift was driven by a few factors. First, the sheer volume of PE capital chasing software deals has driven up acquisition multiples, which means you're buying companies at higher prices. When you buy at 12x revenue instead of 6x revenue, you can't math your way to a good return through cost-cutting alone. You need genuine growth. Second, the exit market has shifted. Strategic acquirers and public market investors are willing to pay enormous premiums for high-growth, high-retention software businesses. A company growing at 20 percent with 120 percent net revenue retention might sell for 15 to 20x revenue, while a company growing at 5 percent with 90 percent net retention might sell for 5 to 7x. The multiple premium for growth is so large that it dwarfs any value you could create through cost reduction.

**ALEX:** What does this mean practically for how PE firms operate their software companies?

**KEVIN:** It means that the best firms are now making investment decisions that would have been unthinkable in the previous era. They're increasing R&D spend. They're hiring world-class product leaders and paying them competitively. They're investing in platform modernization not just for cost savings but to enable faster feature delivery and better customer experiences. They're building dedicated data science and AI teams. They're even accepting lower short-term margins in exchange for higher growth rates, because they know that growth drives multiple expansion, which is ultimately more valuable than margin expansion.

I know one firm that acquired a company doing 80 million in ARR with 35 percent EBITDA margins. A traditional PE approach would have been to push margins to 45 percent and sell on the higher EBITDA. Instead, they invested heavily in product and go-to-market, let margins dip to 25 percent for two years, and grew the company to 160 million in ARR. They sold it at a significantly higher revenue multiple than they bought it at because the growth profile was so attractive. The total return was dramatically higher than the margin-optimization path would have produced.

**ALEX:** That's a fundamental reorientation. It sounds like PE firms are almost starting to behave more like growth equity or even late-stage VC in some cases.

**KEVIN:** The lines are blurring, for sure. You see PE firms leading growth rounds, you see growth equity firms doing buyouts, you see late-stage VCs competing for the same deals. The taxonomy matters less than the underlying approach. The firms that are winning are the ones that understand software economics deeply and invest accordingly. And the firms that are struggling are the ones still running the old playbook -- buying mature software companies, cutting costs, and trying to sell them for more. That playbook still works sometimes, but the returns are compressing because the market has gotten more efficient and acquisition prices have gone up.

### SEGMENT 7: REAL-WORLD SCENARIOS AND PATTERNS (7 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's make this even more concrete. Can you walk us through a couple of anonymized scenarios that illustrate these dynamics? I think stories are where this really comes alive for people.

**KEVIN:** Happy to. Let me give you two scenarios that represent opposite ends of the spectrum -- one where the PE approach worked beautifully, and one where it went badly wrong.

The success story is a company I'll call "DataBridge." DataBridge was a data integration platform, doing about 70 million in ARR, growing at 10 percent, with mediocre margins. The company had been around for 12 years and was a solid business but not an exciting one. The PE firm that acquired it had a clear thesis: this product sits in a market that's about to explode because of the proliferation of SaaS applications and the growing need for data connectivity. The underlying demand is there, but the company isn't capturing it because of product limitations and a weak go-to-market motion.

The first thing they did was bring in a new CTO and a new Chief Product Officer. Not from outside the industry -- they hired people who had deep experience in the data infrastructure space and understood the competitive landscape. The second thing they did was increase engineering headcount by 40 percent over 18 months, focused specifically on building a cloud-native version of the platform that could compete with newer entrants. The third thing was a pricing restructure -- moving from a legacy perpetual licensing model to a consumption-based model that aligned pricing with customer value. And the fourth was a series of small acquisitions that added capabilities in data quality and data governance, which expanded the platform story.

**ALEX:** And the outcome?

**KEVIN:** Over a four-year hold period, DataBridge went from 70 million to 180 million in ARR. Organic growth accelerated from 10 percent to over 25 percent. The consumption-based pricing model improved net revenue retention to over 130 percent. Margins expanded modestly -- from about 20 percent EBITDA to about 28 percent -- because they were investing heavily in growth. But the multiple expansion was dramatic. They bought the company at roughly 5x revenue and sold it at 12x revenue. The total return to the fund was over 4x invested capital in four years, which is exceptional. And the key point is that the majority of the value creation came from revenue growth and multiple expansion, not from cost-cutting.

**ALEX:** Now give me the cautionary tale.

**KEVIN:** The cautionary tale is a company I'll call "FormStack" -- a workflow automation platform doing about 90 million in ARR, growing at 12 percent, with a loyal customer base in healthcare and financial services. The PE firm that acquired FormStack had a traditional operating playbook. Within the first six months, they cut engineering headcount by 30 percent, moved a significant portion of the remaining engineering to an offshore development center, and replaced the CTO with a cost-focused VP of Engineering whose primary mandate was to reduce the technology cost structure.

They also deprioritized the product roadmap. Features that had been promised to customers were delayed or cancelled. The product management team was cut from eight people to two. The remaining product managers were told to focus exclusively on the features that the top 20 customers were requesting, which meant that the platform capabilities that would have benefited the broader customer base -- things like improved APIs, better reporting, a modern UI -- were shelved indefinitely.

**ALEX:** I can see where this is going, but walk me through the consequences.

**KEVIN:** The consequences took about 18 months to materialize, which is the insidious thing about underinvestment in product and technology. In the short term, margins improved dramatically. They went from 22 percent EBITDA to 38 percent within a year. On paper, the value creation plan was working beautifully. But underneath those improving margins, the business was rotting. Engineering velocity had collapsed. The offshore transition had been botched -- key context was lost, code quality declined, deployment failures increased. Customer satisfaction scores started dropping because bugs weren't being fixed and promised features weren't being delivered. And most critically, churn started creeping up.

In healthcare IT and financial services, switching costs are high, so customers don't leave quickly. But they started making plans to leave. Two of FormStack's top 10 customers launched RFPs for replacement solutions. Mid-market customers started declining to renew multi-year contracts and instead went month-to-month, which is a leading indicator of future churn. Net revenue retention, which had been 108 percent when the company was acquired, dropped to 94 percent. That might not sound dramatic, but in SaaS, the difference between 108 percent and 94 percent net retention is the difference between a revenue base that's compounding upward and one that's shrinking.

**ALEX:** So what happened at exit?

**KEVIN:** The exit was painful. The PE firm had modeled selling the company at 8 to 10x revenue after growing it to 120 million in ARR. Instead, after three years, the company was doing 85 million in ARR -- it had actually shrunk -- and the acquirers were offering 4 to 5x revenue because of the declining growth rate, the weakened product, and the elevated churn. The total return to the fund was barely 1x -- they essentially got their money back after three years, which in PE terms is a failure because of the opportunity cost of the capital. And the employees and customers bore the worst of it. Engineers who stayed through the transition were burned out and demoralized. Customers who depended on the product were stuck with a deteriorating platform. It was a value-destruction cycle that was entirely self-inflicted.

**ALEX:** What's the lesson you take from comparing those two scenarios?

**KEVIN:** The lesson is that software companies are fundamentally different from other types of businesses that PE acquires. In a manufacturing company, you can cut costs and optimize operations without fundamentally changing the product. The factory produces the same widgets whether the workforce is 10 percent larger or smaller. But in a software company, the workforce is the product. The code they write, the features they build, the bugs they fix, the architecture decisions they make -- that is the product. When you cut the engineering team, you're cutting the product. When you demoralize the engineering team, you're degrading the product. And when the product degrades, revenue follows, usually with a 12-to-18-month lag that makes the cause-and-effect relationship hard to see in real time. The firms that succeed in software PE are the ones that internalize this truth and act accordingly.

### SEGMENT 8: THE AI INFLECTION AND PE'S NEXT CHAPTER (4 minutes)

**ALEX:** Before we wrap up, I want to touch on something that's clearly on every PE firm's mind right now, which is AI. How is the current wave of AI changing the PE playbook for software companies?

**KEVIN:** AI is the most significant variable in PE software investing since the cloud transition. And it creates both enormous opportunity and enormous risk. On the opportunity side, AI provides a new and incredibly powerful lever for margin expansion. If you own a software company where a significant portion of the value delivery requires human labor -- think customer support, data processing, content moderation, manual quality assurance -- AI gives you a path to automate that labor in a way that was previously impossible. The impact on gross margins is potentially transformative. Companies that run at 60 percent gross margins because of human service delivery could potentially reach 80 or 85 percent gross margins with AI automation. That's a step-change in business quality that dramatically increases exit multiples.

**ALEX:** And on the risk side?

**KEVIN:** The risk is disruption. AI dramatically lowers the barrier to building software, which means that the competitive moats of incumbent software companies are potentially weaker than they've ever been. If you're a PE firm that just paid 10x revenue for a vertical SaaS company, and a well-funded startup can now build 80 percent of your functionality in a fraction of the time using AI-assisted development and foundation models, your investment thesis is in trouble. The defensibility of software companies is shifting from "we have a lot of code" to "we have a lot of data" and "we have deep customer relationships." PE firms need to be evaluating their portfolio companies through that lens. Which companies have proprietary data assets that become more valuable in an AI world? Which ones have customer relationships that are sticky enough to survive a competitive disruption? And which ones are vulnerable because their primary moat was just the accumulated complexity of their codebase, which AI can now replicate more easily?

**ALEX:** That's a sobering framework. It sounds like the next generation of PE operating partners needs to understand AI at a deep level, not just as a buzzword.

**KEVIN:** Absolutely. The technology operating partner of 2020 needed to understand cloud migration, platform consolidation, and engineering effectiveness. The technology operating partner of today and tomorrow needs to understand all of that plus AI strategy, data architecture, and the rapidly evolving landscape of foundation models and AI-native product design. The bar has gone up significantly, and the firms that invest in that expertise will have a meaningful advantage.

### CLOSING (4 minutes)

**ALEX:** Kevin, this has been an incredibly rich conversation. Before we close, I want to ask you a question that I think a lot of our listeners are wondering about. What's the playbook for navigating that transition successfully?

**KEVIN:** That's the right question, and I'll give a few pieces of advice. First, learn the language of value creation. Understand the four levers we discussed today. Understand how your PE firm thinks about returns. Understand the hold period and the exit thesis. When you can frame your technology initiatives in terms of revenue growth, margin expansion, and multiple expansion, you become dramatically more effective at getting investment and support for the things you want to do. Don't come to the board asking for 5 million dollars to reduce technical debt. Come to the board showing how a 5-million-dollar platform investment will improve engineering velocity by 30 percent, which will enable you to ship three new product modules over the next 18 months, which will drive 15 million in incremental ARR. Same initiative, completely different framing.

**ALEX:** What else?

**KEVIN:** Second, build relationships with the operating partners early. Don't wait for them to come to you with a directive. Go to them with your assessment of the technology landscape, your view on the biggest opportunities and risks, and your proposed roadmap. If you can establish yourself as a credible, strategic partner rather than someone who needs to be managed, you'll have much more autonomy and influence. Third, be honest about the problems. PE firms are not naive. They did diligence before they bought the company. They probably already know about the technical debt, the architectural issues, the team gaps. What they're looking for is a leader who will acknowledge those problems, quantify them, and present a credible plan to address them. The worst thing you can do is minimize the issues or promise that everything is fine. Trust is the currency of the PE relationship, and it's earned through transparency, not through optimism.

You will need to make difficult tradeoffs and sometimes sacrifice technical elegance for business pragmatism. If that environment energizes you, PE can be an incredible career accelerator. You'll learn more about business strategy, financial modeling, and organizational transformation in three years at a PE-backed company than in ten years at a big tech company. But if it drains you, if you find the financial focus dispiriting or the pace unsustainable, it's better to recognize that early and make a clean transition rather than grinding through it resentfully.
