# Episode 2: Apple, September 2016
## "Ship a Phone With No Headphone Jack"

**Duration:** ~17 minutes
**Hosts:** Dana Cole & Eli Vance

Last week's decision was about launching something. This one is about *deleting* something. And deletions, it turns out, are where companies show you what they actually believe. <breath> Eli, set the scene.

### THE ROOM: CUPERTINO, LATE 2015 (4 minutes)

**ELI:** It's late 2015, Cupertino, California. Design lock for the fall 2016 iPhone is happening now — hardware decisions get made a year out, so the phone that ships next September is being frozen in this room. And you need to feel the stakes the way this room feels them: the iPhone is roughly two-thirds of Apple's entire revenue. The iPhone 6 cycle that just ended was the most successful consumer product launch ever recorded. And the growth just stopped. Inside these walls, everyone can already see what the world will learn in a few months: for the first time since 2007, iPhone sales are about to *shrink* year over year. The golden goose isn't dead, but it has, for the first time, laid one fewer egg.

**DANA:** And on the table is a proposal from the hardware team that has been circling for years: delete the headphone jack. The engineering case is about real estate. A phone is a fixed-volume war — every cubic millimeter is contested — and the jack is one of the deepest, oldest components in the stack. Remove it and you free up space for a bigger battery, for the new Taptic Engine that makes the redesigned home button work, and you eliminate one of the last big holes you have to seal if you want a water-resistant phone. None of that is fantasy; the volume is real and the engineers can show you exactly where it goes.

**ELI:** The plan on the table has three parts. Part one: the fall 2016 phone ships with no jack. Audio goes through the Lightning port or over Bluetooth. Part two: to soften the landing, the box includes wired EarPods with a Lightning connector *and* a small adapter — a dongle — so your old headphones still work. Part three, and this is the piece that turns a subtraction into a strategy: a brand-new product, fully wireless earbuds called AirPods, with a custom chip — the W1 — designed to fix the single worst thing about Bluetooth, the pairing experience. Price: one hundred and fifty-nine dollars.

**DANA:** Now the context that makes this radioactive. The jack works. Perfectly. It costs Apple nothing in goodwill to keep, and its removal is a pain every single customer feels on day one — every car aux cable, every airplane armrest, every pair of headphones on Earth. Bluetooth's consumer reputation in 2015 is *bad*: pairing hell, dropouts, latency, dead batteries. The press will scream, and they'll have help — Apple bought Beats, a headphone company, for three billion dollars last year, and Apple runs the licensing program that collects a royalty on every Lightning accessory. Any journalist can do that math in an afternoon: Apple breaks the universal standard, then sells you the replacement. And remember the timing — this lands in the first year iPhone sales have ever declined, when rivals are hunting for any billboard that says "Apple has lost it."

**ELI:** So that's the decision. Late 2015: do you lock a fall 2016 phone with no headphone jack — dongles, AirPods, mockery and all — or do you keep the jack, bank the goodwill, and wait until wireless is ready? <breath> The coin came up against me last time, so this week it turned on Dana. She's taking the case against. I'm arguing for the deletion. Nothing after late 2015 is admissible.

[PAUSE]

### THE CASE AGAINST: KEEP THE JACK (5 minutes)

**DANA:** Let me start with the question every product review board on the planet should ask first: what customer problem does this solve? <breath> Sit with that, because the honest answer is: none. Not one iPhone owner has ever asked for fewer ways to listen to music. The jack is invisible when you don't need it and universal when you do. Every removal we've ever gotten credit for — the floppy, the optical drive — removed something *dying*. CD drives were already gathering dust when we pulled them. The headphone jack is not dying. It is the single most successful connector in the history of consumer electronics, and it is on *billions* of working devices. We wouldn't be euthanizing a legacy port. We'd be shooting a healthy one.

**DANA:** Second: look at the year we'd be doing this in. iPhone growth has gone negative for the first time ever. The entire company's narrative for the next twelve months will be "has Apple peaked?" — and into that narrative we would hand every rival a gift-wrapped billboard. Samsung will run ads about it. The late-night monologues write themselves. And the fence-sitters matter here: the whole upgrade machine runs on hundreds of millions of people deciding this is the year to trade up. Give a fence-sitter one concrete, physical annoyance — "my car cable won't plug in" — and some real fraction of them just... waits. In a growth year you can absorb that. In the first *decline* year, you're compounding the exact problem the board is already terrified about.

**ELI:** The adapter's in the box, Dana. Their car cable works fine — through the dongle.

**DANA:** The dongle *is* the problem, Eli — the dongle is a physical apology we're asking customers to carry in their pockets. And it doesn't even fully apologize: with no jack, you cannot charge the phone and listen on wires at the same time. That's not an edge case; that's every desk worker and every long-haul flight. But here's my real argument, the one I'd put on the whiteboard: *who gets paid?* Walk through where the value of this removal flows. The reclaimed space funds our roadmap. The Lightning royalty flows to our licensing program. The wireless future we're forcing runs through AirPods — our product — and Beats — our subsidiary. Now ask what flows to the customer on day one: a dongle. When a company inflicts universal pain and captures the value on its own side of the table, customers have a word for it, and the word is not "courage." That's the difference between a hard call and an arrogant one, and from the outside they look identical for exactly as long as we control the narrative — which is to say, they don't.

**DANA:** And finally — the argument I'd actually win the room with — *waiting is free.* This is not Netflix racing an extinction event. Nobody else can remove our headphone jack; there's no competitor who gets there first. The option to delete the jack in 2017, or 2018, when AirPods are proven, shipping at volume, and Bluetooth's reputation has caught up to reality — that option costs us nothing but one more year of a component we've shipped for a decade. Delete it *then*, into strength, with the replacement already loved. The upside of going now is a few cubic millimeters a year early. The downside is spending our scarcest asset — customer trust, in a declining year — to save space we could have in twelve months anyway. <sigh> That trade should not clear.

---

### THE CASE FOR: DELETE IT NOW (5 minutes)

**ELI:** Everything Dana said is true, and I want to concede the strongest version of it before I answer: this will hurt, the pain is real, day one belongs entirely to her side of the argument. <breath> Now let me tell you why you delete it anyway, and why "waiting is free" is the one sentence in her case that's false.

**ELI:** Start with the space, because "a few cubic millimeters" undersells what's actually being bought. The jack isn't just a hole; it's one of the deepest mechanical assemblies in the phone, and it dictates layout around it. The volume it holds hostage is precisely the volume for the things customers *do* ask for, every single survey, every single year: battery life and durability. Nobody asks for a smaller headphone list, correct — but nobody asks for component-level tradeoffs at all. Customers ask for outcomes. "Better battery, survives the sink" *is* the customer request; it just arrives at this table denominated in cubic millimeters. Our job in this room is currency conversion. That's the job.

**ELI:** Second — the transition never gets cheaper, only more expensive. This is the actual answer to "waiting is free." The install base grows by a couple hundred million people a year. Every year we wait, more cars, more speakers, more habits accrete around the port, and the blast radius of the eventual removal grows. If we don't have the nerve to remove a port at six hundred million users, we will not magically find it at a billion. Incumbency compounds, Dana — you taught me that last week, in a Netflix room. The pain of this deletion is the smallest today that it will ever be. "Later" isn't a cheaper version of this decision. It's a bigger one.

**DANA:** That assumes the removal has to happen at all. Maybe wireless just... arrives on its own schedule, and the jack fades like the floppy did — without us taking the blame.

**ELI:** Except wireless *can't* arrive on its own schedule — that's my third argument. Bluetooth audio in 2015 is stuck in a chicken-and-egg market: headphone makers won't invest seriously in wireless while everyone defaults to the free wired pair in the box, and customers won't switch while wireless remains a flaky premium curiosity. Somebody has to break the loop, and only one actor on Earth *can*: the platform owner. Standards don't die of old age; they die when someone with a platform sets a date. And here's what makes it strategy rather than vandalism: we're not just setting the date, we're shipping the fix. The W1 chip attacks the actual disease — pairing hell — not the symptom. AirPods can't become a mass product in a world where the jack makes them optional. Remove the jack, and we're not killing a port; we're creating the market that our best new product needs to breathe. The removal and the AirPods aren't two decisions. They're one decision, and separating them by a year — Dana's plan — breaks both halves.

**ELI:** Last argument: we have run this exact play four times. The floppy in ninety-eight. The optical drive in 2008. Flash in 2010. The thirty-pin connector in 2012. Every single time, the sequence was identical: universal outrage, roughly two years of mockery, then the industry quietly follows and the transition gets remembered as inevitable. The outrage is not a signal that the decision is wrong. It's the *tax* on being the one who moves first — and the prize for paying it is that we own the transition: the timing, the bridge products, and yes, the margin. <laugh> Dana asks "who gets paid?" like it's an accusation. It's the plan. The alternative isn't that nobody gets paid — it's that we wait, someone else forces the transition, and *they* do.

**DANA:** And if the outrage doesn't fade in two years? If this is the one where the pattern breaks?

**ELI:** Then we ship jacks again in 2018 and eat the crow. The reversal is cheap. But the pattern won't break, because it never breaks for the same reason: customers are angry about the fence until they notice the field. Ship it.

[PAUSE]

### THE REVEAL: WHAT APPLE DID (4 minutes)

[MUSIC STING]

**DANA:** September 7th, 2016. The Bill Graham Civic Auditorium in San Francisco. Apple announces the iPhone 7: no headphone jack, water resistant, bigger battery, new Taptic home button. Lightning EarPods and a dongle in every box. And Phil Schiller stands on that stage and explains the removal with one word — "courage" — and the internet detonates on contact. The mockery is instant, global, and *very* funny. The dongle becomes a meme with its own hashtag. Late night does exactly the monologues I predicted. Google's Pixel launch a month later pointedly advertises its headphone jack as a feature.

**ELI:** And AirPods stumble out of the gate — announced that same day at one hundred fifty-nine dollars, promised for late October, then delayed into mid-December. When they do ship, the photos of little white toothbrush-heads sticking out of people's ears get their own round of ridicule. For about a year, the whole affair looks like the case Dana made: self-inflicted pain, in a declining year, to sell us our own headphones.

**DANA:** And then — and by now you know this show's rhythm — the compounding starts. The W1 pairing experience turns out to be the real product: flip the lid, they just connect. AirPods become impossible to manufacture fast enough for over a year. They cross from gadget to cultural object — the thing you see on every train platform on Earth. Apple's wearables segment, which barely existed as a reporting line, grows into a business doing on the order of *forty billion dollars* a year by the early twenties — AirPods, Watch, and Beats together out-earning most of the Fortune 500 on their own. And the industry that ran the attack ads? Google removed the Pixel's headphone jack one year after advertising it. Samsung removed theirs within three, and quietly deleted the ads that had mocked the dongle. The iPhone 7 itself, by the way, sold perfectly well — the fence-sitter exodus never materialized.

**ELI:** Intellectual honesty, though, because this show earns its keep on the other half: the dongle era was genuinely user-hostile, and everyone who lived it knows it. You really couldn't charge and listen on wires without buying a second adapter. Apple pulled the dongle from the box two years in — the apology stopped shipping before the pain did. And "courage" itself became a permanent piece of tech vocabulary for corporate tone-deafness. The decision was right. The *word* was arrogant. Both things are true, and the room that made the first call also chose the second.

### WHAT TRANSFERS (5 minutes)

**DANA:** Alright — what you carry out of this room. I'll start, and I'm starting with my concession from the losing side. I argued "waiting is free," and history says that's the seductive lie in every removal decision: for a platform owner, the cost of a transition *compounds with the install base*. If you're ever going to delete the thing, the blast radius is smallest right now. "Next year, when we're stronger" usually means "next year, when it's bigger."

**ELI:** Two: subtraction is only a strategy if you own the replacement path — the whole path. Apple didn't ship an absence; it shipped Lightning EarPods in the box, a free bridge for the old world, and a genuinely better future at a price. Remove something and ship only the absence, and you're not courageous, you're just cheaper. The test isn't whether you dare to delete. It's whether the customer's very next step is one you've already paved.

**DANA:** Three — budget for the gap. The criticism of a removal arrives in week one, at maximum volume, aimed at named executives. The benefit compounds silently over years and gets attributed to inevitability. That asymmetry means these decisions need political capital *reserved in advance* — a leadership team that has agreed, before the keynote, that two years of mockery is the tax and nobody flinches. If your organization punishes the person who signed the removal during the mockery window, you will never make one of these calls again, right or wrong.

**ELI:** And four — the courage-versus-arrogance test is real, even though I won the argument. Ask where the value released by the customer's pain flows *on day one*. Apple paired the pain with same-day benefit — water resistance, battery, a free adapter — so the story survived. If the only beneficiary visible at launch is your own margin, the customers aren't wrong to call it a toll booth, no matter how right your ten-year thesis is. You need the thesis *and* the day-one receipt.
