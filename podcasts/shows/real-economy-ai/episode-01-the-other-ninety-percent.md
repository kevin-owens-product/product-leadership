# Episode 1: The Other Ninety Percent
## "Why AI Value in Private Equity Lives Outside Software"

**Duration:** ~49 minutes
**Hosts:** Maya & Grant
**Podcast:** AI in the Real Economy

---

### Cold open

[INTRO MUSIC]

**MAYA:** Picture two portfolio companies owned by the same fund. One is a software business with two hundred engineers. The other runs four hundred HVAC technicians across three states. Which one has more to gain from AI this year?

**GRANT:** Most people would say the software company. And I think most people are wrong. The software company has been told for three years that AI is its future, it has engineers who already use the tools every day, and its buyers already expect an AI story. The HVAC business is still sending a quarter of its inbound calls to voicemail, building quotes by hand, and invoicing a week after the job is done.

**MAYA:** So the opportunity is bigger where the starting point is lower.

**GRANT:** Bigger, and often easier to measure. When a call that used to go to voicemail turns into a booked job, you can see it in the revenue line the same month. When a quote that took three days now takes three hours, you can see the win rate move.

**MAYA:** Welcome to AI in the Real Economy, the show about AI value creation in the businesses that make, fix, deliver and care for things. I'm Maya.

**GRANT:** And I'm Grant. This season we're spending four long episodes on the kinds of companies that make up most private equity portfolios: services, healthcare, manufacturing and consumer businesses. We'll ask where AI actually moves EBITDA, how to size it, how to run it, and how it goes wrong.

**MAYA:** A note on sources before we start. Everything we cite comes from public reports, press coverage and published research. When a number comes from a vendor or from a fund describing its own portfolio, we'll say so, because those numbers are usually true in the way that a company's best quarter is true.

**GRANT:** And when we use an example company to walk through the math, we'll tell you it's a made-up example. No real portfolio companies were harmed in the making of this episode.

### The new deal math

**MAYA:** Let's start with why this matters now, and not five years ago. What's changed in private equity?

**GRANT:** The math changed. Bain published its midyear private equity report this year, and the headline was what they called a triple shock that stalled the industry's latest recovery attempt. An AI-driven selloff in software valuations, redemption stress in private credit, and an energy price spike. Bid-ask spreads widened, investment committees pulled back, and exits slowed again.

**MAYA:** And the numbers that jumped out at you?

**GRANT:** Three of them. First, implied holding periods of about seven years, which is well beyond the historical norm. Second, about thirty three thousand unsold portfolio companies waiting for an exit. Third, and this is the one every operating partner should have on a sticky note, Bain's framing of "twelve is the new five."

**MAYA:** Explain that.

**GRANT:** A decade ago, a deal needed roughly five percent annual EBITDA growth to deliver a two and a half times return. Bain's estimate now is that the same deal needs something like ten to twelve percent. Higher entry prices, more expensive debt, and less help from multiple expansion. So the growth has to come from the business itself.

**MAYA:** Which means operational value creation during the hold.

**GRANT:** Exactly. You can't count on buying low and selling high when everybody paid high and nobody's buying. You have to make the business genuinely better while you own it, and you probably have to own it longer than you planned. And Bain put AI squarely in that picture. Their phrase was that inaction is now a strategic choice.

**MAYA:** There's a timing point too. If you're holding for seven years, the AI capability available at exit will be very different from what's available at entry.

**GRANT:** That's right, and it cuts both ways. A company that builds AI into how it operates during the hold can show a buyer a better business. A company that does nothing may find that its competitors did, and that the buyer's diligence team asks hard questions about why the margins look like they did in 2022.

**MAYA:** Bain had another point that I think is underrated. They talked about avoiding the middle of the holding period.

**GRANT:** Yes. The idea is that value is often lost in the long middle of a hold, after the first-year plan has run out of steam and before exit preparation starts. Refreshing the value creation plan and the incentives in that middle stretch matters. And AI is a natural reason to refresh a plan, because the opportunity set today is different from what it was when the original plan was written.

### Why the real economy

**MAYA:** Now the obvious objection. Isn't AI a technology story? Why look for it in a plumbing roll-up or a contract manufacturer?

**GRANT:** Because AI value shows up wherever there's a lot of repetitive judgment work done by people, and wherever revenue leaks because nobody had time to catch it. Non-software companies are full of both. Call centers. Dispatch desks. Estimating. Claims and billing. Quality inspection. Scheduling. Collections. Compliance paperwork.

**MAYA:** Give me a picture of what that looks like on a profit and loss statement.

**GRANT:** Let me use a made-up example. Call it Ridgeline Services, a regional commercial cleaning and facilities maintenance company. Eighty million dollars of revenue, about two thousand employees, most of them frontline. Labor is the biggest cost by far. Then there's a back office of maybe a hundred and fifty people: customer service, scheduling, billing, payroll, recruiting, account management.

**MAYA:** And where does AI touch that?

**GRANT:** Almost everywhere except the actual cleaning. Recruiting frontline staff, which in that kind of business is a constant churn problem. Scheduling crews across hundreds of sites. Answering customer requests and complaints. Writing and pricing bids for new contracts. Checking that work was done to the contract standard. Billing accurately and on time. Each of those is a workflow with real people, real cost and real leakage.

**MAYA:** And the labor market makes it urgent.

**GRANT:** It does. Foley and Lardner published a piece in May on AI, automation and robotics in private equity, and they singled out labor-constrained industries: logistics yards, food service, construction, field services and healthcare delivery. Businesses facing labor shortages, wage inflation or big seasonal swings. In those businesses, the first job of AI isn't cutting people. It's doing work you can't hire for, or freeing the people you have for the work only they can do.

**MAYA:** I want to stay on that, because it changes the conversation with management teams.

**GRANT:** It changes everything. If you walk into a services company and say "we're going to use AI to reduce headcount," you've just made every manager in the building an opponent. If you say "you have twenty open roles in scheduling and customer service that you can't fill, and your best people are burning out, so let's take the worst parts of their day away," you have allies. And in most of these businesses, the second framing is closer to the truth.

**MAYA:** Foley also made an exit argument.

**GRANT:** They did. With a jammed exit environment and longer holds, value creation during the hold matters more. And they drew a useful distinction. One-time cost cuts tend to get competed away. If you cut your prices because your costs fell, your competitors eventually do the same. But building AI into how the business operates, at the platform level, gives you more durable margin and scalability. That durability is what a buyer pays a multiple for.

### What the evidence actually says

**MAYA:** Let's look at evidence. I want both the exciting numbers and the honest ones.

**GRANT:** Good, because the honest picture is more useful. Start with what the leaders say. Permira's team gave an interview to Private Equity International in September 2025 about AI value creation beyond the tech sector. They described AI being applied inside sector-specific workflows in services, healthcare and consumer businesses.

**MAYA:** And the examples?

**GRANT:** In pharma services, they said AI in drug formulation cut a cycle from about ten weeks to four or five days. In clinical services, they said AI in adverse event case processing cut intake time by a quarter and improved auditability. In legal services, they described using AI to match lawyers to projects. Those are the fund's own reports about its own companies, so treat them as claims. But notice what they have in common. None of them is a chatbot bolted onto a website. All of them sit inside the core work the customer pays for.

**MAYA:** What about manufacturing?

**GRANT:** Two surveys from this year are worth knowing. Parsec Automation surveyed twelve hundred manufacturing leaders in February, published in July. Seventy two percent said they'd adopted AI in some form, up from fifty three percent in 2024. But only ten percent had deployed it across their operations. Top use cases were quality control, IT operations and supply chain management. Top barriers were implementation cost, data privacy and security, and integration with existing systems.

**MAYA:** And the second?

**GRANT:** Augury, which sells machine health monitoring, so factor that in, surveyed about five hundred US and European manufacturers with revenues above fifty million dollars. They found the share of firms scaling AI across more than half their facilities jumped from fourteen percent to forty two percent year over year. Predictive maintenance was the leading use case, at fifty seven percent. And the top operational challenges were workforce constraints and unplanned downtime, both rising. Data quality was the leading barrier.

**MAYA:** Those two surveys disagree on how many companies are at scale.

**GRANT:** They define scale differently and sample differently, so I wouldn't put much weight on the exact numbers. But they agree on the shape. Nearly everyone has started. A growing minority has scaled. And the things that hold people back are data, integration and cost, not the AI itself.

**MAYA:** Consumer businesses?

**GRANT:** Yum Brands is the most public example. On its third quarter call in early November, the company said more than nine hundred Taco Bell restaurants were using voice AI in the drive-thru, with over fifteen hundred expected by the end of 2026, and it reported about a six percent gain in drive-thru satisfaction and roughly a one and a half percent sales uplift where it's deployed. It also said a kitchen display system at KFC was associated with eight percent faster drive-thru speeds and twelve percent fewer refunds.

**MAYA:** And the honest numbers you promised?

**GRANT:** Healthcare gives us the best example of careful measurement. In April, JAMA published a study led by Mass General Brigham and UCSF that followed more than eighteen hundred clinicians using AI scribes across five hospitals for over two years, compared with more than six thousand seven hundred controls. The scribes cut documentation time by about sixteen minutes a day, around ten percent. Clinicians saw about half an extra patient visit a week. The revenue effect was statistically significant but small, around a hundred and sixty seven dollars a month per clinician.

**MAYA:** That's a lot less than the marketing.

**GRANT:** Much less. And there's a detail in that study I love. The clinicians who used the scribe for more than half of their visits saw three times the documentation reduction. But only about a third of users got to that level. So the technology worked when people used it, and most people didn't use it enough.

**MAYA:** Which tells you the bottleneck.

**GRANT:** Adoption, not capability. And that's the most important lesson for anyone running AI in a real-economy business. The tool is rarely the hard part. Getting people to change how they work, consistently, is the hard part.

### Four levers, in depth

**MAYA:** Let's give people a framework. When you walk into a non-software portfolio company, where do you look?

**GRANT:** I use four levers. Revenue capture, labor leverage, working capital, and quality and risk. Let me take each one properly, with examples.

**MAYA:** Start with revenue capture.

**GRANT:** Revenue capture is money the business has already earned, or nearly earned, but doesn't collect. Missed calls are the classic one. Unquoted leads. Quotes that nobody followed up on. Jobs completed but under-billed because the technician forgot to log a part. Service agreements that expired because nobody called to renew. In healthcare, claims that were denied and never appealed, or visits coded below what was actually documented.

**MAYA:** Why is AI good at this?

**GRANT:** Because leakage is mostly a problem of attention. Every one of those leaks happens because a person was busy, tired or didn't notice. AI doesn't get tired at four on a Friday. It can listen to every call, read every job note, check every invoice against the work order, and flag every expiring contract. It's patient in a way that humans can't afford to be.

**MAYA:** And it's measurable.

**GRANT:** That's the real advantage. You can usually measure revenue capture within weeks, because the baseline is in the data you already have. Last year, this many calls went unanswered. This year, with the AI agent, this many of those calls became booked jobs. That's a clean story for a board.

**MAYA:** Second lever, labor leverage.

**GRANT:** Labor leverage is getting more output from the same people, especially scarce experts. A field technician who spends forty minutes a day on paperwork. An estimator who builds every quote from scratch. A nurse who documents for an hour after every shift. A dispatcher who spends half the morning on the phone rearranging the schedule after one person calls in sick.

**MAYA:** And the value is that you've effectively hired without hiring.

**GRANT:** Right. If you give a technician thirty minutes a day back, and they use it to complete one more job a week, across four hundred technicians that's a lot of revenue capacity, with no recruiting cost and no ramp time. But, and this is the JAMA lesson again, time saved is not the same as value created. If the saved time disappears into longer lunches or more admin elsewhere, you got a happier workforce, which is worth something, but not the EBITDA you modeled.

**MAYA:** So you have to decide in advance what the saved time is for.

**GRANT:** Exactly. Saved time needs a destination. More jobs per day, faster quote turnaround, more patients seen, or a hiring plan that doesn't need to grow as fast as revenue. Write it into the plan, and measure the destination, not just the saving.

**MAYA:** Third lever, working capital.

**GRANT:** This one's underrated because it shows up in cash, not EBITDA. But cash is what funds care about, especially in a world of long holds and low distributions. Invoicing the day the job is done instead of a week later. Catching billing errors before the customer disputes them. Chasing overdue invoices politely and persistently. Forecasting inventory more accurately so you're not sitting on parts you don't need. Each day of days sales outstanding you take out of an eighty-million-dollar business is worth something like two hundred thousand dollars of cash.

**MAYA:** And it compounds across a portfolio.

**GRANT:** It does, and it's often the easiest win to get finance teams excited about, because it's their language.

**MAYA:** Fourth lever, quality and risk.

**GRANT:** Fewer defects, fewer safety incidents, fewer compliance misses, better documentation, fewer customer complaints. It's harder to put a precise number on, but it protects the multiple. A buyer's diligence team will find the defects and the compliance gaps either way. Better to find them yourself, fix them, and show a clean record.

**MAYA:** Which lever usually pays first?

**GRANT:** Revenue capture, almost every time. It's measurable within weeks, it doesn't require anyone to change their job very much, and it funds the harder work. Labor leverage comes second, and it's where the biggest long-term value usually sits, but it needs real change management. Working capital is a great quick win for the finance team. Quality and risk is the slow, durable one that shows up at exit.

### Sizing the opportunity

**MAYA:** Let's do some math. An operating partner walks into our made-up company, Ridgeline Services. How do they size the AI opportunity in the first month?

**GRANT:** They resist the urge to start with a vendor demo, and they start with the company's own data. Let me walk through three workflows.

**MAYA:** Go.

**GRANT:** First, inbound customer requests. Ridgeline gets, say, four thousand calls and emails a month from clients: extra cleaning requests, complaints, schedule changes, new site inquiries. Pull the phone logs and the inbox. Suppose fifteen percent of calls go unanswered, mostly after hours and at lunch. Suppose a tenth of those were requests for additional paid work, worth an average of four hundred dollars, and you'd win most of them if you answered. That's sixty requests a month, call it two hundred and fifty thousand dollars a year, from one workflow. Small, but real, and fast.

**MAYA:** Second.

**GRANT:** Bids. Ridgeline bids on new contracts every week. Each bid takes an estimator two days to build: walk the site, measure, price labor, write the proposal. Suppose they submit two hundred bids a year and win a quarter of them. If AI drafting cuts bid time in half, the same estimators can submit more bids, and respond faster, which in a lot of markets raises the win rate. Even a modest increase in wins on a business with multi-year contracts is worth millions in lifetime revenue.

**MAYA:** And third.

**GRANT:** Billing. Suppose Ridgeline invoices on average nine days after the end of the month, and has a two percent rate of invoice disputes, each of which delays payment by another month. Automating invoice generation from the scheduling and timekeeping systems, and checking each invoice against the contract before it goes out, could cut days to invoice and halve the disputes. That's a cash number, and finance will calculate it in an afternoon.

**MAYA:** Is there a fourth workflow you'd look at?

**GRANT:** Scheduling, because at Ridgeline labor is most of the cost base. Cleaning crews are scheduled by site managers using a mix of software and memory. When someone calls in sick, the site manager spends an hour on the phone finding cover, and often pays overtime to whoever answers first. Pull a year of timekeeping data and look at two numbers: overtime as a share of labor cost, and the number of shifts covered at the last minute. If overtime runs at six percent of a labor bill of, say, fifty million dollars, that's three million dollars, and even a modest reduction from better forecasting and faster cover matching is a seven-figure number.

**MAYA:** And that's the kind of number that moves a valuation.

**GRANT:** It is, but it's also the one that needs the most change management, because it touches how site managers do their jobs every day. So it's rarely the first project. It's the one you size in month one, so everyone knows it's coming, and start properly once the quick win has built some trust.

**MAYA:** So by the end of the first month, the operating partner has three numbers.

**GRANT:** Three ranges, really. Low, middle and high. Each one built from the company's own data, each one with a named owner who agrees the math is plausible. That's worth far more than a consultant's estimate that "AI can deliver fifteen to twenty percent productivity improvement across the back office."

**MAYA:** Why ranges?

**GRANT:** Because you don't know the adoption rate yet. Remember the scribe study: the tool worked for the people who used it, and a third used it heavily. Your middle case should assume realistic adoption, not perfect adoption.

### Operating model

**MAYA:** Let's talk about who runs this. Inside the portfolio company, who owns AI?

**GRANT:** Permira's team said every portfolio company should have a chief AI officer or equivalent with a seat on the management team, and specifically not tucked away in IT. I agree with the spirit of that. I'd put it more simply. The person who owns the workflow has to own the AI in that workflow.

**MAYA:** Say more.

**GRANT:** If the use case is dispatch, the head of operations owns it. If it's billing, the controller owns it. If it's the phone, the head of customer service owns it. They own the target, the rollout and the result. A central AI lead, whatever the title, helps them: picks vendors, sets standards, handles data and security, and keeps a portfolio view of what's working. But the accountability sits with the business owner. When AI is an IT project, it produces impressive pilots and no EBITDA.

**MAYA:** What about the fund's role?

**GRANT:** The fund is the force multiplier. A few things funds do well. Shared playbooks: if one portfolio company has made after-hours call handling work, the next one shouldn't start from scratch. Vendor leverage: a fund can negotiate better terms and do the security review once for many companies. Talent: a small bench of operators who've done this before and can parachute in for ninety days. Forums: Permira described regular AI and technology leader forums across the portfolio, including cross-sector sessions, where people share what worked and what didn't. And expectations: an AI line in the value creation plan, reviewed at board meetings like any other initiative.

**MAYA:** And build versus buy?

**GRANT:** For most real-economy companies, buy. The vendors for vertical workflows, like field service management, revenue cycle, quality inspection and restaurant operations, have spent years on the boring integration work. What you build is the glue: the data connections, the workflow design, the measurement. The exception is when a workflow is genuinely unique to your business and central to your advantage. Then building can make sense, but go in with your eyes open about maintenance.

**MAYA:** There's a vendor-lock-in risk, though.

**GRANT:** There is, and it's worth a clause in every contract. Who owns the data? Can you export it in a usable form? Is your data used to train the vendor's models? What happens to your workflows if the vendor gets acquired or raises prices fifty percent at renewal? We'll come back to data rights in episode four, because it can become a diligence problem at exit.

### Choosing and managing vendors

**MAYA:** You said buy, not build. But a mid-sized company that goes looking for an AI vendor this year finds hundreds of them. How does an operating team choose?

**GRANT:** I'd start by flipping the question. Don't ask which AI vendor is best. Ask which system of record this workflow lives in, and what that system's vendor already offers or integrates with. If your field service software already has an AI call answering product that writes straight into its own dispatch board, that product starts with a huge advantage over a standalone tool, even if the standalone tool sounds better in a demo.

**MAYA:** Because integration is the hard part.

**GRANT:** Integration is the hard part, and it's also where demos lie by omission. Every demo shows the AI doing the clever thing. Very few demos show what happens when the customer's address is wrong in the CRM, or the technician's calendar has a block nobody explained, or the price book has three versions of the same part. Ask the vendor to run on a copy of your data, not theirs, before you sign anything substantial.

**MAYA:** What else goes on the checklist?

**GRANT:** Six things. First, reference customers that look like you, in size and in sector, and actually call them. Ask what broke in month two, not what impressed them in week one. Second, pricing that you can model. A lot of AI products price per minute, per conversation, per resolution or per seat, and the difference matters. Per-resolution pricing sounds aligned with your interests until you realize the vendor defines what counts as a resolution.

**MAYA:** That's a good one.

**GRANT:** Third, data terms, which we'll come back to in episode four. Where your data goes, how long it's kept, whether it trains anyone's model, and how you get it back. Fourth, controls. Can you see every conversation or decision the AI made? Can you set rules about what it may and may not say? Can you switch it off for one site in an afternoon? Fifth, the escalation path to a human, and how well it carries context. A handoff where the customer has to repeat everything is worse than no AI at all.

**MAYA:** And sixth?

**GRANT:** Exit terms. What happens if you leave. How long the transition takes, what format the data comes back in, and whether any of the workflow logic you built belongs to you. Funds think about exits constantly at the company level. They should think about exits at the vendor level too.

**MAYA:** Should the fund pick vendors for the whole portfolio?

**GRANT:** The fund should do the expensive parts once: the security review, the legal review of standard terms, a pricing benchmark, and a short list. Then each company picks from the short list based on its own systems. Forcing one vendor on every company sounds efficient, but a dental group and an HVAC roll-up don't run on the same software, and the integration cost swamps any volume discount.

**MAYA:** What about the big general-purpose AI tools? The chat assistants everyone has on their phone.

**GRANT:** They have a real role, mostly in the office. Drafting, summarizing, analysis, research, writing first versions of policies and proposals. Giving a finance team or an estimating team a properly licensed business version, with the data protections turned on, is one of the cheapest productivity moves available. But they're not a substitute for the workflow tools. The chat assistant doesn't answer the phone at nine at night and book the job into the dispatch board. And the worst outcome is staff pasting customer records into a free consumer account because nobody gave them a sanctioned alternative.

**MAYA:** Shadow AI.

**GRANT:** Shadow AI. It's happening in every company, whether the management team knows or not. The fix isn't a ban. It's a good, approved tool and a one-page policy that people can actually remember.

### What goes wrong

**MAYA:** Now the failure modes. I've seen a lot of real-economy AI programs stall. What are the patterns?

**GRANT:** Five big ones. The first is pilot sprawl. Every site manager tries a different tool, nothing connects to the system of record, and after a year you have twelve pilots, a lot of subscriptions, and no EBITDA. The fix is to pick a small number of workflows and run each one across the whole business, not one site at a time forever.

**MAYA:** Second.

**GRANT:** Treating AI as an IT project, which we just covered. Third, ignoring the people doing the work. In a services business, the technicians, the call center staff and the dispatchers decide whether AI works. If they see it as a threat, they'll route around it. If they see it as taking the worst parts of their day away, they'll make it better by telling you where it fails. Change management isn't a soft add-on here. It is the plan.

**MAYA:** Fourth.

**GRANT:** Measurement theater. Reporting usage instead of outcomes. "Ninety percent of staff have logged in" tells you nothing. "After-hours calls answered went from zero to ninety five percent, and booked jobs from those calls added this much revenue" tells you everything. Insist on outcome metrics from the start, with a baseline taken before the rollout.

**MAYA:** And fifth.

**GRANT:** Data debt. Many real-economy companies run on a patchwork: an old ERP, a field service app, spreadsheets, and a lot of knowledge in people's heads. AI that can't see the data can't help. Every survey we mentioned put data quality or integration near the top of the barriers. Sometimes the first AI project is actually a data project wearing an AI hat, and that's fine, as long as you know it going in and budget for it.

**MAYA:** Any warning signs a board member should watch for?

**GRANT:** Three. A program that reports activities rather than outcomes. A program where nobody outside IT can name the use cases. And a program where the same use case has been "in pilot" for more than two quarters. Any of those means it's time for a hard conversation.

### Bringing people with you

**MAYA:** You said change management is the plan. Make that practical. What does it look like in a business with four hundred technicians or two thousand cleaners?

**GRANT:** It starts before the tool. The first conversation with the front line shouldn't be "here's your new AI." It should be "what's the worst part of your day?" In a field services business the answers are remarkably consistent. Paperwork at the end of the day. Getting sent to a job without the right part. Customers who weren't told the technician was running late. Dispatch calling you mid-job to send you somewhere else. If the first AI project fixes one of those, you've earned the right to do the second one.

**MAYA:** So you pick the use case partly for its politics.

**GRANT:** Partly, yes. The first project should be something the front line will thank you for, not something they'll suspect. Revenue capture on the phone is good for that, because it doesn't change anyone's job much. It just means the calls that used to go to voicemail now become work. Technicians like more booked jobs. Call center staff like not drowning at eight on a Monday.

**MAYA:** And the harder ones, where jobs do change?

**GRANT:** Then you need a few things. A clear, honest story about what the AI will and won't do. Champions on the front line, real people with credibility, who use it first and can tell their colleagues the truth about it. Training that's short and happens in the flow of work, not a three-hour classroom session. A fast feedback channel, so when the AI gets something wrong, the person who noticed can report it in ten seconds and see it fixed. And some patience, because adoption curves are real.

**MAYA:** The scribe study again.

**GRANT:** The scribe study again. A third of users became heavy users. If you'd judged the tool on the average user in month two, you'd have killed it. If you'd judged it on the heavy users, you'd have overestimated it. The job of management is to understand why the heavy users got there and help more people get there.

**MAYA:** What about incentives?

**GRANT:** Incentives matter a lot, and they're often pointed the wrong way. If a call center agent is measured on average handle time, and the AI now handles the easy calls, the agent's average handle time goes up, because they only get the hard calls. Unless you change the metric, you've just made your best people look worse. Same with technicians paid per job: if AI dispatch sends them on more efficient routes but fewer high-value jobs, they'll notice before you do.

**MAYA:** So you have to redesign the scorecard with the workflow.

**GRANT:** Every time. And be honest about headcount. In most real-economy businesses right now, the honest message is that AI lets the company grow without hiring at the same rate, and that the people here will do more of the skilled work and less of the drudgery. If that's true, say it, and keep saying it. If it isn't true, if the plan really does reduce roles, don't pretend otherwise. People can tell, and the cost of being caught out is much higher than the cost of a hard conversation. We'll come back to the legal side of that in the next episode.

**MAYA:** And managers?

**GRANT:** Managers are the layer that gets forgotten. The site manager, the dispatch supervisor, the practice manager. They're the ones who decide every day whether the new way is the way. If they weren't involved in choosing it, and they don't understand the numbers, they'll quietly let people go back to the old way. Bring them in early, give them the dashboard, and make the results part of how they're reviewed.

### Questions we keep hearing

**MAYA:** Let's do a lightning round. These are the questions operating partners and management teams ask most often. First one. Shouldn't we wait for the technology to get better?

**GRANT:** No, and for a specific reason. The technology will keep getting better, but the hard work, which is cleaning up the data, connecting the systems and changing how people work, doesn't get any easier by waiting. A company that spends this year getting its data into shape and running two good deployments will absorb next year's better models in weeks. A company that waited will spend next year doing the groundwork.

**MAYA:** Second. Will AI replace our technicians, nurses, or tradespeople?

**GRANT:** Not in any time frame that matters for a current fund. The physical, skilled, trusted work is the part AI is worst at. What changes is everything around it: the booking, the paperwork, the routing, the follow-up. If anything, AI makes skilled people more valuable, because more of their day goes to the work only they can do.

**MAYA:** Third. We're a small company with messy data. Is this even for us?

**GRANT:** Often more than for bigger companies. A small company with messy data usually has the most obvious leaks. The first project might be very simple: a voice agent that answers after-hours calls and sends a text summary to the on-call person, without even writing into a system. That's not elegant, but it captures revenue. Meanwhile you start the data work.

**MAYA:** Fourth. How much should we budget?

**GRANT:** Less than people fear for the software, more than they expect for the people. Many of these tools cost a few hundred to a few thousand dollars a month per site or per team. The bigger costs are integration work, an internal owner's time, training, and the management attention to see it through. A useful rule is to budget the first year so that the quick win pays for the bigger bet. If the quick win can't pay for itself in a year, it wasn't a quick win.

**MAYA:** Fifth. What about the cost of the AI itself, the usage charges?

**GRANT:** Watch it, but don't obsess. Model prices per unit of work have been falling steadily, and for most real-economy workflows the AI cost per transaction is small compared with the value of the transaction. A booked furnace repair is worth hundreds of dollars. The AI minutes to book it cost a tiny fraction of that. Where usage costs do matter is high-volume, low-value work, like answering simple status questions at scale. There, design matters: use the cheapest approach that does the job well.

**MAYA:** Sixth. Should the fund build its own AI platform for the portfolio?

**GRANT:** Usually not a full platform. A fund isn't a software company, and the portfolio companies run on different systems. What does work is a thin shared layer: approved vendors, shared contract terms, reusable playbooks, a small team of experts, and maybe some shared tooling for things like document processing that look the same everywhere. Build the muscle, not the monolith.

**MAYA:** Seventh. How do we know if a vendor's AI claims are real?

**GRANT:** Ask for a test on your own data with your own success measure, agreed before the test starts. Ask for the numbers from a customer like you, and call that customer. And look at the vendor's incentives. A vendor who sells call handling will tell you missed calls are your biggest problem. They might be right. Check.

**MAYA:** Last one. What's the single biggest predictor of success?

**GRANT:** A named business owner who wants it to work and has the authority to change the workflow. Every other factor, the vendor, the data, the budget, can be fixed by that person. Without them, nothing else matters.

### The exit story

**MAYA:** Let's end the main part with the exit. If you do this well, what does a buyer actually pay for?

**GRANT:** Durable, demonstrable improvement. A buyer doesn't pay for "we use AI." Everyone will say that by 2028. A buyer pays for a better business: higher margins that are clearly sustainable, faster growth with less hiring, better cash conversion, and lower risk. AI is the mechanism, but the story is the numbers.

**MAYA:** How do you make it demonstrable?

**GRANT:** Keep the evidence. Baselines before each rollout. Monthly outcome metrics after. Documentation of how each AI system works, what data it uses, who owns it, and what controls are in place. A buyer's diligence team will ask, and an organized answer is worth a lot more than a slide that says "AI-enabled."

**MAYA:** And the risk side of that story?

**GRANT:** Buyers are getting sharper. Foley noted that representations and warranties insurers are starting to exclude AI-specific risks. So the quality of your AI governance can affect the deal terms. Clean data rights, clear vendor contracts, and documented controls are part of the value, not overhead.


**MAYA:** What does that look like in the data room?

**GRANT:** I'd want four things in it. A one-page register of every AI system in production: what it does, which workflow, which vendor, who owns it, and what data it touches. A short results pack for each of the big ones: the baseline, the monthly trend since launch, and how the numbers were calculated, ideally reconciled by finance, not by the vendor. The contracts, with the data and exit terms easy to find. And the controls: the policy, the approvals, the monitoring, and what happened the last time something went wrong.

**MAYA:** The last time something went wrong?

**GRANT:** Yes. A buyer trusts a company that can say "in March our phone agent started misreading one type of request, we caught it in two days from our weekly review, here's what we changed." That's evidence of a working control. A company that claims nothing ever went wrong is either not looking or not telling.

**MAYA:** And how do you talk about it in the equity story?

**GRANT:** Carefully. The temptation is to rebrand the whole business as "AI-enabled" and ask for a technology multiple. Sophisticated buyers will discount that, and they'll pick it apart in diligence. The stronger story is a services or healthcare or industrial business that's measurably better run than its peers, with margin and cash conversion to prove it, and a credible pipeline of further improvements the next owner can capture. That's a story a buyer can underwrite, and it leaves something on the table for them, which is what makes them want to pay.
