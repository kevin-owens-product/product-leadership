# DEADWATER
## Episode 4: "Twelve Years of Silence"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] Cold cases do not freeze evenly.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The public understanding of a cold case is that it goes still - that at some point investigators reach the edge of what the evidence can tell them and the file closes and there it sits, static, until something breaks. That is not how it actually works. Cold cases breathe. They expand and contract. They surface and subside. And for the people connected to them - the families, the investigators, the survivors - they are never cold at all.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This episode is about the years between. The twelve years between the discovery of the bodies and the formation of the task force that finally broke the case. It is about what those years cost. And about what persisted through them.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In the years when the Deadwater investigation was running on minimal resources, Carol Marsh kept her own calendar. Every year, on the anniversary of the discovery of the bodies, she took the case file home and spent a weekend reading through it. From the beginning. Every page.

[MARSH] It sounds compulsive. My therapist has had thoughts about it. But it was strategic, in my head at least. I was looking for things I'd missed. Connections that hadn't existed when I first read those pages that might exist now because of something else I'd learned in the intervening year. Sometimes I found them. Small things, usually. But small things add up.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] And honestly, it was also about the victims. Reading their files every year meant I was thinking about them as people, not just as evidence sets. Renee had a three-year-old son. Sandra had two daughters who were ten and twelve when she disappeared. Ava was twenty-three and she was saving money to go to nursing school. These are women who had lives and plans and people who loved them. That matters. It should matter to every person who touches a case like this.

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese, who works the case alongside Marsh through the lean years, develops a different approach. He maintains a separate notebook - physical, handwritten - that he calls his "thread book." Every connection, however thin, between any element of the case and any person or place he encounters in his other investigations.

[REESE] I worked nine other active cases during those years. Deadwater wasn't my only case. But it was always in the background. And sometimes things come up in other investigations that ring a bell. A name that's connected to a location. A vehicle description that matches something you read once in a file from a different year. You write it down. Most of it leads nowhere. But you write it down.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] In 2017, I'm working a property fraud case. Completely unrelated. And one of the witnesses in that case mentions, in passing, that they hired an architect to do permit work on a commercial building near the shore. They give me the name of the firm. And something about it pulls at me. I can't tell you why. I write it in the thread book. I move on.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That firm name comes up again five years later in the task force investigation. By that point I had forty-seven entries in my thread book related, however tangentially, to the case. That one was entry number nineteen.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey spends the years between becoming, by any measure, an expert.

[DONNA] I didn't set out to become an expert in anything. I set out to find out what happened to my sister. But the way you find out what happened to your sister is that you learn everything there is to learn about how these cases work, and how they don't work, and what the research says about why some go cold and some get solved. So I started reading. Court documents. Academic papers. Journal articles by forensic investigators. I read everything I could get my hands on.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] At some point I started talking to other families. Other women who had lost someone to an unsolved case, some of them in similar circumstances. We found each other online at first, and then we started meeting in person. There were seven of us originally. By the time the task force was announced in 2022, there were twenty-six families connected to our group. Not all related to Deadwater. Some were related to other cold cases in the county.

[NARRATOR] The group Donna helps build becomes a formal advocacy organization. They lobby for cold case legislation. They push for reforms in how missing persons reports are handled when the missing person is a woman working in the sex trade. They appear before county committees. They testify in Albany.

[DONNA] The legislation we pushed for - and this takes years, it doesn't happen quickly - requires the department to formally link missing persons reports to open homicide investigations when the geographic and demographic profiles match. It sounds obvious. It is obvious. But it was not being done. Ava's mother filed a missing persons report fourteen months before her daughter's body was found. That report was never cross-referenced with the Deadwater file. If it had been, we might have had a lead sooner.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The bill passes in 2019. It is named after Donna's sister. She does not celebrate. She drives home from Albany, makes dinner for her son, and sits at her kitchen table for a long time in the dark.

[DONNA] Renee would have found the whole thing unbearable. She hated attention. She hated anything formal. She would have told me I was being ridiculous and then probably cried and hugged me. She was like that. She felt things fully and then she pretended she didn't.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She pauses.

[DONNA] I miss her every single day. I want people who listen to this to understand that. The legislation matters. The investigation matters. But the thing that never changes is that she's gone and nothing brings her back. Justice is real and it matters. It's just not the same thing as having your sister.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Meanwhile, in Massapequa Park, Roland Voss is living his life.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He gets up at five-thirty every morning. He takes the train to Manhattan. He sits at his desk in a mid-size architecture firm where he has worked for twenty-one years, managing projects with the kind of meticulous, patient attention to detail that his supervisors find invaluable and that the investigators who will eventually study his work patterns will recognize immediately as something else entirely.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He has a wife and a daughter. He coaches a youth sports team on weekends in the fall. He volunteers at the local fire department in a non-active capacity - clerical work, fundraising. His neighbors describe him as quiet, self-contained, occasionally awkward at the block parties but never unpleasant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is six foot four. He weighs two hundred and forty pounds. His hands are large and strong from years of work with models and drafting tools.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Nobody looks at him twice.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] I have spent considerable time over the past three years trying to understand how someone does that. How a person builds two lives simultaneously with such consistency that neither bleeds into the other. Psychiatrists I spoke to for this series describe it as extreme compartmentalization - a cognitive structure that treats different areas of life as literally separate realities, with no contamination between them. It is rare. It is not the same as not feeling guilt. It is, they suggest, more like experiencing guilt and simply not allowing it to operate in the compartment where it would be relevant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What that means, in practice, is that Roland Voss went home after the killings and was a father and a husband and a colleague. And was not, in any observable way, different.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His wife will later say, in court documents, that she had no idea. The investigation corroborates this. She is not a subject of the case. She is a victim of it in a different way - a person whose life was built on something rotten without her knowledge.

[MARSH] I have thought about her a great deal. The wife. I don't use her name because she's asked for privacy. But I think about what it means to share your life with someone, to sleep next to them for twenty-five years, and to have no idea who they actually are. I don't think she's lying when she says she didn't know. I think the answer is more terrible than that. I think he was very, very good at being two things at once.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In 2020, something happens that the task force investigators will later identify as a turning point - though they don't know it yet because the task force doesn't exist yet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A woman contacts the SSPD tip line. She gives her name but asks that it not be publicized. She says that she wants to tell investigators about something she saw approximately ten years earlier, near the highway access road, on a winter morning when she was walking her dog.

[REESE] She'd seen the anniversary coverage on the news. Tenth anniversary. And something she'd been carrying for a decade came to the surface. She'd seen a vehicle at one of the access points. Early morning. A man getting out of the vehicle. Large man. She remembered thinking it was strange because the park was closed and this person was clearly not a park employee - wrong clothing, wrong manner - and she'd made a mental note and then she'd walked past and gone home.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] The vehicle was a dark green pickup truck. Large. An older model. And she remembered, specifically, that the man had looked at her as she walked past. Not threateningly. Just looked. The way someone looks when they want to know if they've been seen.

[NARRATOR] The witness's account is filed. A patrol car is sent to take her statement. The vehicle description is entered into the system.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And then it sits. Because the Deadwater file, in 2020, has three people assigned to it, a pandemic has just upended the department's entire operational capacity, and the SSPD does not yet have a system for automatically cross-referencing new tips against vehicle registration databases.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] When the task force is constituted in February 2022 and the full case archive is reviewed, that witness statement - the dark green pickup truck, the large man, the look - is sitting in the file. It has been there for two years.

[REESE] When I read that statement in the task force review, I thought about entry nineteen in my thread book. The architecture firm near the shore. And I went home that night and I opened the thread book and I looked it up and I did a search.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Roland Voss and Associates. Commercial and residential architecture, established 1994. Massapequa Park, New York. Principal architect and owner: Roland T. Voss.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Registered vehicles at the business address: one dark green 2001 Chevrolet Avalanche pickup truck.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He calls Carol Marsh at ten-fifteen that night.

[REESE] She picked up on the first ring. I said: I think I have a name.

[MARSH] He gave me the name and I wrote it in my notebook and I looked at it for about thirty seconds and then I said: we need to build a case around this person. We need to be absolutely certain before we move.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I had been wrong before. I had had moments over twelve years where I was sure I had something and it turned out to be nothing. I was not going to rush this. Not with this much at stake. Not with this many families who needed it to be right.

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: The Surveillance. The task force begins watching Roland Voss - and discovers that hiding in plain sight is exactly what he's been counting on.
