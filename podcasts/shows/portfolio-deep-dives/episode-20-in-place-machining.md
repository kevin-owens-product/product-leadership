### The problem it solves

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer problem. Why does a plant need machining done on site?

**JAMES:** Because some equipment simply can't go to a shop. A turbine casing, a large pump housing, a heat exchanger, a paper machine roll, a ship's stern tube, a hydroelectric generator component. They're too big, too heavy, or built into the structure. Taking them out means cutting through walls or dismantling half the plant, which would take far longer than the repair itself.

**NORA:** So the alternative to field machining is what?

**JAMES:** Often, a much longer outage, or replacing equipment that could have been repaired. As general background, plants plan major maintenance outages, sometimes called turnarounds in refining or outages in power, and they pack a huge amount of work into a fixed window. When inspection during the outage reveals damage, the repair has to fit inside that window. Field machining makes that possible.

**NORA:** Who's the buyer?

**JAMES:** Mostly maintenance teams at plants, and the maintenance and service teams at equipment manufacturers, the companies that make turbines, pumps or paper machines and service them for their customers. Procurement is typically led by maintenance, with a lot of repeat customers. That's our inference from how the service works.

**NORA:** What do they care about?

**JAMES:** In my view, three things in this order. First, time to return to service. Can you finish inside the window? Second, getting it right. The work has to be in specification first time, because rework inside an outage is a disaster. Third, the credentials and experience of the crew. Price matters, but when an outage day can cost far more than the machining job itself, a cheap contractor who overruns is very expensive.

**NORA:** Why is rework such a disaster?

**JAMES:** Because inside an outage, everything is sequenced. The machining has to finish before the reassembly crew can start, before the inspection, before the restart. If a surface is machined out of specification and has to be redone, every trade waiting downstream waits too. One day's rework can push the whole outage back a day. That's why buyers value providers who measure carefully before and after, and why metrology and machining belong together.

**NORA:** And the equipment manufacturers? You said they're customers too.

**JAMES:** In general, yes. A turbine or pump manufacturer often services its installed base, and when a repair needs in-place machining, it may subcontract to a specialist rather than keep its own field machining crews everywhere. For a provider, being the trusted subcontractor to a manufacturer's service team can be a steady source of work. We don't know which manufacturers, if any, In-Place Machining works with, so that's general industry background.

**NORA:** And the measurement side?

**JAMES:** Metrology is increasingly the front end of these jobs. Before you machine anything, you need to know exactly what you're dealing with. How much material has to come off? Is the surface warped? Will the replacement part fit? A 3D scan gives you a precise digital model of the equipment as it actually is. That's valuable on its own, and it's also the starting point for designing and machining replacement parts.

**NORA:** What's driving demand?

**JAMES:** Our inference is three things. Ageing plant assets that can't be moved to a shop and are often cheaper to repair than replace. Pressure to keep outage windows short. And growing use of 3D scanning and measurement data to fit replacement parts accurately.

### How the work gets done

**NORA:** Let's go inside a job. What does field machining actually look like?

**JAMES:** In general terms, a crew arrives with portable machine tools. Some clamp onto a flange and rotate around it to resurface the face. Some bore out holes in place. Some are portable milling machines that run along a rail bolted to the equipment. Some cut and bevel pipe. The technician sets the tool up precisely on the equipment, which is the hard part, then makes careful cuts, measuring as they go.

**NORA:** And the setup is the skill.

**JAMES:** Very much. On a shop floor, you have a rigid machine bolted to the floor and the workpiece clamped to it. In the field, the workpiece is the machine's foundation. You might be working sideways, upside down, in a cramped space, in heat. Getting the tool aligned and rigid enough to cut to tight tolerances is a craft. That's why experienced field machinists are so valuable and so hard to replace.

**NORA:** What's the life of a field crew like?

**JAMES:** Demanding, in general. Crews travel to wherever the equipment is, often at short notice, and outage work tends to run long shifts, sometimes around the clock, to finish inside the window. Sites have their own safety rules, inductions and permits. Work can be in confined spaces, at height or in heat. Safety is paramount, and so is planning, because a crew that arrives with the wrong tool or a missing fixture loses hours it can't get back.

**NORA:** So logistics is part of the skill.

**JAMES:** A big part. The right equipment, the right fixtures and the right people, at the right site, on the right day. In a company with several regional bases, getting that right across the network is a real operational capability, and it's a place where better data and planning tools can help.

**NORA:** And metrology on site?

**JAMES:** Typically a laser tracker or a 3D scanner. A laser tracker measures specific points very accurately over large distances, so you can check alignment of, say, a line of bearings or the flatness of a large surface. A 3D scanner captures millions of points to build a digital model of a surface or a whole component. Both produce data files that engineers analyse, often in computer-aided design software.

**NORA:** And alignment, from OASIS?

**JAMES:** In a paper mill, for example, there are many large rolls that the paper web runs over at speed. If they're not aligned precisely, you get problems with quality and wear. Alignment services measure and correct that. OASIS's deal being reported in the paper trade press suggests that's where its expertise lay, and Western Machine Works also appears to have a paper roll repair background, judging by how its deal was framed.

**NORA:** What happens to the data after a job?

**JAMES:** That's the big question. In many field services businesses, scan files sit on an engineer's laptop or a project folder, job notes sit in a report, and setups and lessons learned sit in the technician's memory. We have no evidence of how In-Place Machining handles this. But it's where the AI opportunity starts.

### An outage, step by step

**NORA:** Let's do a walk-through. A hypothetical job, invented customer, invented people.

**JAMES:** Let's say a paper mill in the Pacific Northwest has a ten-day planned outage. During inspection, the maintenance manager, call her Linda, finds that the bearing housing on a large dryer roll is worn and the roll alignment along that section of the machine is off. She calls a field machining provider, a company like In-Place Machining.

**NORA:** What happens today?

**JAMES:** An estimator takes the call and asks for photos, drawings, dimensions, access details and the outage dates. Often there's a site visit to scope the job. The estimator then works out the crew, the equipment and the time required, partly from experience and partly from records of similar jobs, if they can find them. A quote goes back to Linda, maybe after a day or two. Meanwhile her outage clock is ticking.

**NORA:** Then the crew arrives.

**JAMES:** A metrology technician scans the bearing housing and measures the roll alignment. The data goes to an engineer, who works out how much material to remove and what the finished dimensions need to be. The field machinists set up a portable boring bar, machine the housing back to specification, and the metrology tech measures again. The alignment crew adjusts the roll positions. A report goes to Linda with before and after measurements.

**NORA:** And it fits in the window.

**JAMES:** Hopefully. But there are risks. If the scope was underestimated, the crew needs more time or more equipment. If the scan reveals something unexpected, the engineering takes longer. If the right specialist is on another job across the country, there's a wait.

**NORA:** Replay it with AI.

**JAMES:** Linda sends photos and basic dimensions through a request portal, or even just by email. An assistant structures the request and searches the company's history. Similar jobs, same type of equipment, similar dimensions, maybe even the same mill if they've worked there before. It pulls up the crew sizes, equipment, durations and any surprises on those jobs, and drafts a scope and a quote for the estimator to review. The estimator adjusts and sends it the same day.

**NORA:** So the estimator starts from evidence.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Then on site, the scan data flows straight into a standard workflow. Software compares the scan to the design dimensions and flags where the material needs to come off, and the engineer reviews it in an hour rather than half a day. Meanwhile the scheduling system has already checked which qualified machinists and which boring equipment are available for those dates.

**NORA:** And afterwards?

**JAMES:** The job record, the scans, the setup notes, the duration and any problems all go into a searchable history. So the next estimator quoting a similar job has better data. And Linda gets a digital record of her equipment's condition, which she can use when planning the next outage.

**NORA:** Does the machinist's job change?

**JAMES:** Not much on the tool itself. The cutting is still skilled hands-on work. What changes is that the machinist arrives with better information, and their setup notes become a resource for others rather than staying in their head. In my view, AI in this business is about everything around the cutting, not the cutting itself.

**NORA:** Let's do one more, from the inside. A day in the life of an estimator.

**JAMES:** Invented again. Call him Tom, an estimator at a regional base. Monday morning, he has nine open requests. Two are from regular customers with outages in three weeks. One is an emergency: a refinery found a damaged heat exchanger flange and needs a crew by Wednesday. The rest are budget enquiries for outages next spring.

**NORA:** Where does he start?

**JAMES:** With the emergency, obviously. He calls the customer, gets dimensions and photos, and tries to remember a similar job. He thinks one was done at another base two years ago, so he emails a colleague there. The colleague is on site and replies in the evening. Meanwhile Tom builds the quote from his own experience, checks which machinists and which flange facing machines are free, and sends it. He's not sure whether he priced in enough time for setup on that type of exchanger.

**NORA:** And the budget enquiries wait.

**JAMES:** They wait. And budget quotes for next year's outages are often where the bigger, more profitable work is won. So the urgent crowds out the important. That's not Tom's fault. It's the shape of the job when knowledge is scattered across bases and people.

**NORA:** With AI?

**JAMES:** Tom puts the emergency request into the system and instantly sees the job at the other base, with its scope, setup time, crew, duration and final cost, plus three other similar jobs. He quotes with more confidence in half the time. The system suggests a draft for each budget enquiry based on similar outage work, which he reviews and adjusts. By lunchtime, everything has gone out. The customers get faster answers, and the company wins more of the work it actually wants.

**NORA:** So a one-stop shop for outage work.

**JAMES:** That's the vision, as we read it. A plant can call one provider to measure, cut, machine and align, anywhere in the country.

**NORA:** And data?

**JAMES:** This is the interesting part. Through Exact Metrology and East Coast Metrology, the company holds metrology and scan capability, and presumably years of scan data from customer jobs. But we found no description of a data platform or any claim about using that data. So it's a potential asset whose current state we can't see.

**NORA:** And governance?

**JAMES:** The critical infrastructure positioning might imply working under regulated-sector requirements, for example at power stations, but we couldn't read the page to verify. We found no AI governance or information security certification.

**NORA:** So, overall?

**JAMES:** Early. Like most field services companies, and like Colt last episode, In-Place Machining doesn't present itself as a technology company. But it has more digital raw material than most, because of the metrology business. In my view, that's the starting point for its AI story.

**NORA:** Why is scan data so useful for AI specifically?

**JAMES:** Because it's precise, structured and comparable. A 3D scan of a flange or a bearing housing is a set of measured points, not a technician's description. If you have hundreds of scans of similar equipment, along with what was done and how long it took, you can start to learn patterns. How much material typically has to come off a worn surface of this type. How long the machining took for a given amount of wear. Which scans predicted problems that showed up later. Text notes are useful, but measurement data is where a field services company can build something genuinely hard to copy.

**NORA:** As long as it's kept, labelled and linked.

**JAMES:** That's the condition. An unlabelled scan file on an old laptop is just a file. A scan linked to the customer, the equipment, the job, the outcome and the cost is an asset.

**NORA:** What would you want to know first?

**JAMES:** Where the scan data lives, in what formats, and how much of it is linked to job records. How estimating is done today across the group, and whether there's a common job history. And how scheduling works across the regional bases. Those answers tell you which AI bet is realistic.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's name the challenges. What's hard, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, the labour model. Every job needs skilled crews on site, travelling to wherever the equipment is. You can't scale that like software. Growth means hiring and developing more field machinists and metrology technicians, who are in short supply.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Integration debt, which we've discussed. Several acquisitions with different systems, brands and practices.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Cyclicality. Demand is tied to heavy-industry capital spending and outage cycles. A downturn in oil and gas, or in power maintenance spending, or a weak paper market, hits volume. Diversification across segments helps, but it doesn't remove the risk.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**JAMES:** Competition from bundlers. Team and Enerpac through Hydratight can offer machining as part of broader turnaround packages, which can pressure price. When a refinery hands one big contractor its entire outage scope, the specialist may become a subcontractor, or be left out.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Visibility and momentum. After a run of acquisitions to early 2023, there's no public news. That makes it hard for outsiders to judge momentum, and in my view it's also a missed opportunity. Recruiting skilled people, winning new customers and eventually telling the story to a buyer all benefit from a visible, confident company.

**NORA:** And the AI-specific challenge?

**JAMES:** Turning scattered data into something useful. Scan files, job reports, quotes and schedules from multiple acquired businesses, in different formats, possibly on individual laptops. The AI work starts with gathering and structuring that, which isn't exciting but is essential. And then convincing experienced estimators and machinists that the tools help them rather than second-guess them.

**NORA:** And the people pipeline? You mentioned a labour shortage.

**JAMES:** It's probably the biggest long-term constraint. Field machining combines machinist skills with the ability to work in difficult field conditions and solve problems on the spot. That takes years to develop. As experienced people retire, their knowledge goes with them unless it's captured. In my view, a company that can train new field machinists faster, through mentoring backed by a good knowledge base, has a real advantage in recruiting and in capacity.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's talk opportunities. Where would you push first?

**JAMES:** The first is quote and job-scope automation, using scan data and past job records to draft quotes and scope jobs faster. We walked through it with Linda. The value lever is estimator and engineer hours, and then win rate. In outage work, the provider who sends a credible, well-scoped quote first often wins the job.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** A scan-to-part workflow. Link the metrology data to the computer-aided machining preparation, so a scan flows directly into the plan for what to machine or what replacement part to make. That cuts engineering rework and reduces the risk of a part that doesn't fit. The lever is margin per job.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** A technician knowledge base. Capture field crew know-how on setups, tolerances and tricky equipment, so new hires get productive faster. Imagine a machinist about to set up on an unfamiliar turbine casing being able to pull up how a senior colleague did it two years ago, with photos and notes. The lever is time to productivity, which in a labour shortage is worth a lot.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Cross-selling across the stack. Bundle metrology, alignment, machining and wire cutting into outage packages for existing customers. A plant that already trusts the alignment team is a warm lead for machining. The lever is revenue per customer.

**NORA:** And beyond those?

**JAMES:** Pricing discipline. Use job history to price by outage window and urgency rather than standard rates. A job that has to be done in a three-day window over a holiday is worth more than one with flexible timing. Geographic expansion, using the Baton Rouge and Western bases to pursue Gulf Coast and Pacific Northwest accounts. And more tuck-in acquisitions in metrology and alignment, which would also build a bigger data asset.

**NORA:** Customers won't love paying more for urgency.

**JAMES:** Maybe not, but they understand it, because they know what a lost outage day costs them. In my view, the key is transparency. A clear rate structure for planned work booked early, and a premium for short-notice or compressed-window work, with the reasons explained. It also nudges customers to book earlier, which helps the company plan crews and equipment. Job history tells you which jobs actually cost more to deliver, so the premiums can be grounded in fact rather than guesswork.

**NORA:** Is there a data product here? Selling the scans themselves?

**JAMES:** Possibly, in my view. A digital record of a customer's critical equipment, scanned at every outage, would let the plant track wear over time and plan replacements. That could become a recurring service: we scan your critical assets every outage and give you a condition report and a forecast. It's speculative, but it's the kind of move that turns a project business into a relationship business.

**NORA:** And scheduling?

**JAMES:** Yes. Outage season can be crowded, with many plants scheduling work at similar times of year. Matching qualified crews and specialised equipment to overlapping jobs across regions is a hard optimisation problem, and good scheduling tools could raise utilisation and reduce travel. I'd put it third or fourth, after quoting and the knowledge base.

### The best AI bet

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months, what is it?

**JAMES:** Quote and job-scope automation from scan data and job history. The company already holds scan data from its metrology businesses and runs many similar jobs, which gives it a base to learn from. It saves skilled time, it speeds response, it improves win rate, and the estimator stays in charge of the final number.

**NORA:** Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: pick one segment and one region, say power generation jobs out of the main Midwest base. Gather the last few years of quotes, final job costs, durations and scan files for that segment into one place. That alone is probably a project. Sit down with the estimators: how do they quote today, what do they look at, where do they get it wrong. And agree the measures: time to quote, quote accuracy against actual job cost, and win rate.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Build a simple tool that, for a new request, finds the most similar past jobs and shows the estimator their scope, crew, equipment, duration and final cost, with a suggested range. Nothing fancy. Run it alongside the normal process. The estimator quotes as usual, then looks at the tool's suggestion, and you record both.

**NORA:** So shadow mode, again.

**JAMES:** It's the right pattern whenever expert judgement is involved. Days sixty to a hundred: compare the estimator's quotes and the tool's suggestions against how jobs actually turned out. Where the tool was closer, show the team. Where it was wrong, find out why. If it's helping, roll it to the next segment and the acquired businesses, and make recording final job data a standard step at job close.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** The most respected estimator or operations leader, paired with someone who can handle the data. The estimators need to feel this is their tool. If it's seen as head office checking their work, it'll fail.

**NORA:** Any governance points?

**JAMES:** Three. Customer scan data belongs to, or is at least shared with, the customer, so agree how it can be used, especially for critical infrastructure clients who may have strict rules. Keep the estimator accountable for every quote. And make sure the data on which the tool learns is clean and consistent, or it'll learn the wrong lessons.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** The technician knowledge base. It addresses the labour shortage directly and protects the company against retirements. And it's relatively low risk, because it's guidance for skilled people, not automation of their work.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from In-Place Machining?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, acquisitions buy data, not just revenue. When In-Place Machining bought metrology businesses, it bought scan capability and, presumably, a history of scans. Many companies don't realise what data they acquired, or let it sit in the acquired firm's folders. Inventory it.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, quoting is a great first AI target in project businesses. Every project business quotes. Quotes are slow, they depend on a few experienced people, and accuracy matters a lot for margin. If you have records of past jobs and their final costs, you can help your estimators start from evidence.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, integration has to be finished to pay off. A buy-and-build creates a lot of value on paper. The value only becomes real when customers experience one company, the data flows across the businesses, and cross-selling actually happens. If you can't show that, a buyer will value you as a collection of shops.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, quiet isn't always golden. In a mature hold, telling your story publicly, new capabilities, new customers, how you use technology, helps with recruitment, with customers and with the eventual sale. A company with no public news for over three years makes outsiders guess, and guesses are rarely generous.

**NORA:** I'd add one: the craft stays human.

**JAMES:** Agreed. In businesses built on hands-on skill, AI's job is to take the friction out of everything around the craft, quoting, planning, paperwork, knowledge sharing, so the craftspeople can spend more time doing what only they can do.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-native machining tools lowering barriers. AI-assisted programming and planning tools could help competing shops offer faster, more sophisticated work, though not the field work itself. Two, bundling by large industrial services groups pressuring price. Three, cyclicality in heavy-industry capital spending and outage budgets.

**NORA:** Four?

**JAMES:** Four, remote inspection and digital twins. A digital twin is a detailed digital model of a physical asset that's kept up to date with data. As drones, fixed sensors and remote scanning improve, some measurement work that requires a technician on site today might be done remotely. That's our inference, and it cuts both ways: it's a threat to some on-site metrology hours, and an opportunity if In-Place Machining builds those twins itself from its scan history.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** After five years, the question of exit becomes more pressing. The story a buyer will want to hear is an integrated platform with clear cross-selling, good margins and a credible technology plan. AI quoting and a scan data asset would strengthen that story. A collection of loosely connected businesses with no recent news would weaken it.

**NORA:** Are there AI-specific risks inside the business?

**JAMES:** Two. First, a quoting tool that learns from bad data. If past job records are incomplete, for example if overruns weren't recorded properly, the tool will systematically underestimate, and the company will win work it loses money on. Second, over-reliance. If estimators stop thinking critically because the tool usually gets close, the unusual job, the one that's different from anything in the history, gets mispriced. The defence is good data at job close, and a culture where the estimator owns the number.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, any new public news at all: an acquisition, a leadership announcement, a new capability. That would end the long quiet period. Second, any sign of a digital platform, a customer portal for scan data or condition reports, or a technology leadership hire. Third, any sign of a sale process, given the length of the hold.
