### The customer: districts and small towns

**NORA:** Let's talk about who actually hires USA Water. You keep saying utility districts. What are those?

**JAMES:** This is a bit of general background, and I'll keep it careful. In Texas, and especially around Houston, a lot of suburban growth has been financed through special districts. A developer builds a new subdivision, and a local district is created to build and run the water, sewer and drainage infrastructure, funded by bonds and paid back through local taxes and fees. Those districts are often run by small elected boards of residents.

**NORA:** So a board of five neighbours is suddenly responsible for a water plant.

**JAMES:** More or less. And they don't have staff. They're not going to hire their own licensed operators, lab technicians, maintenance crews and billing clerks. So they hire professionals. Typically a lawyer, an engineer, a bookkeeper, and an operator. The operator is the company that actually runs the system day to day. That's the role Si Environmental built its business on.

**NORA:** And outside Texas?

**JAMES:** The same logic applies to small and mid-sized municipalities across the country. Industry research from Bluefield, which tracks this market, lists the reasons utilities outsource: aging infrastructure, regulatory demands, financial constraints, workforce shortages and rising costs. If you're a small town and your one licensed operator retires, you have a real problem. A contract operator can step in tomorrow.

**NORA:** What does the customer actually care about? If I'm on one of these boards, what makes me happy?

**JAMES:** In my view, four things, in roughly this order. First, no violations. A boil-water notice or a sewage overflow is a nightmare for a small board. Second, reliability: the water's on, the pumps work, problems are fixed quickly. Third, cost predictability, because the board answers to residents who pay the bills. And fourth, communication. Board members want a clear monthly report that tells them what happened and what's coming.

**NORA:** Not innovation, then.

**JAMES:** Not as a headline, no. Nobody on a district board wakes up wanting AI. They want fewer surprises. If AI shows up for them, it will show up as fewer violations, faster fixes, better reports and steadier costs. That's an important framing for everything we'll say about technology today.

**NORA:** And how sticky are these customers?

**JAMES:** We didn't find data on churn or contract length, so this is our inference. Contract operations is the kind of service that's hard to switch. A new operator has to learn the system, the quirks of each plant, the history of each problem. Boards tend not to change operators over small price differences, because the risk of a bad transition is high. That's a strength. But it also means when you do lose a client, it's usually because something went wrong on service.

### What contract operations actually involves

**NORA:** Let's go inside the work. What does running a water system involve?

**JAMES:** On the drinking water side, you're producing water, usually from wells or a surface source, treating it, typically disinfecting it, storing it in tanks and pushing it through the network at the right pressure. Operators check chemical levels, take samples for testing, keep records, and respond to alarms. On the wastewater side, you're collecting sewage through pipes and lift stations, treating it at a plant, and discharging clean effluent under a permit with limits.

**NORA:** And all of that is regulated.

**JAMES:** Heavily. Federal law sets the framework, the Safe Drinking Water Act for drinking water and the Clean Water Act for discharges, and states run most of the day-to-day oversight. In Texas, that's the state environmental regulator. Operators generally need state licences at different grades depending on the system. And there's a constant stream of sampling, monitoring and reporting obligations. Miss a sample or a report, and you can have a violation even if the water was fine.

**NORA:** So paperwork is part of the product.

**JAMES:** Paperwork is a big part of the product. Levine Leichtman's description of USA Water explicitly includes regulatory compliance as a core service. When a board hires an operator, they're buying the confidence that every required sample was taken, every report was filed and every record could survive an inspection.

**NORA:** What about maintenance?

**JAMES:** That's the other big chunk. Pumps, motors, valves, blowers at the wastewater plant, chemical feed systems, generators, meters. Things wear out. Some maintenance is scheduled, some is emergency. A lift station pump fails at two in the morning, and someone has to go out before the wet well overflows. Emergency work is expensive, overtime-heavy and stressful. Reducing it is one of the clearest ways to improve a contract's economics.

**NORA:** And billing?

**JAMES:** Si Environmental already provided customer billing at the time of the 2020 deal, and today there's a vice president for billing and technical services. For a utility district, that means reading meters, producing bills, collecting payments and handling customer calls. It's a high-volume, rules-heavy back-office service, and that matters for AI, because back-office text and numbers are where today's AI tools are strongest.

**NORA:** What technology does an operator like this typically use?

**JAMES:** In general terms, the systems often have SCADA, which stands for supervisory control and data acquisition. It's the industrial control and monitoring layer: sensors, alarms, remote readings of levels, flows and pressures. Some have geographic information systems mapping their pipes, and asset management software. But here's a caution. We did not find any public evidence of which systems USA Water uses or how its data is organised. And in a business built by acquisition, serving many separate clients, it's very likely the technology varies a lot from site to site.

**NORA:** And the client owns the SCADA, not the operator.

**JAMES:** Often, yes. Which matters for data rights. If USA Water wants to build predictive models on SCADA data, it has to work within whatever its clients allow.

**NORA:** One more on the work itself. How is an operator business actually staffed?

**JAMES:** In general terms, and this is industry background rather than anything USA Water has published, you have licensed operators running routes, often with a mix of licence grades because bigger or more complex plants need more senior people. Behind them sit maintenance technicians, electricians and mechanics who handle repairs. Then supervisors who plan the work, a compliance function that tracks every sampling and reporting deadline, and back-office teams for billing and accounts. And there's an on-call rota, because water systems don't keep office hours.

**NORA:** So the cost base is basically people and vehicles.

**JAMES:** People, vehicles, parts, chemicals in some contracts, and lab fees. Mostly people. Which is why everything we'll say about AI comes back to how many systems each skilled person can look after safely.

### A day in the life

**NORA:** Let's make this concrete. Give me a hypothetical day. An invented operator, not a real person.

**JAMES:** Let's call him Marcus. He's a licensed operator for a contract firm like USA Water, and he covers a route of, say, six utility districts in the suburbs west of a big city. Each district has a small water plant, a wastewater plant or a connection to a regional one, and a few lift stations.

**NORA:** What does his morning look like?

**JAMES:** He starts at the first water plant around six. He checks the chlorine residual, looks at the tank levels, listens to the pumps, glances at the chemical feed. He writes the readings on a log sheet or enters them in a tablet. Then he drives to the next site and does it again. By mid-morning he's taken the samples due today, which go to a lab. One lift station had a high-level alarm overnight, so he checks the floats and the pump run times and finds one pump is drawing more current than it should.

**NORA:** So that's a maintenance ticket.

**JAMES:** It is. He calls or messages the maintenance supervisor, who schedules a crew. If that pump fails completely before the crew gets there, it becomes a night call-out. Meanwhile, a resident calls the district's billing line complaining about a high bill, and the billing team needs to know whether a meter was misread or there's a leak.

**NORA:** And the afternoon?

**JAMES:** More rounds, plus paperwork. Monthly operating reports for the state. A summary for the district board meeting next week. Someone has to pull together the readings, the work orders, the sample results, any exceptions, and turn them into a report the board can understand. In many operations, that's still done by hand from logs, spreadsheets and emails.

**NORA:** So where's the waste in Marcus's day?

**JAMES:** In my view, three places. Driving and routine checking at sites where nothing has changed. Re-typing readings and assembling reports. And the reactive call-out that could have been a planned repair if someone had spotted the trend in that pump a week earlier. None of those are Marcus's fault. They're the shape of the work.

**NORA:** And the thing he's actually expert at?

**JAMES:** Judgement. Knowing that this plant always smells a bit odd after heavy rain but that one doesn't. Knowing which board member wants a phone call before anything goes in the report. That knowledge lives in Marcus's head, and in a workforce that's getting older, that's a real risk for the business.

### The market and the competitors

**NORA:** Let's widen out. How big is this market?

**JAMES:** The best figure we found comes from Bluefield Research, which describes third-party operations and maintenance for US drinking water and wastewater utilities as valued at nearly three billion dollars a year. Their page is dated late 2024, and it doesn't say which year the valuation refers to. So treat it as an order of magnitude: a market in the low billions, not the tens of billions.

**NORA:** And growth?

**JAMES:** We didn't find a reliable growth rate, so I won't give one. What we can say is that the drivers Bluefield lists, aging infrastructure, regulation, money pressure and workforce shortages, are all long-term and all point toward more outsourcing, not less. That's our view, not a forecast.

**NORA:** Who are the competitors?

**JAMES:** There's a mix. At the large end there's Veolia North America, part of the big French environmental services group, which runs contract operations among many other things. Jacobs, the large engineering firm, has an operations and maintenance business. Inframark operates municipal water and wastewater systems. Woodard and Curran is an engineering and operations firm. Alliance Water Resources is another operations peer. And then there's U.S. Water Services Corporation, an employee-owned firm based in Florida.

**NORA:** Tell me about that one, since it sounds like the closest match.

**JAMES:** It's the closest scale peer we found. Founded in 2003, employee-owned, and in 2022 it bought the operations and maintenance business of the engineering firm Ramboll in the Americas. After that deal it described itself as about seven hundred professionals across eighteen states. That gives you a sense of what a scaled contract operator looks like. And it's a Florida firm, which matters if USA Water is pushing into Florida too.

**NORA:** And American Water?

**JAMES:** American Water is a different model, but it competes for the same small systems. It's a large regulated utility, and rather than operating systems under contract, it buys them outright. In June 2026, it announced it had completed a purchase of Nexus Water Group systems in eight states, for about three hundred and fifteen million dollars. When a big utility buys a small system, that system no longer needs a contract operator.

**NORA:** So for USA Water, a district selling itself to American Water is a lost customer.

**JAMES:** Potentially, yes. That's the consolidation threat. A small system has a few choices. Run it yourself, hire an operator, or sell to a big utility. As the big utilities get more active, the pool of systems that want an operator could shrink in some places.

**NORA:** What's USA Water's edge against the big names?

**JAMES:** In my view, local density and relationships. In contract operations, being close matters. If you have dozens of districts in one metro area, your operators' routes are short, your maintenance crews can reach a failure fast, and you can share specialists. A national giant might have scale overall but less density in a specific suburb. And Si Environmental's leadership says its team has two hundred and thirty-eight years of combined experience, by its own count. That's a company that knows its districts.

**NORA:** Let's talk about the acquisition strategy, because I think it's central to the investment.

**JAMES:** It is. The contract operations market is fragmented. There are many small and regional operators, often founder-owned, often run by people who came up as operators themselves. A platform like USA Water can buy those firms, keep their client relationships and local crews, and give them better back-office support, procurement, safety programmes and technology.

**NORA:** What does each acquisition actually give them?

**JAMES:** Contracts, mostly. Each add-on brings a portfolio of districts or municipalities, the operators who know them, and sometimes a new geography. If the PitchBook list is right, Florida Utility Solutions and Southeastern Environmental Services sound like moves into Florida and the Southeast, though we couldn't confirm what those businesses do.

**NORA:** And what's hard about it?

**JAMES:** Integration, as always. Every small operator has its own way of logging rounds, its own spreadsheets, its own billing software, its own maintenance tracking, if it has any. Its own culture. When you buy five of them in eighteen months, you inherit five ways of doing everything. The benefit of the platform only shows up when you bring some of that together.

**NORA:** Bring together what, specifically?

**JAMES:** I'd prioritise three things. A common way of recording operator rounds and readings. A common maintenance and work-order system. And a common billing platform where the company does billing. Those are the systems that touch every contract, every day. They're also, not by coincidence, the foundations any AI would need.

**NORA:** So integration and AI are really the same project.

**JAMES:** In a business like this, I think so. You can't run AI on data that lives in twelve different spreadsheets formatted twelve different ways. The first AI job at a buy-and-build platform is usually joining up data across the acquired businesses. That's not glamorous, but it's where the value starts.

**NORA:** What about pricing on acquisitions?

**JAMES:** There's a subtler lever. When a platform buys a small operator, it often finds contracts priced below market, or without proper cost escalation, because the founder never wanted to rock the boat with the board. Over time, the platform can bring those contracts to a fair price at renewal, tied to its costs. Done badly, that annoys clients. Done well, with better service and better reporting to justify it, it's a meaningful source of margin. That's our view of how these platforms typically work.

### Where USA Water is with AI

**NORA:** So, the AI question. Where is USA Water today?

**JAMES:** Honestly, we found no company-specific evidence of AI at all. The 2024 announcement from Levine Leichtman talks about acquisitions and geographic expansion, not technology platforms or AI. The trade coverage didn't mention technology. The leadership page lists no AI or data roles. And as we said, we couldn't review the holding company's own site.

**NORA:** No evidence isn't the same as nothing happening.

**JAMES:** That's right, and I want to be fair. Plenty of companies in this sector use software every day without talking about it. They may well have digital logging, work-order systems or remote monitoring at some sites. We just can't see it. What we can say is that AI isn't part of how the company presents itself publicly.

**NORA:** Is that unusual for the water sector?

**JAMES:** Not really. Water utilities are conservative, for good reasons. You don't experiment with the system that keeps people from getting sick. That said, there's movement. There's an American Society of Civil Engineers journal paper surveying AI adoption in US drinking water operations, which tells you the profession is studying it seriously. We only saw the title, not the findings, so I won't characterise them. And the trade press has started using the phrase augmented operator, the idea that AI's role is to help a shrinking pool of licensed operators do more, not to replace them.

**NORA:** I like that framing.

**JAMES:** So do I, and I think it fits USA Water well. The company's product is skilled people. The scarce resource in the industry is skilled people. AI that makes each operator more effective attacks the constraint directly.

**NORA:** What about the cybersecurity side? I know that's been in the news.

**JAMES:** It has. In July 2026, there were reports of a sweeping cyberattack on water systems in multiple US states, and federal officials were reported to be on edge about it. Separately, a Senate committee advanced the 2026 Water Resources Development Act, which is the regular federal water infrastructure bill, with provisions expanding cybersecurity support for water systems. We only saw headlines on both, so I'll keep it at that. But the direction is clear. Water systems are a target, and anyone operating them, including contract operators, is under pressure to take cyber seriously.

**NORA:** And how does that interact with AI?

**JAMES:** It cuts both ways. Connecting more systems and pulling more data into the cloud to run models can increase the attack surface if it's done carelessly. But AI is also used for spotting unusual activity on networks. For a contract operator, I'd say the order matters: get the security basics right first, then add the clever stuff.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's name the challenges. What's hard for USA Water, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, the workforce. The whole industry is short of licensed operators, and USA Water's growth depends on hiring, training and keeping them. Every new contract needs people. Every retirement takes knowledge out of the business.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Integration load. Two sponsor changes in about four years, plus a series of add-ons, means a lot of change for a mid-sized company. The leadership team has to run the existing contracts, absorb acquisitions and, presumably, prepare for the next owner one day. That's a lot of plates spinning.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Fragmented systems and data, which we've talked about. Many clients, many plants, many different control systems owned by different districts, and acquired businesses with their own tools. It's hard to get one clear picture.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**JAMES:** Regional concentration. Texas and the Southeast are good places to be, with population growth and lots of new districts. But concentration means exposure to regional risks, including the weather. Hurricanes, floods and freezes all hit water systems hard, and a contract operator is the one on the front line when they do.

**NORA:** Say more about that. Why does weather matter so much?

**JAMES:** As general background, Texas has had some severe reminders. The winter storm in February 2021 caused widespread power and water problems across the state, with many systems issuing boil-water notices. The Gulf Coast also gets hurricanes and flooding. When power fails, lift stations stop unless generators kick in. When pipes freeze, they burst. When floods hit, treatment plants can be overwhelmed.

**NORA:** And the operator gets the call.

**JAMES:** The operator gets every call. That's where a contract operator earns its reputation, or loses it. Having enough crews, generators, spare pumps and a clear emergency plan across dozens of districts at once is a real operational capability. In my view, it's also a place where better data helps: knowing which lift stations have backup power, which tanks are low, which sites flood first. That's not fancy AI, it's good situational awareness, and it's the foundation for anything more sophisticated later.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Visibility, which is partly our problem as researchers but also, I'd argue, a business issue. The public picture of USA Water is inconsistent. Two headquarters, an outdated chief executive listing on one aggregator, conflicting add-on counts. That doesn't affect a district board, which knows its operator personally. But when you want to recruit operators, win municipal contracts outside your home market, or one day tell your story to a buyer, a clear and consistent public presence helps. In my view, that inconsistency is also a small hint that data hygiene inside the business may be uneven, though that's an inference.

**NORA:** And the AI-specific challenge?

**JAMES:** The revenue model. Contract operations are usually priced around the labour and costs needed to run the system. If AI genuinely cuts operator hours per contract, clients will eventually ask why the fee hasn't come down. So USA Water has to think about whether savings go to margin, to the client, or into better service. That's a commercial question as much as a technical one.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's move to opportunities. If you were advising USA Water, where would you look first?

**JAMES:** The first and biggest is automating operator rounds and compliance reporting. Every contract involves recurring rounds, readings, samples and reports. That's the largest recurring labour cost in the business, and the work repeats across every district. A digital round with an AI assistant could log readings by voice or photo, flag anything out of range against that site's history, and draft the monthly operating report and the board summary automatically for a human to check.

**NORA:** What's the value?

**JAMES:** Labour hours per contract, plus fewer compliance slips. We didn't find any public figure for either at USA Water, so I won't invent one. But if an operator spends a meaningful part of each week on paperwork, giving even a slice of that back means more districts per operator. In a labour-constrained market, that's growth capacity, not just cost saving.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Predictive maintenance on the clients' SCADA and asset data. Pump current, run times, vibration where it's measured, flows and levels. A model can spot that a pump is drifting from its normal pattern days before it fails. That turns a two a.m. emergency into a planned Tuesday repair. The lever is margin on each contract through less overtime and fewer emergency parts orders. And it's a better story for the board: fewer overflows, fewer outages.

**NORA:** But you said the data may not be there.

**JAMES:** That's the catch. We couldn't confirm what SCADA, mapping or asset management systems are in use across USA Water's clients. So predictive maintenance is probably a second-wave project, after the data foundations are in place. I'd start it at the few sites with the best instrumentation and prove it there.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Billing and customer service. USA Water bills customers for at least some districts. AI is well suited to billing exceptions: flagging a reading that looks wrong, spotting a probable leak from a usage spike, drafting a clear explanation for the resident, and handling routine calls like payment dates or service starts. Lower handling cost, fewer billing errors, happier residents and happier boards.

**NORA:** What else?

**JAMES:** Fourth, pricing and contract discipline. Tie renewals to cost indices and use service data to show the board what they're getting. If you can show a district that you hit every sample, responded to every alarm within a set time and cut emergency call-outs, you have a strong case for a fair price. Fifth, continued acquisitions into new geographies, which is the stated plan. And sixth, industrial operations and maintenance as an adjacency. The U.S. Water Services deal with Ramboll shows there's a sizeable industrial market next door, though we have no evidence USA Water is pursuing it.

**NORA:** Is there a knowledge opportunity, given what you said about Marcus?

**JAMES:** Yes, and I'd add it to the list. Capturing operator know-how. Site notes, past problems, the fix that worked last time, all searchable by the next operator on that route. An assistant that can answer, what happened the last time this lift station alarmed after heavy rain? That protects the business against retirements and speeds up training new operators.

**NORA:** Let's go back to the pricing worry for a moment, because I think it's the trickiest part. If AI makes the work cheaper, how should USA Water charge for it?

**JAMES:** There are a few models, and this is our view rather than anything the company has said. One is to keep the fee and raise the service level: same price, but faster response times, better reporting, fewer violations. Another is to share a defined slice of savings with the client, which builds trust. A third is to move part of the fee toward outcomes, for example a bonus for zero compliance violations or for keeping unplanned outages under a threshold.

**NORA:** Would a small district board actually want an outcome-based contract?

**JAMES:** Some would, some wouldn't. Boards like predictability, so a big variable component could worry them. I'd start with a small performance element on top of a stable base fee. The real point is that the operator needs to measure outcomes well before it can price on them. Which, again, comes back to clean, consistent data from every site.

### The best AI bet

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months, what is it?

**JAMES:** Operator rounds and compliance reporting. It's the biggest recurring cost, it repeats across every contract, the risk is manageable because a licensed human still reviews and signs off, and it doesn't depend on getting access to every client's control system. You can start with a tablet and a well-designed form.

**NORA:** Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: pick one region, ideally a cluster of districts around Houston where the operating practices are most mature. Map exactly how rounds are logged today, what reports are produced for whom, and how long each takes. Agree three measures: operator hours on paperwork per district, report errors or late filings, and operator satisfaction, because if the operators hate it, it won't stick.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Roll out a simple digital round on tablets in that cluster, if it isn't already there, with consistent fields across every site. Then add an assistant that drafts the monthly operating report and the board summary from those readings and the work orders. Run it in parallel: the operator or supervisor still produces their usual report, and you compare the two.

**NORA:** Shadow mode again.

**JAMES:** It's essential in a regulated service. You never want an AI-drafted report going to a regulator that nobody checked. Days sixty to a hundred: switch the pilot cluster to the AI-drafted reports with human review and sign-off, measure the time saved and the error rate, and decide on rollout to the next region, including the acquired businesses. At day one hundred, you should have real numbers instead of a hypothesis.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** One named leader with operations credibility, not just technology credibility. The operators need to see that it was built by people who understand the work. I'd pair that person with whoever owns billing and technical services today, because that team already sits at the junction of operations and systems.

**NORA:** And what about the governance side?

**JAMES:** Three rules from the start. A licensed human signs every compliance submission. Client data stays within the terms each district has agreed. And anything that touches control systems gets a security review before it goes anywhere near production. Write those down, share them with clients, and you've turned a risk into a selling point.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** Predictive maintenance at the best-instrumented sites. It has a bigger potential payoff per site, but it needs better data, so it comes second.

### A second walk-through: the board meeting

**JAMES:** Picture a district of a few thousand homes on the edge of a growing metro. The board meets monthly in the evening. Five residents, a lawyer, the district engineer, and the operator's account manager, who we'll call Dana.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses, especially services companies owned by private equity, learn from USA Water?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, in a services business, AI is a capacity tool before it's a cost tool. When your growth is limited by how many skilled people you can hire, giving each person back a few hours a week is effectively hiring. Frame it that way internally and your people will see it as help, not as a threat.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, integration is the AI strategy in a buy-and-build. Before you can do anything clever, you need the same data recorded the same way across every acquired business. The platform that standardises its rounds, work orders and billing first will be the one that can actually use AI later.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, think about your pricing before your customers do. If AI makes your work cheaper to deliver, a smart client will eventually notice. Decide in advance how you'll share the value: maybe better service levels at the same price, maybe outcome-based fees tied to compliance and reliability. Don't wait to be asked.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, tell your story. In a quiet industry, a company that clearly explains how it uses technology to deliver safer, more reliable service will stand out, to clients, to recruits and, eventually, to buyers. Right now USA Water's public story is thin and inconsistent. That's easy to fix and, in my view, worth fixing.

**NORA:** I'd add one from what you said about Marcus. Capture the knowledge before it walks out the door.

**JAMES:** Agreed. Any business with an ageing skilled workforce should be asking, where does our expertise actually live, and what happens when those people retire? AI-searchable site notes are one of the cheapest insurance policies you can buy.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, consolidation by larger players. American Water's Nexus purchase shows big utilities buying small systems, which can take potential clients out of the operator market and push prices. Two, AI reshaping the revenue model. If AI cuts hours and clients push to share the savings, fee-based contracts could shrink unless USA Water moves toward pricing on outcomes. That's our inference, but it follows from the logic.

**NORA:** Three?

**JAMES:** Three, AI-enabled challengers. A software-first operator, with heavy automation and remote monitoring, could bid lower on labour-heavy contracts. We haven't seen that happen at scale in this market, but it's a plausible threat. Four, municipal in-sourcing. Some cities may bring work back in-house if they can hire operators, though the labour shortage makes that hard. And five, cyber and regulatory risk. A breach or a serious compliance failure at a client could cost contracts and create liability.

**NORA:** Is there an AI-specific risk inside the operation itself?

**JAMES:** Yes. If an assistant drafts compliance reports, it could get something wrong, a misread value, a missing sample, a sentence that sounds right and isn't. In a regulated service, that's serious. The defence is design: the AI drafts from structured readings rather than inventing text, every number traces back to a logged reading, and a licensed person reviews and signs. Over-reliance is the quieter risk. If operators stop looking closely because the system usually gets it right, mistakes slip through. Training and spot audits matter.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** Execution on the buy-and-build. Levine Leichtman bought the business in early 2024, so we're nearly three years into the hold. The value story at exit will rest on how many contracts it has, how well it integrated them, and whether it has a credible operating platform. AI that has demonstrably increased operator capacity would strengthen that story a lot.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, confirmation of the add-ons, ideally a press release that names the acquired businesses and the new regions. Second, any announcement of a common operating platform, digital rounds, work-order systems or remote monitoring across the group. And third, any public statement on cybersecurity, given the attacks on water systems this year. If USA Water publishes something on how it protects client systems, that's a sign of maturity.
