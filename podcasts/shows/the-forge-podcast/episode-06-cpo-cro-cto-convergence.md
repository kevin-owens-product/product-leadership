# Episode 6: The Converged Executive
## "Merging CPO, CRO, and CTO into One AI-Native Operating System"

**Duration:** ~47 minutes
**Hosts:** Alex (Interviewer) & Kevin (Expert)

---

### INTRO (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** Welcome back to The Forge Podcast, where we explore the intersection of private equity, software transformation, and the AI-native future. I'm Alex, and as always, I'm joined by Kevin. Today we're digging into a topic that I think is going to make a lot of executives uncomfortable, which probably means it's exactly the right conversation to have. We're talking about the convergence of the CPO, the CRO, and the CTO into a single operating model. Kevin, before we get into the mechanics, set the scene for us. Why is this coming up now, and why does it matter?

**KEVIN:** It matters because the organizational architecture that most software companies run on was designed for a world that no longer exists. In the classic SaaS model, you had three distinct systems. Product defined what to build based on market research and customer feedback. Engineering figured out how to build it and kept the lights on. Revenue, meaning sales, marketing, and customer success, figured out how to package, sell, and expand it. Those three systems communicated through interfaces: roadmap reviews, sprint demos, quarterly business reviews, pricing committees. And that worked, because the product was a relatively static artifact. You shipped a release, customers used it, sales sold it, and the cycle repeated on a quarterly or monthly cadence. The latency between those domains was tolerable because the product itself didn't change between releases.

**ALEX:** And in an AI-native company, that latency becomes a problem.

**KEVIN:** It becomes more than a problem. It becomes structurally incompatible with how the product actually works. In an AI-native product, the thing customers experience is continuously changing. Product behavior is tuned through prompts, policies, model routing, workflow automation, guardrails, and feedback loops. The product is not a shipped artifact anymore. It is a living system that learns, adapts, and evolves in near-real-time. And that means the thing customers buy, the thing engineering operates, and the thing product designs are no longer three separate objects connected by interfaces. They are one unified system. So the question becomes: can you run a unified system with a fragmented leadership model? And the answer, increasingly, is no.

### SEGMENT 1: The Structural Forces Behind Convergence (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's get specific about why this convergence is happening. You said it's structural, not just a trendy reorg. Walk us through the forces that are actually pushing these roles together.

**KEVIN:** There are four forces, and they compound each other. The first is what I call the collapse of the build-ship-sell cycle. In traditional SaaS, product decisions and revenue outcomes were separated by months. You decided to build a feature in Q1, shipped it in Q2, sold it in Q3, and measured its revenue impact in Q4. That separation meant you could have different leaders making decisions at different stages of the cycle without much coordination cost. But in an AI-native product, a change to a prompt template or a model routing policy can alter product behavior, user outcomes, and revenue dynamics within hours. There is no buffer zone between a product decision and a revenue consequence. So the person making the product decision needs to understand the revenue implications in real time, and the person accountable for revenue needs to understand what the product is actually doing at a technical level. When cause and effect are separated by months, you can afford separate leaders. When they are separated by hours, you cannot.

**ALEX:** That makes intuitive sense. What's the second force?

**KEVIN:** The second force is the data unification imperative. In the old world, product data, engineering telemetry, and revenue data lived in separate systems with separate schemas and separate owners. Product looked at feature usage and NPS scores. Engineering looked at uptime, latency, and error rates. Revenue looked at pipeline, bookings, churn, and expansion. Each domain had its own data stack, its own dashboards, and its own definition of success. In an AI-native company, those data streams have to merge because the AI system needs all of them simultaneously. Your model routing decisions depend on understanding which customers are using which capabilities, how reliably those capabilities perform, and what the revenue impact of degraded performance would be. You cannot make intelligent decisions about model selection or prompt optimization without understanding the full picture. And when your data has to be unified, the leadership that governs that data eventually has to unify too, because someone has to own the integrated view.

**ALEX:** So the data layer is forcing an organizational convergence. What's the third force?

**KEVIN:** The third force is the continuous delivery of value rather than features. Traditional product management was organized around features. You had a roadmap, you shipped features, and you measured adoption. The product manager's job was to decide which features to build and in what order. But in an AI-native product, you're not shipping features in the traditional sense. You're tuning a system that delivers outcomes. The distinction matters enormously. When a customer success manager notices that a particular customer segment is churning, the response isn't to add a feature to the roadmap. The response might be to adjust a prompt, retrain a model on that segment's data patterns, or modify a workflow policy. That's a product change, a technical change, and a revenue intervention all at once. It doesn't fit neatly into any one leader's domain. And when your value delivery mechanism doesn't respect organizational boundaries, your organization has to evolve.

**KEVIN:** The fourth force is competitive pressure, and this one is brutal. Companies that have already converged their product, technology, and revenue leadership can move dramatically faster than companies that haven't. They can identify a revenue opportunity, make a product change, deploy it technically, and measure the impact in days rather than quarters. That speed advantage compounds over time. If your competitor can run ten optimization cycles in the time it takes you to run one, they will outlearn you, outperform you, and eventually outprice you. So convergence isn't just an efficiency play. It's a survival play. The companies that figure this out first will have a structural advantage that's very difficult to overcome.

**ALEX:** So to summarize: the build-ship-sell cycle collapsed, the data has to unify, value delivery is continuous rather than feature-based, and competitors who converge first gain compounding speed advantages. Those are the four forces.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And the critical insight is that none of these forces are about executive egos or org chart aesthetics. They are structural consequences of what AI-native products actually are. You can resist the reorg, but you can't resist the physics.

### SEGMENT 2: What the Converged Operating Model Looks Like (12 minutes)

**ALEX:** Okay, so let's assume a company is convinced that convergence is necessary. What does it actually look like in practice? Because I think a lot of people hear "merge the CPO, CRO, and CTO" and they imagine you're just giving one person three business cards.

**KEVIN:** Right, and that's exactly the wrong way to think about it. This is not about taking three existing roles and stapling them together under one title. It's about redesigning how product, technology, and revenue decisions get made in a company where those three domains are inseparable. Let me describe what the converged model actually looks like day to day. At the top, you have what we call the Chief Product and Revenue Officer, or sometimes the Chief Operating Officer in companies where the CEO wants to retain the strategic vision role. This person owns the integrated outcome: customer value delivery and revenue generation through the AI-native product. They are not a super-manager who micromanages three departments. They are the single point of accountability for the unified system.

**ALEX:** So what sits underneath that person? How does the org actually work?

**KEVIN:** Underneath, you reorganize around what we call outcome teams rather than functional departments. An outcome team is a cross-functional group that owns a specific business outcome end to end. For example, you might have an outcome team responsible for new customer activation. That team includes product designers, engineers, data scientists, and revenue operators. They own the entire activation experience, from the first touch through onboarding through the moment the customer reaches their first value milestone. They make product decisions, technical decisions, and commercial decisions as a unit because all three types of decisions affect their outcome.

**ALEX:** How is that different from the cross-functional squads that a lot of companies already run?

**KEVIN:** It's different in three crucial ways. First, in most squad models, the squad makes product and engineering decisions, but commercial decisions still go through a separate revenue organization. The squad can decide what to build, but pricing, packaging, sales motions, and customer success interventions are handled by a different group. In the converged model, the outcome team has authority over all of those levers. If the activation team determines that the pricing structure is creating friction in onboarding, they have the authority to propose and test a pricing change, not just file a request with the revenue team. Second, the metrics are different. Traditional squads measure things like feature adoption, velocity, and bug counts. Outcome teams measure business outcomes directly: activation rate, time to value, revenue per activated customer. The technical and product metrics still exist, but they are intermediate indicators, not the primary scorecard. Third, the talent composition is different. An outcome team in the converged model includes people who would traditionally sit in sales engineering, customer success, or revenue operations. These aren't consultants who visit the team occasionally. They are permanent members who bring commercial context to every product and technical decision.

**ALEX:** Can you give a concrete example of how this plays out? Like, walk me through a week in the life of an outcome team.

**KEVIN:** Sure. Let's take the activation team I mentioned. On Monday, the team reviews their outcome dashboard. They see that activation rates for enterprise customers dropped three percentage points last week. In a traditional org, this would trigger a multi-week investigation across departments. Product would look at the onboarding flow. Engineering would check for bugs. Customer success would call the customers. In the converged model, the team attacks it holistically on Monday afternoon. The data scientist on the team runs a cohort analysis and discovers that the drop correlates with a specific industry vertical. The engineer checks the model routing logs and finds that a recent prompt update degraded performance for that vertical's document types. The revenue operator pulls up the pipeline and identifies four enterprise deals in that vertical that are at risk. By Tuesday, the team has a plan. They roll back the prompt change for that vertical, deploy a specialized prompt variant, and the revenue operator briefs the account executives on what happened and what's being fixed. By Thursday, activation rates are recovering. By Friday, the team has a retrospective where they add a vertical-specific regression test to their deployment pipeline and update their model routing policy to include vertical-aware prompt selection. That entire cycle, from detection to resolution to prevention, happened in one week with one team. In a traditional org structure, that same cycle takes three to six weeks because it has to traverse three separate organizations with three separate prioritization processes.

**ALEX:** That's a compelling example. But I want to push on something. How does this work at scale? You can't have one outcome team for every possible business scenario.

**KEVIN:** You're right, and this is where the architecture of the converged model matters. You typically have five to eight outcome teams, each responsible for a major outcome domain. Common ones are acquisition, activation, engagement, expansion, and retention. Those map roughly to the customer lifecycle, and they cover the full scope of the business. Underneath those outcome teams, you have shared platform capabilities, things like the core AI infrastructure, the data platform, the model management system, and the common design system. Those are staffed by specialists who serve all the outcome teams. The converged executive's job is to allocate resources across outcome teams, resolve conflicts when team objectives collide, set the overall strategy, and ensure the shared platforms evolve to serve the teams effectively. They're not making day-to-day product or technical decisions. They're designing the system that makes those decisions well.

### SEGMENT 3: Decision Rights and Accountability (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about decision rights, because this is where convergence gets tricky. In a traditional org, it's pretty clear who decides what. The CPO owns the roadmap, the CTO owns the architecture, the CRO owns the go-to-market motion. When you converge those roles, how do you avoid everything becoming a consensus-driven mess where nobody actually has the authority to make hard calls?

**KEVIN:** This is the number one failure mode I see in companies that attempt convergence without thinking it through. They merge the roles but don't redesign the decision rights framework, and they end up with either chaos or paralysis. The answer is a tiered decision rights model that distinguishes between three types of decisions. The first type is outcome-level decisions. These are decisions about what outcomes the company is pursuing, how resources are allocated across outcome teams, what the acceptable risk thresholds are, and what the overall product and commercial strategy looks like. These are made by the converged executive with input from outcome team leads. These decisions are relatively infrequent, maybe weekly or biweekly, and they set the constraints within which teams operate.

**ALEX:** What's the second type?

**KEVIN:** The second type is team-level decisions. These are decisions about how to pursue the assigned outcome within the constraints set by the converged executive. This includes things like which experiments to run, which model variants to deploy, how to structure the user experience, what pricing tests to conduct, and how to engage specific customer segments. These decisions are made by the outcome team lead with the full cross-functional team. They happen daily or multiple times per day. The critical principle here is that team-level decisions do not require approval from the converged executive. The team has genuine autonomy within their outcome domain. If the activation team wants to test a new onboarding flow that includes a different pricing structure for the first thirty days, they can do that without asking permission, as long as it falls within the risk thresholds and strategic constraints set at the outcome level.

**ALEX:** And the third type?

**KEVIN:** The third type is platform-level decisions. These are decisions about the shared infrastructure, the core AI models, the data architecture, and the security and compliance frameworks. These affect all outcome teams and require a different governance model. Platform decisions are made by platform leads in consultation with outcome team leads, with the converged executive as the tiebreaker when there are conflicts. The governance rhythm here matters a lot. You need a weekly platform review where outcome teams surface their infrastructure needs, platform teams present their roadmap and capacity constraints, and conflicts get resolved before they become blockers.

**ALEX:** How do you handle accountability when things go wrong? In a traditional org, if revenue misses target, the CRO is accountable. If the product has quality issues, the CTO is accountable. If the product-market fit is off, the CPO is accountable. With convergence, does everything just roll up to one person?

**KEVIN:** The converged executive is ultimately accountable, yes. But the accountability model has to be more nuanced than that to actually drive the right behaviors. What we implement is a shared accountability framework with clear primary and contributing roles. For any given outcome, there is a primary accountable team and contributing teams. If the activation outcome is off target, the activation team lead is the primary accountable person. But there are contributing factors that might sit with other teams. Maybe the acquisition team is sending lower-quality leads that are harder to activate. Maybe the platform team deployed a model update that degraded activation performance. The converged executive's job during accountability reviews is to trace the causal chain, not just assign blame. The question is never just "who missed the number?" It's "what happened in the system that produced this result, and what do we change in the system to produce a better one?" That's a fundamentally different accountability conversation than the one most traditional orgs have, where each leader defends their department's performance and blames the handoff to the next department.

**ALEX:** I want to push on the political dimension here, because in practice, accountability conversations are also power conversations. How do you prevent the converged executive from playing favorites or creating an inner circle?

**KEVIN:** Transparency is the primary mechanism. All outcome metrics, team performance data, resource allocation decisions, and decision logs are visible to the entire organization. You cannot play favorites when everyone can see the scoreboard. Beyond that, the converged executive should have a structured decision-making process that's documented and consistent. When they allocate resources across outcome teams, the criteria are explicit and the reasoning is shared. When they resolve conflicts between teams, the rationale is documented. This isn't just about fairness, although fairness matters. It's about building organizational trust in the converged model. People will resist convergence if they believe it creates an opaque power center. They will embrace it if they can see that it produces better decisions faster.

### SEGMENT 4: Integrated Metrics and the Death of Departmental KPIs (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about metrics, because I think this is where the rubber really meets the road. Every executive I've ever worked with manages through metrics. If you change the metrics, you change the behavior. What happens to the measurement framework when you converge these roles?

**KEVIN:** This is one of the most important and most underestimated aspects of convergence. In a traditional SaaS org, each department has its own KPI stack. Product tracks things like monthly active users, feature adoption rates, NPS, and time in app. Engineering tracks deployment frequency, change failure rate, mean time to recovery, and uptime. Revenue tracks pipeline, bookings, ARR growth, net retention, and CAC payback period. Each department optimizes its own metrics, and in theory, the CEO ensures those departmental metrics add up to a coherent company outcome. In practice, they often conflict. Engineering optimizes for stability, which slows down product velocity. Product optimizes for feature breadth, which increases engineering complexity. Revenue optimizes for deal size, which drives product toward enterprise features that a smaller customer base actually needs. These conflicts are managed through negotiation, escalation, and horse-trading, which is incredibly slow and lossy.

**ALEX:** So what replaces the departmental KPI stacks?

**KEVIN:** An integrated outcome metrics framework. Instead of each department having its own scoreboard, you have a single set of outcome metrics that the entire converged organization is accountable for. Let me walk through what those look like. At the top level, you have what we call North Star Outcomes. These are three to five metrics that represent the fundamental health and growth of the business. For a typical AI-native product, the North Star Outcomes might be something like: customer outcome achievement rate, which measures whether customers are actually getting the results they purchased the product for; revenue per outcome delivered, which ties revenue directly to value creation; system reliability at the outcome level, which measures not just uptime but whether the AI system is producing correct and valuable results; and time to value, which measures how quickly new customers reach their first meaningful outcome.

**ALEX:** Those are very different from traditional SaaS metrics. Can you unpack what changes?

**KEVIN:** The most important shift is from activity metrics to outcome metrics. Traditional SaaS measures activity: how many users logged in, how many features were used, how many tickets were closed, how many deals were booked. Those are all inputs. The converged model measures outcomes: did the customer actually achieve what they were trying to achieve? Did the AI system produce accurate and useful results? Did the customer's business improve as a result of using the product? This matters enormously because it aligns the entire organization around customer value rather than internal activity. When your metric is feature adoption, you build more features. When your metric is customer outcome achievement, you might decide to remove features that distract from the core outcome. When your metric is deals booked, you sell to anyone with a pulse. When your metric is revenue per outcome delivered, you focus on customers where you can actually deliver value, which improves retention and unit economics.

**ALEX:** How do you handle the leading indicators though? You can't wait until the end of the quarter to find out whether your outcomes are on track.

**KEVIN:** Right, and this is where the integrated metrics framework has layers. Underneath the North Star Outcomes, you have what we call System Health Indicators. These are the leading metrics that predict whether your outcomes are trending in the right direction. They include things like model accuracy by use case, prompt effectiveness scores, workflow completion rates, customer engagement quality, not just quantity, and expansion signals. These System Health Indicators are monitored continuously, often in real time, and they serve as the early warning system. If model accuracy drops for a particular use case, that's a leading indicator that customer outcomes in that area are about to degrade, which will eventually show up as churn, which will show up as revenue decline. The converged model catches it at the model accuracy stage rather than waiting for the revenue impact to materialize.

**ALEX:** How do outcome teams use these metrics differently than traditional departments?

**KEVIN:** Each outcome team has a primary North Star Outcome that they own, plus a set of System Health Indicators that they monitor. But here's the key difference: they also track the impact of their work on other teams' outcomes. The activation team monitors not just their own activation metrics but also how activation quality affects downstream retention and expansion. This creates a systems-level awareness that traditional departmental metrics actively discourage. In a traditional org, the sales team doesn't think about retention because that's customer success's problem. In the converged model, the acquisition team absolutely thinks about retention because their outcome metrics include downstream effects. If they acquire customers who churn quickly, that shows up in their scorecard, not just in the retention team's scorecard.

**ALEX:** That feels like it could create some tension. If everyone is partially responsible for everything, does anyone feel fully responsible for anything?

**KEVIN:** It's a legitimate concern, and the design of the framework addresses it through the primary-contributing distinction I mentioned earlier. Each outcome has a primary owner who bears the majority of the accountability. Contributing teams bear a smaller share. The weights are explicit and agreed upon in advance. So the activation team might own eighty percent of the activation outcome and contribute twenty percent to the retention outcome. The retention team owns eighty percent of retention and contributes twenty percent to activation. This creates shared awareness without diffusing accountability. And in practice, it leads to much better cross-team collaboration because teams are incentivized to help each other rather than throw problems over the wall.

### SEGMENT 5: The Talent Profile of a Converged Executive (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about the human being who sits in the converged executive chair. What does this person actually look like? Because the skill sets of a traditional CPO, CRO, and CTO are very different. A CPO is typically a design thinker and market strategist. A CTO is a technologist and systems architect. A CRO is a sales leader and commercial operator. Can one person actually be all three?

**KEVIN:** The honest answer is that very few people today have all three skill sets at the depth that a traditional CPO, CRO, or CTO would have in their respective domains. And that's okay, because the converged executive role is not about being the best product person, the best technologist, and the best commercial leader simultaneously. It's about being able to operate across all three domains with enough fluency to make integrated decisions. The mental model I use is the T-shaped leader, but extended. The converged executive needs deep expertise in at least one of the three domains and working fluency in the other two. Working fluency means they can engage in substantive conversations with specialists, they can evaluate the quality of recommendations, and they can spot when a decision in one domain is going to create problems in another domain. They don't need to write the code, design the UI, or close the deal themselves.

**ALEX:** What backgrounds tend to produce this profile? Are you seeing people come from one domain more than others?

**KEVIN:** In practice, I see two pathways that work well. The first is what I call the product-to-platform leader. This is someone who started in product management, moved into a CPO role, but along the way developed genuine technical depth and commercial acumen. They understand architecture well enough to evaluate technical trade-offs. They understand revenue mechanics well enough to structure pricing and go-to-market strategies. The best product leaders have always had some of this cross-domain fluency. In the converged model, it becomes a requirement rather than a nice-to-have. The second pathway is the technical founder who scaled commercially. These are people who started as engineers or CTOs, built products that succeeded in the market, and learned the commercial side through experience. They have deep technical intuition, they developed product sense through building things customers love, and they learned revenue through the necessity of growing a business. What both pathways share is that the person has operated across domain boundaries by choice or necessity, not just within a single domain for their entire career.

**ALEX:** What about the CRO pathway? Can a revenue leader grow into this role?

**KEVIN:** It's possible but less common, and here's why. The converged executive needs to make technical judgment calls, things like whether a model architecture will scale, whether a data pipeline is designed correctly, or whether a prompt engineering approach is sound. Those judgment calls require a level of technical intuition that's hard to develop late in a career if you've never been close to the technology. Revenue leaders who have spent their careers in sales and marketing often have excellent strategic instincts and deep customer understanding, but they struggle with the technical dimension. That said, I've seen CROs who were former solutions engineers or who spent significant time in product-led growth environments make the transition successfully. The key variable is not which domain they came from. It's whether they've voluntarily crossed domain boundaries throughout their career.

**ALEX:** What about the softer skills? What does this person need beyond domain fluency?

**KEVIN:** Three things stand out. First, systems thinking. The converged executive has to see the product, technology, and revenue as one interconnected system rather than three separate domains that happen to share a building. This means they naturally think about second-order effects, feedback loops, and emergent behaviors. When they make a pricing decision, they simultaneously consider how it affects product design incentives and technical architecture requirements. That kind of integrated reasoning doesn't come naturally to most people. It has to be developed through experience managing complex, interdependent systems. Second, they need extremely high comfort with ambiguity and rapid decision-making under uncertainty. The converged model moves fast, and many decisions involve trade-offs across domains where there is no obviously right answer. The converged executive has to be able to make a call, learn from the results, and adjust without agonizing. Third, they need what I call organizational empathy, which is the ability to understand how different parts of the organization experience change and to design transitions that respect people's expertise and identity. Convergence threatens people's professional identities. A CTO who is told they now report to a converged executive might feel like their domain is being devalued. Managing those dynamics requires genuine emotional intelligence, not just structural authority.

### SEGMENT 6: Organizational Resistance and Managing the Transition (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** That's a perfect segue into what I think is the hardest part of all this: actually making the transition. You can design the perfect converged org chart on a whiteboard, but implementing it means changing how real people with real careers and real egos work together. Where does the resistance come from, and how do you manage it?

**KEVIN:** Resistance comes from three sources, and each one requires a different management strategy. The first source is identity threat. Senior leaders in product, engineering, and revenue have built their careers around domain expertise. The CPO's identity is built around being the voice of the customer and the guardian of the product vision. The CTO's identity is built around technical excellence and architectural judgment. The CRO's identity is built around market understanding and revenue generation. Convergence can feel like an existential threat to those identities because it implies that their domain expertise alone is no longer sufficient. The management strategy for identity threat is not to minimize it or pretend it doesn't exist. You have to acknowledge it directly and reframe it. The conversation needs to be: your domain expertise is more valuable than ever, but it's now being deployed within a system that amplifies its impact. A CTO who understands revenue dynamics is a better CTO, not a lesser one. A CPO who understands technical architecture makes better product decisions, not worse ones. You're not losing your expertise. You're gaining context that makes your expertise more powerful.

**ALEX:** What's the second source of resistance?

**KEVIN:** The second source is legitimate structural concern, and this is the resistance you should actually listen to carefully. When experienced leaders push back on convergence, sometimes they're identifying real risks. A CTO might say, "If product and revenue priorities dominate decision-making, we'll accumulate technical debt that eventually destroys the platform." A CRO might say, "If product and engineering people are making commercial decisions, we'll underprice our value and leave money on the table." A CPO might say, "If revenue metrics drive product decisions, we'll build whatever customers ask for instead of what they actually need." Every one of those concerns is valid. They're not just turf protection. They're pointing to real failure modes of poorly executed convergence. The management strategy here is to design explicit safeguards into the converged model that address each concern. Technical debt gets managed through platform health metrics that are elevated to the same importance as revenue metrics. Commercial discipline gets maintained through pricing governance frameworks that require analytical rigor even when teams have pricing authority. Product vision gets preserved through strategic guardrails that prevent pure revenue optimization from overriding long-term product differentiation. When leaders see that their concerns have been heard and addressed in the structural design, their resistance usually shifts from opposition to constructive engagement.

**ALEX:** And the third source?

**KEVIN:** The third source is loss of power, and I want to be blunt about this. In most organizations, the CPO, CRO, and CTO are each running large organizations with significant headcount, budget, and influence. Convergence, if it means those three roles collapse into one, means that at least two of those leaders are either losing their C-suite position or being asked to operate under a peer who was previously their equal. That is a real loss of power, and no amount of reframing changes that reality. The management strategy here depends on the individuals involved. In the best cases, one of the three leaders is the natural choice for the converged role, and the other two are comfortable operating as senior leaders within the new structure because they believe in the model and see an opportunity to have greater impact even with less positional authority. In more difficult cases, one or more of the existing leaders will choose to leave, and that needs to be handled with respect and generosity. What you absolutely cannot do is pretend that the power dynamics don't exist or try to create a "co-lead" model where three people share the converged role equally. Co-leadership at this level almost never works because it recreates the coordination overhead you were trying to eliminate.

**ALEX:** How do you sequence the transition? Do you do it all at once, or gradually?

**KEVIN:** Gradually, and in a very specific sequence. Phase one is what I call "convergence of information." You don't change any titles, roles, or reporting lines. You simply ensure that the CPO, CRO, and CTO are operating from a single integrated data set and participating in shared decision-making forums. This usually means creating a unified dashboard, establishing a joint operating rhythm such as a weekly leadership sync where decisions are made collectively, and killing the separate departmental reviews that allow leaders to optimize in isolation. This phase typically takes two to three months, and its primary purpose is to build the muscle memory of integrated decision-making before you formalize it. Phase two is "convergence of teams." You start reorganizing around outcome teams, but you do it with one or two pilot teams rather than a wholesale reorg. You pick a high-visibility outcome, maybe activation or retention, staff a genuine cross-functional team, give them real authority over their outcome, and let them demonstrate the model. This phase takes three to six months, and it serves two purposes: it produces concrete evidence that the converged model works, and it creates internal advocates who've experienced the benefits firsthand. Phase three is "convergence of leadership." Based on the evidence from phases one and two, you formalize the converged executive role, complete the team reorganization, and implement the full integrated metrics framework. By this point, the organization has been living parts of the converged model for six to nine months, so the structural change feels like a formalization of what's already happening rather than a disruptive revolution.

**ALEX:** That phased approach sounds much more manageable than a big-bang reorg. How long does the full transition typically take?

**KEVIN:** Nine to fifteen months from kickoff to full convergence, depending on company size and organizational complexity. Larger companies with deeply entrenched departmental cultures take longer. Smaller companies or those with founder-led cultures where the founder already operates across domains can move faster. The critical thing is not to rush it. If you try to do the full convergence in three months, you'll get the org chart change but not the behavioral change, and you'll end up with the worst of both worlds: a converged structure that operates like three separate departments wearing the same jersey.

### SEGMENT 7: When Convergence Goes Wrong (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** Before we wrap up, I want to spend a few minutes on the failure modes, because we've painted a largely optimistic picture of convergence. When does it go wrong, and what should people watch out for?

**KEVIN:** There are three major failure modes. The first is power concentration without checks. When you put product, technology, and revenue under one leader, you've created a very powerful role. If that person doesn't have strong governance around them, meaning a CEO who provides genuine oversight, a board that asks hard questions, and a team that feels empowered to push back, you can end up with a fiefdom where one person's biases and blind spots shape the entire business without correction. I've seen converged executives who came from a revenue background systematically underinvest in platform quality because they didn't viscerally understand the long-term cost of technical debt. I've seen converged executives from engineering backgrounds build technically elegant products that nobody wants to buy because they couldn't see the market clearly. The safeguard is robust governance, peer challenge, and regular external review.

**ALEX:** What's the second failure mode?

**KEVIN:** The second is premature convergence, which means converging before the organization has the maturity to operate the model. Convergence requires a high level of data infrastructure maturity, because the integrated metrics framework depends on reliable, real-time data. It requires a high level of team maturity, because outcome teams need members who can operate across domain boundaries. And it requires a high level of leadership maturity, because the converged executive and the outcome team leads need to navigate ambiguity and make trade-offs that most traditional managers have never faced. If you converge before those maturity prerequisites are in place, you get dysfunction dressed up as innovation. Teams can't make good decisions because they don't have the data. Leaders can't operate across domains because they don't have the skills. And the converged executive can't run the integrated system because the system isn't actually integrated yet. It's still three separate systems pretending to be one.

**ALEX:** And the third failure mode?

**KEVIN:** The third is what I call convergence theater, which is when a company changes the titles and the org chart but doesn't change the actual operating model. The converged executive exists on paper, but product, engineering, and revenue still operate as separate departments with separate priorities and separate metrics. The outcome teams exist on the org chart, but they don't have real authority over their outcomes. The integrated metrics framework exists in a dashboard, but people still manage to their departmental KPIs. This is the most common failure mode, and it's pernicious because it gives the organization the illusion of having modernized without any of the actual benefits. The converged structure absorbs all the disruption cost of the transition but delivers none of the speed and integration benefits. The way to avoid it is to be relentlessly honest about whether the behavioral change is actually happening, not just the structural change. Are decisions being made faster? Are teams genuinely operating across domain boundaries? Are the integrated metrics actually driving behavior? If the answer to those questions is no after six months of living in the converged model, something is fundamentally broken and needs to be addressed.

If your product is a unified system that blends product behavior, technical infrastructure, and commercial logic into one living whole, but your leadership model treats those as separate domains with separate owners, you have a structural mismatch.
