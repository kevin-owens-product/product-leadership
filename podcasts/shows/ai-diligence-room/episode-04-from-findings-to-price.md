### Sorting the findings

**NADIA:** In episode two, you said every finding should be labeled. Remind us of the labels.

**OWEN:** Four labels. Valuation issues change the model, and therefore the price. Contract issues go to counsel to be addressed in the purchase agreement, through representations, indemnities or covenants. Closing conditions must be fixed before the deal closes. And plan items go into the post-closing plan, with a cost and an owner. Some findings get more than one label.

**NADIA:** How do you decide which label?

**OWEN:** By asking three questions. Is the issue already reflected in the numbers, or does it change what the business will earn? If it changes earnings, it's a valuation issue. Is it a risk that might or might not materialize, where we want the seller to stand behind it? That's a contract issue. Is it something so serious that we wouldn't want to own the business until it's fixed, and the seller can fix it before closing? That's a closing condition. And is it something we can fix ourselves after closing, at a known cost? That's a plan item.

**NADIA:** Give me examples of each.

**OWEN:** From our made-up companies. Ledgerline's human reviewers belonging in cost of revenue: valuation. FleetPilot's uncertain rights to use older customer data for a pooled model: contract, maybe with a plan item to update contracts at renewal. A target using a licensed data set for training in clear breach of the license, where the licensor would need to agree a fix: possibly a closing condition. FleetPilot's missing evaluation suite: plan item.

**NADIA:** And the severity?

**OWEN:** Rate each finding by likelihood and impact, the way you'd rate any risk. A high-impact, high-likelihood finding might change your view of the whole deal. A low-impact one might just be a line in the plan. The danger is a diligence report with forty findings all given equal weight. The deal team needs to know the three that matter.

### Valuation: what changes in the model

**NADIA:** Let's start with valuation. What AI findings most commonly change the model?

**OWEN:** Five come up again and again. First, gross margin, when the cost of delivering AI has been understated, whether that's human reviewers outside cost of revenue or AI usage costs that are growing faster than revenue. Second, the growth assumptions, when projected AI product revenue depends on launches, adoption or pricing that the evidence doesn't support.

**NADIA:** Third.

**OWEN:** Pricing durability, when the disruption test shows that seat-based or hourly revenue is exposed. That changes the forecast and the exit multiple. Fourth, required investment, when the target needs to spend money on its AI platform, data, security or compliance that isn't in management's plan. Building evaluations, a model routing layer, EU AI Act documentation, or replacing a model whose license doesn't fit. And fifth, adjusted EBITDA, when management's figures include AI savings that haven't happened yet.

**NADIA:** How do you handle those AI savings add-backs?

**OWEN:** Skeptically. Management may present adjusted EBITDA that includes, say, two million dollars of annualized savings from an AI initiative that launched two months ago. The buyer's quality of earnings provider should test whether those savings are already in the run rate, partly in it, or still a projection. Savings that are a projection belong in the value creation plan, where the buyer can decide how much to pay for them, not in the base earnings.

**NADIA:** And how much should a buyer pay for AI upside?

**OWEN:** That's the old private equity question with a new label. How much of the value you plan to create should you pay the seller for? Generally, as little as competition allows. If the AI upside is something the buyer will create with its own playbook and effort, the buyer shouldn't hand it to the seller in the price. If it's something the seller has already built and proven, the seller deserves credit for it.

**NADIA:** So proven AI value gets paid for, planned AI value mostly doesn't.

**OWEN:** That's the principle. In competitive auctions, buyers often end up paying for some of the planned value to win. Then it's critical that they're confident they can deliver it, and diligence is where that confidence comes from.

### The exit multiple question

**NADIA:** Last episode, you said disruption bites hardest in the exit multiple. How does that show up in the deal model?

**OWEN:** Most deal models assume an exit multiple, often similar to the entry multiple. For an AI-exposed business, that's a big assumption. If the business looks more exposed at exit than it does today, the multiple could be lower. If the plan works and the business looks clearly less exposed, it could be higher.

**NADIA:** So you model it explicitly.

**OWEN:** As a sensitivity, at minimum. What do the returns look like if the exit multiple is two turns lower because buyers in five years see the business as exposed? If the deal still works, good. If it only works with a flat or rising multiple, then the AI plan isn't just about earnings, it's about perception, and the plan should be designed with the exit buyer's diligence in mind. That's actually a theme of our sister show, Talking AI to the Board and LPs.

**NADIA:** And on the upside?

**OWEN:** A business that's genuinely transformed, with evidence, can earn a premium. But I'd be careful about modeling a multiple expansion for AI. Exit buyers have become skeptical of AI stories, for all the reasons we covered in episode one. Model the earnings improvement, and treat multiple expansion as a bonus.

### When to walk away

**NADIA:** We've talked about how to fix findings with price and terms. When should a buyer simply walk away?

**OWEN:** I'd say four situations. First, when the AI claim is central to the value and turns out to be substantially false. Not exaggerated at the edges, but false at the core, like the undisclosed-human pattern where the economics depend on automation that isn't there. At that point, the problem isn't just the AI. It's that you can't trust the rest of what management told you.

**NADIA:** Second.

**OWEN:** When the core asset rests on data the company has no right to use, and there's no realistic way to fix it. If the main model was built on content that's clearly licensed against training, or scraped from a competitor, and the owner is likely to sue, the risk may simply be too big to insure, indemnify or price. The Ross case is the cautionary tale.

**NADIA:** Third.

**OWEN:** When the disruption test says the business will be structurally smaller, and the price only works if it isn't. Some sellers' price expectations were set before the market started pricing AI risk. If the gap can't be closed, walk away, and maybe come back later.

**NADIA:** And fourth?

**OWEN:** When management won't engage honestly. If the team refuses data access, gives inconsistent answers and gets defensive about every question, you'll have the same problems after closing, except then they're yours. A good business with an honest team and real problems is a better investment than a polished story with a team you can't trust.

**NADIA:** And the hardest part?

**OWEN:** Walking away after weeks of work and advisor fees, when the investment committee is expecting a deal. The discipline to walk away is what makes the rest of the diligence worth doing.

### The purchase agreement: AI representations

**NADIA:** Let's move to the contract. What are AI representations and warranties?

**OWEN:** Representations and warranties are statements the seller makes about the business in the purchase agreement. If they turn out to be untrue, the buyer may have a claim. Purchase agreements have always included representations about intellectual property, data privacy, information technology and compliance with laws. AI-specific representations have become more common in the last two or three years, as buyers realized the standard ones didn't quite cover AI risks.

**NADIA:** What do they typically cover?

**OWEN:** It varies a lot, and it's negotiated. But a typical set might include: a list of the AI systems the business develops or uses in material ways, disclosed in a schedule. That the business has the rights it needs to the data used to train or operate its AI systems. That its use of AI complies with applicable laws, including privacy and AI-specific laws. That it complies with the licenses and terms of the AI models and tools it uses. That it has policies for responsible AI use and follows them. And that there haven't been material incidents, like data breaches, harmful outputs or regulatory inquiries, involving its AI.

**NADIA:** What about the claims we talked about in episode one?

**OWEN:** Some buyers add a representation that the business's public and customer-facing statements about its AI capabilities are accurate and not misleading. Given the regulatory actions on AI-washing, that's a reasonable ask, especially if the AI claims were part of why the buyer valued the business as it did.

**NADIA:** And sellers push back?

**OWEN:** Of course. Sellers typically try to limit the representations with knowledge qualifiers, meaning "to the seller's knowledge," and materiality qualifiers, meaning only material issues count. They'll also want to disclose known issues in the schedules, which takes them out of the representations. That's why diligence matters: what's disclosed is usually not something the buyer can claim on later, so the buyer needs to understand it and price it before signing.

**NADIA:** So the disclosure schedule is where the findings show up.

**OWEN:** Often, yes. A well-prepared seller will disclose the issues it knows about, which protects it. The buyer then decides whether to accept the risk, price it, or ask for a specific protection.

### Specific indemnities, escrows and covenants

**NADIA:** What are the specific protections?

**OWEN:** The main one is a specific indemnity. For a known issue, the seller agrees to cover losses arising from that issue, whatever the general representations say. For example, if a target used a data set whose license may not permit training, the buyer might ask for a specific indemnity for any claims by the data owner. Because it's a known issue, insurance probably won't cover it, so the seller's indemnity is the main protection.

**NADIA:** And to make sure the seller can pay?

**OWEN:** An escrow or a holdback. Part of the purchase price is held back for a period, to fund any indemnity claims. For a significant AI-related risk, like pending litigation over data use, the buyer might negotiate a specific escrow sized to the potential exposure.

**NADIA:** And covenants?

**OWEN:** Covenants are promises about what the parties will do. Between signing and closing, the buyer usually wants the seller to run the business in the ordinary course, and may add AI-specific limits, like not launching new AI products that use customer data in new ways, not entering significant new AI vendor commitments, and not changing data practices without consent. And sometimes there's a covenant to fix something before closing, like obtaining a license amendment or updating privacy notices.

**NADIA:** And closing conditions?

**OWEN:** Those are things that must happen before the buyer is obliged to close. They're relatively rare for AI issues, because they create deal uncertainty that both sides dislike. But for a fundamental problem, like a core model trained on data the target clearly has no right to use, where the licensor must agree a settlement, a closing condition might be appropriate.

---

### Insurance and the exclusions

**NADIA:** Let's talk about representations and warranties insurance. It's become standard in many private equity deals. How does AI fit?

**OWEN:** R&W insurance covers losses from breaches of the representations that weren't known at signing. It lets sellers walk away cleanly and gives buyers a creditworthy source of recovery. Foley noted that R&W insurers are starting to exclude AI-specific risks. That's a significant development, because it means the AI representations the buyer worked hard to negotiate might not be insured.

**NADIA:** Why are insurers excluding AI?

**OWEN:** Because the risks are new, hard to measure, and potentially large. Copyright litigation over training data. Privacy claims over AI use. Regulatory penalties under new laws. Insurers don't yet have much history to price those risks, so some are excluding them, or excluding them unless the diligence was strong.

**NADIA:** And how does a buyer keep the exclusions narrow?

**OWEN:** Mostly with good diligence. Underwriters read the diligence reports. If they see that the buyer did a thorough review of data rights, model licenses, privacy and AI-specific compliance, and found the business in reasonable shape, they're more willing to cover AI representations, or to narrow the exclusion to specific identified risks. If the diligence on AI was one paragraph in a general technology report, they'll likely exclude it broadly.

**NADIA:** So the diligence report is written for two audiences.

**OWEN:** At least two: the deal team and the insurer. A clear, well-organized AI section, with the scope, the work done and the findings, is worth a lot in the insurance negotiation. And where an exclusion remains, the buyer has to decide whether to negotiate a seller indemnity for that risk, or accept it and price it.

**NADIA:** Are there specialized AI insurance products?

**OWEN:** There's a small but growing market. A few insurers and specialist firms have launched coverage for AI-specific risks, like an AI system underperforming against agreed metrics or producing harmful errors. It's early, and coverage varies a lot. For most mid-market deals, the practical questions are still the R&W exclusions and whether the target's existing policies, like cyber, technology errors and omissions, and media liability, cover AI-related claims. Check those policies' wording, because some are adding AI exclusions too.

---

### Earnouts and AI products

**NADIA:** What about earnouts? Where part of the price depends on future performance.

**OWEN:** Earnouts come up when buyer and seller disagree about the value of something uncertain, and AI products are a classic case. The seller says its new AI product will double revenue. The buyer isn't sure. So they agree that part of the price will be paid only if the AI product hits certain targets.

**NADIA:** Sounds sensible.

**OWEN:** It can be, but AI earnouts have particular pitfalls. First, definitions. What counts as AI product revenue? If customers buy a bundle, how is revenue allocated? If the product is repriced or merged with another, how does that count? Second, control. After closing, the buyer runs the business. If the buyer decides to change the AI product strategy, switch models, or integrate it with another product, the seller may claim the buyer undermined the earnout. Third, costs. If AI usage costs rise, does the earnout measure revenue, which ignores costs, or profit, which the buyer controls?

**NADIA:** So how do you structure it?

**OWEN:** Clear definitions, tied to metrics that can be measured from the systems. Agreed rules on what the buyer can and can't do with the product during the earnout. A measure that reflects what the buyer actually values: often gross profit from the product rather than revenue. And a shorter period, because AI markets move fast, and a three-year earnout on an AI product is a long time.

**NADIA:** And disputes?

**OWEN:** Earnout disputes are common in general. A clear mechanism, like an independent accountant for calculations, helps. But the best protection is clarity at signing.

---

### Key people and retention

**NADIA:** Let's talk about people again. In episode one, you mentioned key person risk in AI teams. How does that show up in the deal?

**OWEN:** In retention arrangements. If the target's AI capability depends on a handful of people, the buyer wants to keep them through the transition and beyond. That typically means retention bonuses paid over time, new equity in the post-deal company, and sometimes changes to roles or titles. The cost of those arrangements belongs in the deal model.

**NADIA:** And non-competes?

**OWEN:** Non-competes are increasingly restricted in some places, so buyers rely more on incentives than on restrictions. The best retention tool is giving the key people a compelling reason to stay: interesting work, resources to do it well, and a share in the upside. AI engineers are in high demand, and a new owner that cuts the AI budget will lose them quickly.

**NADIA:** And founders?

**OWEN:** If the founder is the AI visionary, their role after closing matters. Some founders stay and thrive under private equity ownership. Some don't. Discuss the role openly during the deal, and design incentives accordingly. And make sure the AI knowledge isn't only in the founder's head.

---

### Carve-outs and shared AI

**NADIA:** What about carve-outs? Buying a division from a larger company.

**OWEN:** Carve-outs have a particular AI problem. The division often uses AI tools, models and data that belong to the parent. The enterprise agreement with the AI provider is in the parent's name. The data platform that feeds the division's AI is shared. The AI team might be a central group that supports several divisions. When the division is sold, all of that has to be separated.

**NADIA:** How do you handle it?

**OWEN:** With a careful separation plan and a transitional services agreement. The TSA lets the division keep using the parent's systems for a period after closing, while it sets up its own. For AI, the TSA should cover access to the AI tools and models, the data platform, any shared AI services, and support from the AI team. It should also address data: which data belongs to the division, which models trained on shared data the division can keep, and how data is separated.

**NADIA:** That last point sounds tricky.

**OWEN:** It can be very tricky. If the parent trained a model on data from all its divisions, and the division uses that model, who owns it after the sale? Usually the parent keeps it, and the division needs its own. That might mean retraining, which takes time and data, or licensing the parent's model during a transition. Find this out early, because it can affect the price and the timeline.

**NADIA:** And the AI provider contracts?

**OWEN:** Can't usually be assigned without consent, and the division won't get the parent's volume discounts. So the division may face higher AI costs after separation. That should be in the model too.

### Negotiating the adjustment

**NADIA:** Let's talk about the negotiation itself. A buyer finds a problem and wants to lower the price. How does that conversation go well?

**OWEN:** It goes well when it's specific and evidenced. A price chip that says "we have concerns about the AI" will be rejected, and it damages trust. A price adjustment that says "your human review team costs four million dollars a year and sits in G and A. Moving it into cost of revenue lowers gross margin by six points. At the multiple we're paying, that's worth this much, and here's the analysis" is much harder to argue with.

**NADIA:** And timing?

**OWEN:** Raise it as early as possible. Sellers hate surprises in the final week, and a late chip can blow up a deal. If the claim test in week two shows a problem, flag it to the seller's advisors in week three, and let them respond. Sometimes there's an explanation you missed. Sometimes they'll disclose more. Either way, the final negotiation goes better.

**NADIA:** And the alternatives to a price cut?

**OWEN:** There are several. A specific indemnity, if the issue is a risk rather than a certainty. An escrow. An earnout, if the issue is uncertainty about future AI revenue. A seller-funded fix before closing. Or a change in structure, like rollover equity, where the seller keeps a stake and shares the risk and the upside. Often a mix of these works better than a simple price reduction, because it lets both sides express their actual views.

**NADIA:** And in a competitive auction?

**OWEN:** In a competitive auction, the buyer has less leverage, and other bidders may not have found the same issue. Then the question is whether the finding changes your view of the value enough to bid lower and risk losing. Sometimes the right answer is to lose the auction to a bidder who didn't do the work. That's painful, but it's not a mistake.

### When AI litigation is already pending

**NADIA:** What if the target already faces AI-related litigation? A copyright claim over training data, say, or a privacy class action.

**OWEN:** Then it's a known issue, so it's excluded from R&W insurance, and the protection has to come from the seller. The buyer needs counsel's assessment of the merits, the likely range of outcomes, the cost of defense, and the timeline. AI copyright cases in particular can take years, and the law is still developing, so the range of outcomes can be wide.

**NADIA:** And the structure?

**OWEN:** Typically a specific indemnity for the litigation, an escrow sized to a realistic downside, and agreement on who controls the defense and settlement decisions. The buyer will want a say, because a settlement that requires changing the product, like deleting a model or stopping use of certain data, affects the business it now owns.

**NADIA:** And beyond the money?

**OWEN:** Think about the operational consequence of losing. If the court ruled against the target, what would it have to change? Retrain a model without certain data? Stop a product feature? Pay a license fee going forward? Those consequences can matter more than any damages award, and they should be in the scenarios.

---

### AI in add-on acquisitions

**NADIA:** Last deal topic. Many private equity platforms grow by add-on acquisitions. How does AI diligence work for a small add-on?

**OWEN:** Lighter, but not absent. For a small add-on, a full technical review is usually overkill. But a short questionnaire and a call can cover the essentials: what AI tools the business uses, what data goes into them, whether any customer-facing AI exists, what the contracts say, and whether there are any incidents or disputes. That can be done in a day.

**NADIA:** And the integration?

**OWEN:** The bigger AI question for add-ons is integration into the platform. If the platform has built AI capabilities, like central call handling, AI dispatch or AI-assisted billing, the add-on's value depends on how quickly it can join. So the diligence question shifts: how compatible are the add-on's systems and data with the platform's? How long will migration take? What will the AI do for this business once it's integrated? That's the roll-up lesson from the Real Economy show: the platform comes before the AI.

**NADIA:** And the risk of importing problems?

**OWEN:** Real. An add-on that's been feeding customer data into unapproved tools, or has a website chatbot making promises, brings those risks into the platform. The first thirty days list we described applies to every add-on, on a smaller scale.

### FleetPilot: from findings to terms

**NADIA:** Let's go back to our made-up FleetPilot, the dispatch software company from episode two. What happened to the findings in the deal?

**OWEN:** Let's recap the five findings. Uncertain rights to use older customer data for a pooled model. An undocumented open-weight model with one key person. Heavy concentration on one model provider with no evaluation suite. An agent with security exposure to prompt injection. And AI costs growing faster than AI revenue.

**NADIA:** And the labels?

**OWEN:** The data rights issue became a contract issue and a plan item. The buyer asked for a specific representation that FleetPilot had the right to use customer data as it does, and, since the seller disclosed the older contracts, a specific indemnity for claims from those customers, backed by a modest escrow. The plan included updating those contracts at renewal over two years.

**NADIA:** The key person?

**OWEN:** A retention package for the engineer who maintains the model, and a plan item to document the training process and hire a second person within six months.

**NADIA:** Concentration and evaluations?

**OWEN:** A valuation item, because building a routing layer and an evaluation suite costs money, about six months of a small team's time, which the buyer added to the investment case. And a plan item to do it.

**NADIA:** The security exposure?

**OWEN:** This one was more serious. The agent was being rolled out to more customers. The buyer asked for a covenant that FleetPilot wouldn't expand the agent's rollout between signing and closing without the buyer's consent, and agreed with management a security review and approval steps for high-risk actions as a first-thirty-days priority after closing.

**NADIA:** And cost?

**OWEN:** A plan item, with an upside in the model: if cost optimization worked as the team estimated, gross margin on AI features would improve meaningfully in year one. The buyer didn't pay the seller for that upside, because it hadn't happened yet.

**NADIA:** And insurance?

**OWEN:** Because the AI diligence was thorough and clearly documented, the R&W insurer agreed to cover the AI representations, except for the known data rights issue, which was covered by the seller's specific indemnity instead. The prompt injection risk was disclosed, so it was excluded too, and the buyer relied on fixing it fast.

**NADIA:** And the price?

**OWEN:** Slightly lower than the seller's initial ask, mainly because of the gross margin and investment adjustments. The seller accepted, partly because the buyer explained the adjustments clearly with evidence, rather than just chipping the price.

### Pricing the fix list

**NADIA:** You keep saying plan items need a cost. How precise does that need to be?

**OWEN:** Precise enough to matter in the model, which usually means a range. Building an evaluation suite for a mid-sized AI product might take a few engineers a few months. Adding a model routing layer, similar. A security review and fixes for an agent, a few weeks of specialist time plus engineering. Updating customer contracts, legal time spread over renewal cycles. EU AI Act documentation for a high-risk system, potentially many months of work across product, engineering and legal.

**NADIA:** And who estimates it?

**OWEN:** The technical advisor, checked with the target's own engineers, who usually know best. Add a contingency, because these projects always take longer than planned. And then put the total into the investment case as a one-time cost, alongside the other post-closing investments. A buyer who forgets the fix list ends up funding it anyway, out of the returns.

---

### The handoff to the hundred days

**NADIA:** Let's talk about the handoff. Why is it so often neglected?

**OWEN:** Because the deal team is exhausted, the advisors' engagement ends at signing or closing, and the operating team that takes over often wasn't involved in diligence. So the diligence report sits in a folder, and the operating team rediscovers the same issues three months later.

**NADIA:** How do you fix it?

**OWEN:** Make the handoff a formal step. Before closing, the deal team, the technical and commercial advisors, and the operating team hold a working session. They go through every plan item and every risk, agree owners, priorities and timelines, and turn it into the first draft of the hundred-day plan. Ideally, someone from the operating team has been part of the diligence from the start, so they already know the details.

**NADIA:** What does the AI part of the hundred-day plan typically include?

**OWEN:** Two lists. A fix list: the risks from diligence that need attention, like security fixes, contract updates, documentation, key person cover, policy and inventory. And a value list: the upside opportunities, like cost optimization, new pricing, AI product improvements, and internal productivity. The fix list goes first, because some of it may be urgent, but the value list should start in parallel, because that's what the investment case depends on.

**NADIA:** And for the non-AI-company targets?

**OWEN:** For a services or industrial business, the hundred days look more like what the Real Economy show described: find the money in the workflows, pick a quick win and a bigger bet, measure in dollars. The diligence should have given a head start on which workflows and what the data looks like.

### The first thirty days on AI risk

**NADIA:** You said the fix list comes first after closing. What actually happens in the first thirty days?

**OWEN:** Five things, for almost any target. First, confirm the AI inventory. The one from diligence was a good start, but now you have full access. Talk to every function, check the software subscriptions and the network data, and find the tools nobody mentioned. There are always some.

**NADIA:** Second.

**OWEN:** Close the urgent gaps. Any sensitive data flowing into unapproved tools gets stopped, and people get an approved alternative the same week. Any customer-facing AI without monitoring gets a named owner and a review routine. Any agent with excessive permissions gets them reduced. And the specific risks from diligence, like FleetPilot's prompt injection exposure, get their fixes started.

**NADIA:** Third.

**OWEN:** Put a simple policy and approval process in place, if there isn't one. One page on what's allowed, what isn't, and how to get a new tool approved. A small group, often the CTO or COO with legal and security, who approves new AI tools quickly. The goal is to make the safe path the easy path, not to slow everything down.

**NADIA:** Fourth.

**OWEN:** Make sure the incident plan covers AI. If the phone agent says something harmful, or the AI leaks customer data, or a model provider goes down, who gets called, who decides, and who talks to customers? A short addition to the existing incident plan is usually enough.

**NADIA:** And fifth?

**OWEN:** Report it to the board. A one-page update at the first board meeting: what AI the company uses, what risks were found, what's been fixed, and what's in progress. That sets the tone that AI is a governed part of the business, and it gives the board what it needs for its own oversight duties, which we cover in detail in The Rulebook.

### Scoping the advisors

**NADIA:** Let's get practical about advisors. When a deal team hires a technical advisor for AI diligence, what should the scope say?

**OWEN:** Be specific, because a vague scope produces a vague report. The scope should list the questions: the claim test with production data, the system review, data rights mapping with counsel, model licenses and concentration, cost drivers, quality and monitoring, AI security, technical debt, and regulatory classification inputs for counsel. It should say what access the advisor needs and by when. And it should specify the deliverable: findings labeled by type and severity, with cost estimates for plan items.

**NADIA:** And the commercial advisor?

**OWEN:** For the disruption test, the commercial advisor's scope should include the task analysis, customer research designed around customers' AI plans, competitor and funding analysis, pricing model review, and scenarios with an exit multiple sensitivity. Many commercial diligence scopes still treat AI as a paragraph in the market overview. For exposed businesses, that's not enough.

**NADIA:** And legal?

**OWEN:** Counsel's scope should explicitly include AI: data rights and licenses, privacy and AI-specific regulation in the relevant jurisdictions, AI-related litigation or inquiries, the AI terms in customer and vendor contracts, and drafting the AI-specific provisions in the purchase agreement. And the advisors should talk to each other. The technical advisor finds the data sources, and counsel checks the rights. If they work in separate silos, things fall through the gap.

**NADIA:** And the cost?

**OWEN:** A focused AI technical review is usually a modest fraction of total diligence spend, and on deals where AI is central to value, it's one of the best-value parts of the budget. One price adjustment or one avoided problem typically pays for it many times over.

### The seller's side: getting ready

**NADIA:** Let's flip to the seller. If you're planning to sell a company in the next couple of years, what does all this mean?

**OWEN:** It means you can expect every serious buyer to run some version of the diligence we've described, and you can prepare for it. Sellers who prepare get better prices, faster deals, and fewer surprises. Sellers who don't prepare get price chips, specific indemnities, escrows and insurance exclusions.

**NADIA:** What's in the preparation?

**OWEN:** An AI readiness pack, put together a year or so before the sale. An inventory of AI systems, with owners, vendors, data used and purpose. A data rights register, with the terms for each data source. The contracts with AI vendors and model providers, with data and exit terms easy to find. The AI policy, and evidence that it's followed. Evaluation and monitoring results for any AI products. Security testing results. And for any AI claims in your marketing or management presentations, the evidence behind them.

**NADIA:** And fixing things?

**OWEN:** Fix what you can before the sale. Update customer contracts to cover AI use of data. Replace data sets or models with problematic licenses. Move human reviewer costs to where they belong in the accounts, so the buyer doesn't find it. Make sure staff are using approved AI tools. All of that is cheaper to fix before a buyer finds it than after.

**NADIA:** And vendor due diligence?

**OWEN:** Many sellers commission their own diligence reports, including technology, before going to market. Adding a strong AI section to vendor diligence can head off buyer concerns and support the insurance process. It also forces the seller to see its business the way a buyer will.

**NADIA:** And the AI claims in the marketing materials?

**OWEN:** Be accurate. Every AI claim in the information memorandum is a claim the buyer will test, and possibly a representation the seller will have to stand behind. An honest, evidenced AI story beats an inflated one every time, because the inflated one gets discovered, and then the buyer starts doubting everything else too.

---

### What sellers' management presentations get wrong

**NADIA:** One more seller-side point. In auctions, management presentations are where the AI story gets told. What do they get wrong?

**OWEN:** Three things. Overclaiming, which we've covered. Using vocabulary instead of evidence: "agentic," "AI-native," "proprietary models," with no numbers behind them. And putting the AI story at the start, as the headline, when the business is really a strong services or software company with good AI capability. Buyers see dozens of AI headlines. What they remember is a clear business with proof.

**NADIA:** So what works?

**OWEN:** Start with the business: what it does, why customers pay, and the financial track record. Then show AI where it matters, with evidence. "We introduced AI-assisted invoice coding two years ago. Here's the automation rate by quarter, here's the effect on gross margin, here's what customers say." Then the roadmap, with honest risks. And invite the buyer's team to test it. Confidence that invites scrutiny is the strongest signal a seller can send.

### A diligence checklist for the season

**NADIA:** Let's give people a checklist for the whole season.

**OWEN:** Here's the short version. Scope the four questions: threat, claim, risk, upside. For claims: decompose, get production data, look for undisclosed humans, test the product, and call customers the seller didn't choose. For the stack: draw the system, check data rights for every source and personal data use, review model licenses and concentration, examine the layer around the model, understand cost drivers, ask about quality monitoring, test AI security, and measure AI technical debt.

**NADIA:** And then?

**OWEN:** For disruption: run the task test, check pricing models, look for signals in the data, do customer research on their AI plans, and build scenarios including the exit multiple. Then label every finding as valuation, contract, closing condition or plan. Adjust the model, negotiate representations, indemnities and covenants, work with the insurer to keep exclusions narrow, structure earnouts carefully, retain key people, and plan carve-out separations. And hand it all over, formally, to the hundred-day team.
