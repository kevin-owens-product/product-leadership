### What a credit bureau does

**NORA:** Let's step back and explain what a credit bureau actually does, because I think most people only know them as the company that gives them a score.

**JAMES:** At its simplest, a credit bureau collects information about how people and businesses borrow and repay. Banks and other lenders share data about their customers' loans: how much, what type, whether payments are on time. The bureau combines that information from many lenders into one file per person or business. When a lender is deciding whether to lend, it asks the bureau for the applicant's report, and often for a score that summarises the risk.

**NORA:** Why would banks share their data with each other, effectively?

**JAMES:** Because everyone benefits. If you only know how a borrower has treated you, you might miss that they've just taken out five other loans elsewhere and are falling behind on all of them. Pooling information through a bureau reduces bad lending across the whole system. In many countries, sharing is required by regulation.

**NORA:** And then there's business information.

**JAMES:** Right. Bureaus also hold data on companies: who owns them, who runs them, how they pay their suppliers, whether they've defaulted. That's useful for lending to small businesses, and also for checking who you're dealing with. And that leads to a third area, which is identity and compliance. Before a bank opens an account, it has to verify who the customer is, and check whether they're on sanctions lists or are a politically exposed person.

**NORA:** Explain politically exposed person.

**JAMES:** A politically exposed person, often shortened to PEP, is someone in a prominent public role, or close to someone who is, like a minister, a senior official or their family. Banks are required to apply extra scrutiny to them, because of the higher risk of corruption and money laundering. Checking customers against those lists is part of what's called know your customer, or KYC, and anti-money laundering, or AML.

**NORA:** So Creditinfo's product lines are...

**JAMES:** In its own words, consumer and business credit bureaus, KYC and PEP data and business information, fraud and identity, and decisioning software. That last one is important. It's not just selling the data, it's selling the software that lenders use to make the decision, applying their own rules and scores to the bureau data.

**NORA:** Why does decisioning software matter so much?

**JAMES:** Because it moves you from being a data supplier to being part of the lender's workflow. A data feed can be swapped for another one. Software that runs your entire loan approval process is much harder to replace. And it's the natural home for AI models in lending.

### Why frontier markets

**NORA:** Let's talk about the markets. Why would a company focus on Uganda, Namibia, Latvia or the Caribbean rather than the big markets?

**JAMES:** A few reasons, in my view. First, the big markets are taken. In the United States and Britain, the three big bureaus are entrenched. Competing head on is very hard. Second, frontier markets are where credit is growing fastest. Digital lending through mobile phones has expanded access to credit in many African countries, and lenders need data to make those decisions safely.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, policy. Many governments and regional bodies want to widen access to credit, what's often called financial inclusion. Creditinfo's own mission is framed around that. When a country decides it needs a modern credit bureau, a specialist with experience setting them up in other countries has a real advantage.

**NORA:** And the licences are a moat.

**JAMES:** In our view, yes. Credit bureaus are licensed and regulated in each country. Getting a licence, building trust with the central bank and the lenders, and collecting years of repayment data takes a long time. That's very hard for a new entrant to replicate quickly.

**NORA:** But the data in those markets is thinner.

**JAMES:** Much thinner. That's the challenge and the opportunity. In a market where most adults have never had a formal loan, a traditional bureau file is empty for most people. That's why alternative data matters so much. Things like mobile money transactions, phone bill payments, and the records of fintech lenders. In June 2025 Creditinfo announced partnerships in Kenya with Kamoa and Little App, two local companies, around credit access and financial inclusion.

**NORA:** And that's the woman from the cold open.

**JAMES:** That's exactly her. If a bureau can include signals from the kinds of payments she makes every day, with her consent and within the rules, a lender can see her as a reliable person rather than a blank file.

**NORA:** Why is Kenya such a focus for this kind of thing?

**JAMES:** As general background, Kenya is widely known as one of the pioneers of mobile money, where people send, receive and store money through their phones, often without a bank account. That created a huge digital record of everyday financial behaviour, and a wave of digital lenders offering small loans through apps. It also created problems, like people taking several small loans from different apps at once. Good bureau data helps lenders see that, and helps responsible borrowers get better terms.

**NORA:** So the same technology that widens access can create new risks.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Which is why a strong, neutral credit information system matters more, not less, as digital lending grows.

### A loan decision, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through a loan decision. An invented example, not a real lender or person.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's call our stallholder Grace, and the lender a mid-sized digital lender in Kenya, call it Jua Credit. Grace downloads Jua's app on a Tuesday afternoon and applies for a loan of, say, a few hundred dollars' worth of Kenyan shillings, to be repaid over three months.

**NORA:** What happens first?

**JAMES:** Identity. The app asks for her national ID number and a selfie. In the background, Jua checks that the ID is real, that it matches the face, and that the phone number has been linked to her for a while, rather than being a new SIM card a fraudster just bought. Some of those checks might come from a bureau, some from a specialist identity provider.

**NORA:** Then credit.

**JAMES:** Then Jua's decisioning system asks the bureau for Grace's report. Let's say it shows one small loan from a microfinance institution four years ago, repaid on time, and nothing else. Traditionally, that's a thin file. Jua's rules might say: not enough history, decline, or approve a tiny amount at a high rate.

**NORA:** But if there's alternative data?

**JAMES:** Then the picture changes. Suppose, with Grace's consent, the bureau also has information from a fintech partner showing years of regular mobile payments and on-time bill payments. The score now reflects a reliable person with a stable small business. Jua's system approves a larger amount at a lower rate. All of this happens in a minute or two.

**NORA:** And then the loan is repaid, or not.

**JAMES:** Right, and that's the important part. Jua reports Grace's repayments back to the bureau each month. If she repays on time, her file gets richer, and the next lender sees a stronger borrower. That's the virtuous circle a credit bureau creates. Over time, more people move from invisible to visible, and lending becomes safer and cheaper.

**NORA:** Where's the AI in that story?

**JAMES:** Potentially in several places. In the identity and fraud checks, spotting patterns that suggest a fake application. In the scoring itself, using machine learning to make better predictions from thin and alternative data. And in the decisioning software, helping the lender set its rules and monitor how its loans perform. Creditinfo has a foot in all of those. The question is how much of the AI it owns.

**NORA:** Why is fraud growing so fast?

**JAMES:** As general background, because more of finance is digital. When you open an account on your phone without visiting a branch, the bank has to be sure you're who you say you are. Fraudsters use stolen identities, synthetic identities made by combining real and fake details, and automated attacks. And now AI tools make it easier to produce convincing fake documents and faces. So the defenders need better data and better models too.

**NORA:** And we haven't mentioned the other well-known names.

**JAMES:** Right, there are other significant players in bureau and business data, for example CRIF and Dun and Bradstreet, but we didn't research them for this episode, so I won't make any claims about them.

### Where Creditinfo is with AI

**NORA:** Let's take stock. Where is Creditinfo with AI?

**JAMES:** It's an interesting mix of early and absent. Early, because back in July 2019 the company described an automated machine learning platform for bureaus, a system to generate predictive models more quickly. That was ahead of many companies at the time. But we don't see it on current product pages, so its status today is unknown. Our guess is that it may be dormant or folded into other products.

**NORA:** What would an automated machine learning platform for a bureau actually do?

**JAMES:** In general terms, it speeds up building scorecards. A bureau or a lender wants a model that predicts the chance a borrower will default. Traditionally, a statistician builds that by hand over weeks: selecting variables, testing them, checking stability. An automated platform tries many combinations quickly and proposes the best-performing models, which experts then review. For a group running bureaus in many small markets, each needing its own models, that kind of tool could be very valuable.

**NORA:** So why might it have gone quiet?

**JAMES:** We don't know. It could have become an internal tool rather than a product. It could have been overtaken by newer methods. Or it may simply not be marketed any more. But if it still exists inside the company, it's a foundation worth dusting off. That's our speculation.

**NORA:** And now?

**JAMES:** Now, the AI-labelled items we found are mostly through partners. The Lucinity partnership is framed around AI workflows, but that's Lucinity's AI using Creditinfo's data. There's a thought leadership piece on the website about large language models in financial services, but we couldn't date it or read it. We didn't find a named AI product on the website, AI hires, an AI team or an AI budget.

**NORA:** So it has the data, but not the product.

**JAMES:** That's the cleanest way to say it. Creditinfo's data asset is real: bureau data across many countries, with licences that are hard to copy. But from the outside, it hasn't turned that asset into AI features it owns and sells.

**NORA:** What about governance?

**JAMES:** This is a highly regulated business, so governance matters a lot. Credit data is personal financial data. Every bureau operates under data protection rules and financial regulation. In February and March 2026 the Icelandic Data Protection Authority published decisions involving Creditinfo's credit reporting. We only saw the titles, not the content, so I don't know what they concluded. But it's a reminder that regulators watch this industry closely.

**NORA:** And AI-specific rules?

**JAMES:** As general background, the European Union's AI Act treats AI systems used to assess people's creditworthiness as high risk, which brings extra requirements around data quality, documentation, transparency and human oversight. Creditinfo operates in several EU countries, including Latvia and Estonia. So any AI scoring product in Europe would need to be built with those rules in mind from day one. We didn't find any AI-specific governance publicly, nor did we search for security certifications, so I can't say what's in place.

**NORA:** Is there a fairness angle, like we discussed with Resolution Economics?

**JAMES:** Very much. Credit scoring decides who gets access to money. If a model is biased against a particular group, it can deepen exclusion rather than reduce it. For a company whose mission is financial inclusion, getting fairness right isn't just compliance. It's the point of the business.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's list the challenges. What keeps leadership up at night, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, the product gap. As we said, a valuable data asset without owned AI products. Buyers of data businesses increasingly pay for the products and platforms built on the data, not just the data itself.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, partner dependence. Fraud capability comes through Equifax Kount and NOTO. AI compliance workflows come through Lucinity. That's a fast way to get capability, but it means sharing margin and not owning the intellectual property. That's our inference, since we don't know the commercial terms.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, integration. Uganda, Namibia, Estonia, Latvia, the CEMAC regional bureau, eleven Caribbean countries through EveryData. Each has its own systems, its own regulations, its own data formats and often its own language. Building one technology platform that serves all of them is a huge job, and it's a precondition for rolling out AI products across the group.

**NORA:** Is there a people side to that integration?

**JAMES:** A big one. Each acquired bureau has its own team, its own culture and its own relationships with local regulators and lenders. Those relationships are valuable and fragile. Moving everyone onto a common platform, common processes and common products, without losing local trust or key people, is a delicate balance. The companies that do it well usually keep strong local leadership for relationships, while centralising the technology and product work.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, regulation. Data protection authorities, central banks, financial regulators and, in Europe, the AI Act. Every country has its own rules, and they're tightening. A company operating in dozens of markets has to manage a very complex compliance map.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, timing. At nearly six years into the hold, the AI story needs to be proven, not promised, before a sale process. That puts pressure on the next twelve to eighteen months.

**NORA:** Is there a talent challenge as well?

**JAMES:** I'd expect so. Building credit models and AI products requires data scientists and machine learning engineers who understand both the technology and the regulation. Those people are in demand everywhere. A group spread across many smaller markets may find it harder to attract them than a global giant would. That's our inference.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's flip to opportunities. If you were on the board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** The first is AI-assisted KYC, PEP and fraud alert triage, built on Creditinfo's own local data and sold to bank and microfinance clients as an API, which is a way for software systems to connect directly.

**NORA:** Explain alert triage.

**JAMES:** When a bank screens customers and transactions against watchlists and fraud rules, it gets alerts. Huge numbers of them, and most are false positives. A customer whose name is similar to a politically exposed person, a payment that looks unusual but is innocent. Compliance analysts have to review each one. That's slow and expensive. AI can help rank the alerts, explain why each was raised, pull the relevant data together and suggest whether it's likely to be real. The analyst still decides, but much faster.

**NORA:** And why should Creditinfo do it rather than Lucinity?

**JAMES:** Because Creditinfo has the local data, the business registries, the PEP lists and the credit files in its markets. Clients already consume that data in AI workflows, as the Lucinity partnership shows. If Creditinfo owns the triage layer, at least for its own markets, it captures margin that currently flows to partners. That's our inference about the economics.

**NORA:** Wouldn't that annoy Lucinity, a partner?

**JAMES:** Possibly, and it's a real tension. One way through, in my view, is to focus on the markets and customer types where Creditinfo is strongest, for example smaller banks and microfinance institutions in its bureau countries, which may never buy a full compliance platform. Another is to make the triage capability available inside partners' platforms too, as a premium data product. Either way, the aim is to sell an answer, not just raw data.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, own more of the fraud layer. Pair the Equifax Kount platform with local bureau data and an in-house scoring layer, so Creditinfo adds its own value rather than just reselling.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, cross-selling across the new footprint. Take EveryData's consumer bureau and advisory products and combine them with Creditinfo's business information and decisioning products across the Caribbean. And fourth, geographic expansion. The company has said it's exploring Africa and the Middle East for the Equifax partnership, and it already has a presence in the Gulf.

**NORA:** And more acquisitions?

**JAMES:** Fifth, more bureau roll-ups where the global players are stepping back from smaller markets. The pattern from 2023 to 2026 suggests that's a deliberate strategy. Sixth, alternative data scoring for thin-file borrowers, through mobile money and fintech partners like the Kenyan ones. And seventh, pricing. Moving from charging per report to usage-based pricing through APIs, which fits how digital lenders actually work. We didn't find any pricing data, so that's our suggestion.

**NORA:** What about the decisioning software? Is there an AI angle there?

**JAMES:** A big one, in my view. Imagine a lender's credit manager asking the decisioning system, in plain language: "Why did approvals in the northern region fall last month?" Or, "What happens to our default rate if we raise the approval threshold for first-time borrowers?" A generative AI analyst built into the decisioning software, working on the lender's own data and the bureau data, could answer those questions in minutes. For small lenders without big analytics teams, that's very valuable.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** One AI bet for the next eighteen months. What is it?

**JAMES:** AI-assisted KYC, PEP and fraud alert triage on Creditinfo's local data, delivered as an API. The value is analyst hours saved per alert for clients, plus revenue from a new product. It builds directly on data clients already use, and it moves Creditinfo up from data feed to workflow.

**NORA:** Let's walk through it. An invented bank.

**JAMES:** Sure. Picture a mid-sized bank in Latvia, call it Daugava Bank. Its compliance team of eight analysts reviews hundreds of screening alerts a day. Most are false positives: a customer called Jānis Bērziņš whose name partly matches a politically exposed person's relative, a company whose director shares a name with someone on a sanctions list.

**NORA:** And today each alert means what?

**JAMES:** Today an analyst opens the alert, looks up the customer, searches the business registry, checks the watchlist entry, compares dates of birth and addresses, and writes a short note explaining why it's a false positive or why it needs escalation. Maybe ten or fifteen minutes each. Multiply by hundreds a day.

**NORA:** And with the triage tool?

**JAMES:** The alert arrives already enriched. The tool has pulled Creditinfo's data on the customer and the watchlist entry, compared the identifying details, and written a short draft explanation: "Name match only. Date of birth differs by twenty-two years. Customer's registered address and business history are unrelated to the listed person. Suggested: low risk, close." The analyst reads it, checks the evidence, and closes it in a minute. The tool ranks the genuinely suspicious alerts at the top of the queue, so the team spends its time where it matters.

**NORA:** And the regulator?

**JAMES:** The regulator needs to see that a human made the decision, that the reasoning is recorded, and that the system is tested and monitored. So every suggestion has to be explainable and logged, and the bank needs to be able to show how often the tool's suggestions were right. That's not a burden to bolt on later. It's part of the product.

**NORA:** Now the hundred days.

**JAMES:** First thirty days: choose the market and the partners. Pick one or two markets where Creditinfo has rich local data and the regulation is well understood, perhaps Latvia, where it now owns the bureau outright. Recruit three to five design partner banks or microfinance institutions with real alert volumes. Agree the success measures: time per alert, false positives correctly closed, genuine risks caught. Settle the data rules, including how the tool complies with data protection law and, for credit-related uses, the AI Act.

**NORA:** Then?

**JAMES:** Days thirty to sixty: build and test on history. Take a sample of past alerts from the design partners, where the final decisions are known. Run the tool on them. Where does it agree with the analysts? Where does it disagree, and who was right? Tune it, and set the threshold for what it can suggest closing.

**NORA:** And the last stretch?

**JAMES:** Days sixty to a hundred: shadow mode. On live alerts, the tool makes suggestions alongside the analysts' normal work, without changing the process. Measure the time it would save and the accuracy. At day one hundred, decide on a launch, a price per alert or a subscription, and the next markets.

**NORA:** And who owns it?

**JAMES:** A product leader with a clear mandate across data, technology and commercial, reporting close to the chief executive. With his background running a large bureau business, the chief executive is well placed to sponsor it. But someone has to own it day to day.

**NORA:** And this needs the integration work you mentioned.

**JAMES:** It does, but it doesn't have to wait for all of it. Starting in one or two markets lets the team prove the product while the wider platform work continues. Then each newly integrated market becomes a new place to sell it.

### The lender's analyst, step by step

**NORA:** You mentioned a generative AI analyst inside the decisioning software. Let's walk through that too, because I think it shows a different side of the opportunity. Another invented example.

**JAMES:** Sure. Picture a microfinance institution in Uganda, call it Kyaka Finance. It lends to small farmers and traders. It has a credit manager, call her Ruth, and a tiny analytics team of one person, who is also responsible for the monthly reports to the board and the regulator.

**NORA:** And what's Ruth's problem?

**JAMES:** Arrears are rising in one region, and she doesn't know why. Today, she asks her one analyst to pull the numbers. He exports data from the decisioning system and the loan book into spreadsheets, spends two days cutting it by product, branch, loan size and borrower type, and comes back with a few charts. By then the board meeting is close, and there's no time to test any ideas.

**NORA:** And with the AI analyst?

**JAMES:** Ruth types a question into the decisioning software. "Why are arrears rising in the western region this quarter?" The assistant looks at Kyaka's own loan data and the bureau data, within the rules on what it's allowed to see. It comes back with a short answer and charts. Most of the increase is in loans to first-time borrowers made in two branches. Those borrowers were approved at a higher average amount than in other branches. And bureau data shows that a larger share of them had other active loans with digital lenders at the time.

**NORA:** So it's spotted that people were borrowing from several places at once.

**JAMES:** Exactly, which is a classic risk in fast-growing digital lending markets. Ruth then asks a follow-up. "What would have happened to approvals and arrears if we'd capped first-time loans at the regional average?" The assistant runs a simple scenario on historic data and shows the trade-off: fewer loans approved, lower arrears. Ruth takes that to the board, with her own judgment on top.

**NORA:** And where's the human in that?

**JAMES:** Everywhere that matters. Ruth decides what to ask and what to do. The assistant does the slicing and the first draft of the analysis. Her one analyst stops spending days on spreadsheets and spends his time checking the findings and thinking about policy. For a small institution, that's like adding an analytics team it could never afford to hire.

**NORA:** What does it need to work?

**JAMES:** Three things, in my view. Clean, consistent data, which brings us back to the integration challenge. Clear rules on what the assistant can access, especially bureau data about people who aren't Kyaka's customers. And explanations that a credit manager can check, so she isn't asked to trust a black box. If those are in place, it's a product many small lenders would pay for, and it's one that only a company with both bureau data and decisioning software can offer.

**NORA:** So it plays to Creditinfo's unusual combination.

**JAMES:** That's the point. A pure AI company doesn't have the bureau data. A pure bureau doesn't have the decisioning workflow. Creditinfo has both, at least in some markets. That's a real right to win, if it builds the product.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Creditinfo?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, where the giants retreat, specialists can win. Creditinfo has built a business in markets that the global players found too small or too complex. Many industries have equivalent corners.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, data is not the same as a product. Owning valuable data is a great start, but in the AI era, value increasingly flows to whoever owns the workflow where decisions are made. If you're a data company, ask where your data is used, and whether you could own that step.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, partnerships are a fast start, not a finish. Partnering with Equifax, Lucinity and NOTO brought capabilities quickly. But at some point, a company has to decide which of those capabilities it wants to own, because that's where the margin and the long-term value sit.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, build governance into the product from the start, especially in regulated markets. Explainability, logging, human oversight and fairness testing are much easier to design in than to retrofit, and in credit and compliance, they're what customers and regulators will ask for first.

**NORA:** I'll add one from the outside. Purpose can be a strategy.

**JAMES:** I agree. Creditinfo's mission is financial inclusion. That's not just a nice line. It's why governments choose it to run regional bureaus, and it points straight at the right AI products: better scoring for people who have been invisible to lenders.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-native KYC and decisioning platforms taking the customer relationship and reducing the bureau to a commodity data feed. That's our inference. Two, global bureaus with bigger AI budgets competing in the same markets. Three, data protection rulings and the EU AI Act's high-risk rules restricting how credit models can be built and used. Four, open banking and alternative data reducing lenders' reliance on traditional bureau files.

**NORA:** Explain open banking quickly.

**JAMES:** Open banking lets customers share their bank account data directly with other providers, with consent, through secure connections. A lender could look at your actual income and spending instead of only your credit history. That's a competitor to bureau data in some uses, and also an opportunity, if a bureau can combine the two.

**NORA:** And for the owner?

**JAMES:** Five, exit timing. Nearly six years into the hold, a buyer will want proof that the data can become AI-era products and revenue. And six, integration risk across a growing number of countries, each with its own systems and regulators.

**NORA:** Are there risks that come simply from operating in frontier markets?

**JAMES:** Yes, as general background. Currency swings can change what revenue earned in local currency is worth in euros or dollars. Political change can alter how regulators treat credit bureaus, or who wins the next tender for a national bureau. And economic shocks can raise default rates sharply, which hurts lenders and can squeeze their spending on data and software. A group spread across many such markets diversifies those risks, but it can't eliminate them. We didn't find any specific issues of this kind for Creditinfo. It's simply part of the territory.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch for?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, a named AI product launch from Creditinfo itself, rather than through a partner. Second, how EveryData is integrated across the Caribbean. Third, whether the Equifax partnership expands to Africa and the Middle East. And fourth, any more bureau acquisitions from the global players.

**NORA:** And a bonus?

**JAMES:** Any AI or technology leadership hire. That would be a strong signal that the company is moving from partner-led to owned AI.
