### The problem Club Champion solves

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer. Who walks into a Club Champion studio?

**JAMES:** Our read, and this is general knowledge of the market rather than company data, is a keen amateur golfer who plays regularly, cares about improving, and is willing to spend meaningfully on equipment. Not usually a complete beginner. Someone who has a sense that their clubs might be holding them back, or who is about to buy new clubs anyway and wants to get it right.

**NORA:** What's the actual problem, though? Why not just buy what the reviews say is the best driver this year?

**JAMES:** Because "best" depends on the golfer. Let me give you the basic physics, as plainly as I can. When you hit a golf ball, a few things decide where it goes. How fast the ball leaves the club. The angle it launches at. How much backspin it has. And whether the clubface was pointing where you meant it to at impact. Change the shaft, and you can change how the club behaves during your swing, which changes those numbers.

**NORA:** So a shaft that's too stiff for me...

**JAMES:** Might mean you don't load it properly and you launch the ball too low. A shaft that's too soft might mean the face arrives at impact in a different place each time. Too much spin and the ball balloons up and falls short. Too little and it can fall out of the sky. A head with the wrong weighting might make your miss worse. And then length and lie angle affect how you stand and where the ball tends to go.

**NORA:** And no golfer can figure that out on their own.

**JAMES:** Not easily, and that's the core problem. You need measurement and you need expertise. Measurement comes from a launch monitor, a device that tracks the ball and club and reports dozens of numbers. Club Champion's own technology page talks about its use of TrackMan, one of the best-known launch monitor makers. Expertise comes from the fitter, who interprets the numbers, watches your swing, and knows which combination to try next.

**NORA:** And then there's the shopping problem.

**JAMES:** Yes. Even if you knew your ideal spec, you'd struggle to buy it. Big brands offer custom options, but each only within its own catalogue. If your best driver is one brand's head with another company's shaft, you need someone who can build that. That's what the assembly facility does.

**NORA:** So the customer problem is really three things. Find out what works for me, across all brands, and then actually get it built.

**JAMES:** That's a clean summary. And there's a fourth thing underneath, which is confidence. A golfer spending a lot on clubs wants to know they're not wasting money. A fitting with data is partly about reassurance. You can see on the screen that the new combination gave you more distance and a tighter spread.

**NORA:** You said "spending a lot". Is price an issue?

**JAMES:** Clearly enough that Club Champion has a blog post addressing it directly, titled along the lines of "Is Club Champion too expensive? The real cost of custom fitting." When a company publishes a rebuttal to an objection, it's usually because salespeople hear that objection a lot. That's our inference, but I think it's a safe one. And there's an independent price comparison site that compares Club Champion with independent fitters, which we didn't read in detail, but its existence tells you consumers are shopping on price.

### Inside a fitting

**NORA:** Let's go inside a studio. What happens in a fitting, roughly?

**JAMES:** I'll describe the general shape of a modern fitting, based on how the industry broadly works, and then we'll get to what's specific to Club Champion and its AI. It usually starts with a conversation. What clubs do you play now, what's your handicap, what's your typical miss, what do you want to improve. Then you warm up and hit your current clubs, so the fitter gets a baseline.

**NORA:** On the launch monitor.

**JAMES:** Yes. Every shot gets measured. Club speed, ball speed, launch angle, spin rate, carry distance, where it lands relative to the target. After a dozen or so shots, patterns appear. Maybe you launch the ball low with too much spin. Maybe your strikes are scattered across the face.

**NORA:** Then the fitter starts swapping things.

**JAMES:** Right. The fitter picks a head, tries a few shafts, watches the numbers change, narrows down, then tries other heads. A good fitter is part scientist and part coach. They're reading the data, but also reading you. Are you getting tired? Are you swinging differently because you're excited about the new shiny club? Did you just hit one great shot that's distorting the average?

**NORA:** And the combinations are enormous.

**JAMES:** That's the key point. If you have dozens of heads and hundreds of shafts, plus options for length, loft, lie and grips, the possibilities run into the tens of thousands for each club type. A figure that has circulated in coverage of Club Champion is more than sixty-five thousand head and shaft combinations, but we couldn't verify it, so take it as indicative. No human can test more than a tiny fraction. So fitting is really a search problem. Where in that enormous space should you even start looking?

**NORA:** Which is where the Co-Pilot comes in, I'm guessing.

**JAMES:** Exactly. And once the fitting is done, the fitter puts together a recommended specification, often for the whole bag, and gives the golfer a price. If the golfer says yes, the order goes to assembly. The clubs are built and shipped back.

**NORA:** And what does the fitting itself cost the golfer?

**JAMES:** We're not going to quote a fee, because we didn't verify current pricing and it can vary by fitting type. What I'd say is that, in this industry generally, fitters handle the fee in different ways. Some charge for the fitting and credit it against a purchase, some charge it separately, some give it away. That's part of the competitive picture, because a free fitting at a big retailer and a paid fitting at a specialist feel very different to a golfer deciding where to go.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** Let's talk market. How big is custom club fitting?

**JAMES:** I'm going to disappoint you. We didn't find a reliable size figure. There are market research firms that publish reports on golf equipment and even on golf club fitting services specifically, but we didn't open them and I don't want to quote a number we can't stand behind. What I can give you is the shape of the market.

**NORA:** Go on.

**JAMES:** Picture four kinds of fitter. First, the specialist chains, like Club Champion and True Spec Golf, which are multi-brand and fitting-first. Second, the big-box retailers, the best known being Golf Galaxy, which is part of Dick's Sporting Goods and has opened what it calls performance centres with fitting inside. Third, the brands themselves, like TaylorMade's Kingdom fitting experience, which golfers on enthusiast forums compare directly with Club Champion. And fourth, thousands of independent fitters and club pros, who are fragmented, local and often cheaper.

**NORA:** Let's take them one at a time. True Spec.

**JAMES:** True Spec Golf is probably the closest like-for-like competitor in the United States. Also a custom fitter. In 2026 it ran a free summer fitting promotion, according to its own blog in August, and in September it announced a fitting partnership with Arizona State University's men's golf team. We couldn't find who owns True Spec. The free fitting promotion is worth noticing, because it attacks the fee directly.

**NORA:** Golf Galaxy.

**JAMES:** Golf Galaxy's advantage is that it's attached to one of the biggest sporting goods retailers in the country. Fitting is bundled with a purchase, the stores are everywhere, and Dick's has the scale to invest. Its disadvantage, from a specialist's point of view, is that it's a retailer first. The fitting may be good, but it lives inside a store that sells a lot of other things.

**NORA:** And the brands.

**JAMES:** This is the interesting one. Brands have every reason to want to own fitting. If a golfer is fitted inside a brand's own facility, they'll almost certainly buy that brand. And the brand gets the data about the golfer, which is valuable for designing future products. TaylorMade's Kingdom is one example. And Wilson has launched an AI fitting platform it calls Wilson Fit AI, with a next-generation version covered by the golf retail trade press. We didn't verify the dates or details, but the direction is clear.

**NORA:** So the brands are building AI fitters too.

**JAMES:** Yes. And then there's a fifth category that didn't exist a few years ago, which is free online AI fitting. There's a site called thegolfshop dot AI that offers a free AI club and ball fitting tool, and companies like Smart Golf Fitting offering fitting software. We don't know much about either of them. But the idea is that you answer questions, maybe enter some numbers, and get a recommendation without ever going into a studio.

**NORA:** Is that a real threat? It's not the same as hitting balls on a launch monitor.

**JAMES:** It isn't the same, and I'd be sceptical that an online quiz matches a full fitting. But it doesn't have to match it. It just has to be good enough for a lot of golfers, and free. That's the classic shape of disruption. The question for Club Champion is not "is the free tool as good as us", it's "how many of our potential customers will decide good enough is fine".

**NORA:** So where does Club Champion sit?

**JAMES:** In our view, it's trying to be the premium, independent expert. More choice than a brand, more depth than a big-box store, more consistent than a local independent. That's a defensible position, but it's squeezed from several sides at once. Brands want the data, retailers want the sale, free tools want the recommendation, and independents compete on price.

**NORA:** Any trends that cut across all of that?

**JAMES:** A few. Fitting is increasingly bundled with launches and partner programs, so it becomes a distribution channel, not just a service. Launch monitor and simulator data is becoming the raw material for AI recommendations. And trade-in programs are blurring the line between new and used clubs. Club Champion is in all three of those, which we'll talk about next.

### AI at Club Champion today

**NORA:** Let's do the honest stock-take. What do we actually know about Club Champion's AI?

**JAMES:** We know it launched something called the AI Fitter Co-Pilot in 2023, and that it was covered in golf trade press at the time. We know Club Champion has a technology page describing its fitting technology, including TrackMan launch monitors. And there's a company video about using the Co-Pilot across the whole bag. Beyond that, honestly, a lot of the detail we've seen comes from coverage we only saw at headline level.

**NORA:** What does a co-pilot do, in principle?

**JAMES:** The name is the clue. It doesn't replace the fitter, it assists. Our understanding is that it takes the golfer's data, things like swing speed and ball flight numbers, and suggests which head and shaft combinations are most likely to suit them. Instead of the fitter starting from instinct, they start from a shortlist. Claims that have circulated say its component suggestions are accurate ninety-five to one hundred percent of the time, but that's a self-reported claim with no third-party check that we found, and it's not obvious what "accurate" means in this context.

**NORA:** What would it mean?

**JAMES:** Probably something like "the combination the golfer ended up choosing was on the Co-Pilot's shortlist". That's a useful measure, but notice it's partly circular. If the fitter starts from the shortlist, the golfer is more likely to end up choosing from it. A tougher test would be: did golfers fitted with the Co-Pilot hit the ball better six months later than golfers fitted without it? We haven't seen that kind of evidence, and to be fair, almost nobody in the industry publishes it.

**NORA:** And the data behind it?

**JAMES:** In principle, a fitting chain with around a hundred studios generates a lot of data. Every fitting is a set of measured shots with known equipment. Over years, that's a substantial record of which combinations worked for which kinds of golfers. That's the kind of data asset AI thrives on. But two cautions. First, the launch monitor data is captured with a third party's hardware, TrackMan's, so the question of who controls what isn't entirely simple. Second, we found no public security certifications or AI governance statement, which doesn't mean they don't exist, just that they're not visible.

**NORA:** Any sign of an AI team?

**JAMES:** We didn't find any public AI or data hires. We've seen a name mentioned as a possible outside build partner, but we couldn't verify it, so I'll leave it out. Our guess is that, like many mid-sized consumer businesses, Club Champion built the Co-Pilot with outside help and a small internal team. That's a perfectly sensible model, but it raises the question of how fast the product keeps improving after launch.

**NORA:** So the headline is: one named AI product, in the core workflow, since 2023.

**JAMES:** That's fair. And let me put that in perspective, because across this series we've seen lots of companies with no AI product at all. Having an AI tool inside the moment of value, the fitting itself, is genuinely ahead of many peers. The open question is the one we raised at the top. It's three years old. The brands have now built their own. Is it still a differentiator?

### Fitters and the craft

**NORA:** I want to talk about the people, because this business seems to depend completely on the fitter.

**JAMES:** It does. The fitter is the product, in a sense. A golfer remembers the person who spent ninety minutes with them, explained what the numbers meant and found them twenty extra yards. That relationship is what brings people back and what drives word of mouth.

**NORA:** So how does a fitter feel about an AI co-pilot?

**JAMES:** It depends hugely on how it's introduced. There are two stories a fitter could tell themselves. One is: this tool makes me faster and lets me spend more time on the bits that need a human, like reading the golfer, explaining, coaching. The other is: this tool is being brought in so the company can hire less experienced fitters and pay them less. Both stories are plausible from the outside, and which one the fitters believe will decide whether they actually use it.

**NORA:** Which is right, in your view?

**JAMES:** Honestly, probably a bit of both, and that's not necessarily bad. Running a hundred studios means hiring and training a lot of fitters. Great fitters are scarce. A co-pilot that helps a newer fitter perform closer to an expert's level is valuable for consistency. Our view is that the best framing is "the AI raises the floor, and the fitter raises the ceiling".

**NORA:** I like that. What does raising the ceiling look like?

**JAMES:** Things software is bad at. Noticing that a golfer is nervous and swinging tense. Noticing that they want to hear their old clubs were fine, or that they actually want permission to buy the expensive driver. Explaining why a heavier shaft feels wrong at first but will help. Knowing when to stop the session because the golfer is tired and the data is getting worse. That's craft, and it's what a premium price pays for.

**NORA:** And the training side?

**JAMES:** This is where I think AI could quietly do a lot. Every fitting generates data on what the fitter tried and what worked. Over time you could show a new fitter how experienced fitters approached similar golfers. You could flag sessions where the fitter's final choice differed from the Co-Pilot's suggestion and learn from those, both to improve the tool and to coach the fitter. That's speculative on our part, but it's a natural next step.

**NORA:** What about the leadership churn you mentioned?

**JAMES:** It matters for the same reason. A fitting philosophy is culture. The founder designed the process. When leadership changes several times, there's a risk the process drifts, or that different studios develop different habits. The integration of TXG and Golf Principles adds another layer, because those businesses had their own fitters, their own methods and their own loyal customers. Bringing all of that under one process while keeping what made each special is real management work.

### A fitting, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through a fitting, before and with the Co-Pilot. Invented golfer, not a real customer.

**JAMES:** Let's call him Marcus. Fifty-two, plays most weekends, a mid-teens handicap. He's been playing the same irons for eight years and his driver for five. His typical miss is a high slice with the driver, and he feels his irons don't go as far as they used to. He books a full-bag fitting.

**NORA:** Old way first.

**JAMES:** In a traditional fitting, Marcus warms up, hits his own clubs, and the fitter looks at the numbers. The fitter sees his driver swing speed, sees high spin and a face that's open at impact. Based on experience, the fitter thinks: let's try a couple of heads that help close the face, and a few shafts in a certain weight and flex range. They try one, then another. Some work, some don't. They narrow down. Then they do the same with irons, wedges, putter. An expert fitter will get somewhere good. A less experienced one might go down a few blind alleys, or might lean on the combinations they personally like.

**NORA:** And it takes time.

**JAMES:** It does. And Marcus gets tired. By shot one hundred and fifty, his swing isn't the same as at shot twenty. So the data gets noisier just as the decisions get finer.

**NORA:** Now with the Co-Pilot.

**JAMES:** Marcus warms up, hits his own clubs, and the numbers flow in. The Co-Pilot looks at his profile against what the company has learned from many past fittings, and suggests a shortlist. Say, three driver heads and five shafts most likely to reduce his spin and help square the face. The fitter looks at the list, maybe swaps one option because of something they noticed about Marcus's swing, and starts testing from a much better place.

**NORA:** So fewer blind alleys.

**JAMES:** Fewer blind alleys, and more of Marcus's fresh swings spent on the combinations that matter. When the fitter gets to irons, the Co-Pilot might suggest a shaft profile consistent with what worked in his driver, saving more time. At the end, the fitter has a whole-bag recommendation, and can show Marcus a comparison: here's your old driver, here's the new combination, here's the difference in carry distance and dispersion.

**NORA:** Where does the fitter earn their money in that version?

**JAMES:** In three places. Choosing what to test from the shortlist, using judgment the software doesn't have. Reading Marcus, including his fatigue, his confidence and his budget. And explaining. Marcus is about to spend a lot of money. He wants someone he trusts to tell him why. The fitter can say: the software suggested these, I tried this one because of your tempo, and here's what we saw.

**NORA:** Let me push back. Couldn't Marcus have used a free online tool and got a similar answer?

**JAMES:** For a first pass, maybe. An online tool could probably say "you might want a lower-spin driver head and a slightly heavier shaft". But it couldn't measure Marcus's actual swing, couldn't let him feel the difference, couldn't fine-tune lie angles based on real strikes, and couldn't build the club. The honest pitch for Club Champion is that the free tool gives you a direction, and the fitting gives you an answer you can hit.

**NORA:** Now give me a second walk-through. Something that isn't the classic fitting.

**JAMES:** Let's take Priya. She's thirty-four, newer to golf, plays maybe twice a month, and she's been using a hand-me-down set from her father. She's interested but nervous about spending, and she's heard fittings are expensive. Today she might not come in at all. She'd read about free fittings at a big-box store, or try the online tool, or just buy a box set.

**NORA:** What could Club Champion do for Priya?

**JAMES:** This is where an opportunity sits, in our view. Imagine a shorter, cheaper, AI-led fitting. Priya books a half-hour slot, hits a handful of shots with a few clubs, and the Co-Pilot does more of the work on the shortlist. A fitter is still there, but the session is quicker and priced to match. She leaves with a recommendation for a few clubs, maybe including some quality used clubs taken in through a trade-in program, rather than a whole new set. She gets value, Club Champion gets a new customer it can grow with.

**NORA:** So the AI lets you serve a cheaper customer profitably.

**JAMES:** That's the idea. Whether Club Champion does anything like this, we don't know. But it's a natural answer to the price objection and to the free tools.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about challenges. What would you worry about if you were running this business?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. The first is the brands. If the big club makers build good AI fitting into their own facilities and retail partners, and use it to pull golfers toward their own products, an independent fitter's role as neutral expert gets harder to sustain. And the brands have huge product data, because they design the heads and know their own engineering.

**NORA:** But brands also need retailers. They can't fit everybody themselves.

**JAMES:** True, and that's the counterweight. Club Champion's partnership news suggests brands still value it as a channel. In May 2026, Club Champion was named among a select group of global partners to fit and carry McLaren Golf's debut club line. A new brand wants credible fitters to launch through. So brands are both competitors and partners. That's a delicate balance.

**NORA:** Second challenge?

**JAMES:** Second, free and cheap AI. We've covered that. If a good-enough recommendation becomes free, the value of a paid fitting must come from the parts AI can't easily copy: measurement, touch, the human and the build.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, price. Golf equipment is discretionary spending. When people feel less well-off, they delay new clubs. We didn't find data on how Club Champion's sales move with the economy, so this is general reasoning, but a premium, high-ticket purchase is exposed to consumer confidence. And the existence of that pricing blog tells you the objection is already real in good times.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, keeping the AI fresh. A co-pilot launched in 2023 is a long time ago in AI terms. Models have changed, expectations have changed. If it was built with an outside partner and not invested in since, it could start to feel dated, especially next to new brand-built tools. Nobody outside knows how much it's been updated, and we couldn't find public detail.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Operational complexity. Around a hundred studios, operations in three countries, a single central assembly site, components from many brands. That's a lot of inventory and logistics. The more brands and combinations you offer, which is your selling point, the harder it is to keep the right heads and shafts in stock at the right time. That's a challenge, and as we'll see, also an opportunity.

**NORA:** You haven't mentioned data and trust.

**JAMES:** It belongs on the list. Golfers aren't handing over medical records, but they are sharing personal data, payment data and swing data. The higher-stakes issue is commercial. If Club Champion has years of data on which components work for which golfers, brands would love that data. How it's governed, and whether golfers know how it's used, matters. We found no public statement on AI governance or security certifications.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Okay, opportunities. If you were on the board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** The first is to extend the Co-Pilot beyond the studio. Remote and pre-visit fitting. A golfer could start online, share some information or launch monitor data if they have their own device, and get a preliminary recommendation. That turns the free-tool threat into a funnel. You're offering the same convenience, but routing serious golfers to a studio for the full experience.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, demand forecasting for components. This is unglamorous but potentially very valuable. With around a hundred studios feeding one assembly facility, knowing which heads and shafts will be in demand, by week and by region, helps you avoid tying up cash in the wrong stock and avoid telling golfers they'll have to wait. The fitting data itself is a leading indicator. If the Co-Pilot is suggesting a particular shaft more often this month, that's a signal for purchasing.

**NORA:** That's clever. The recommendation engine as a demand forecast.

**JAMES:** In our view that's one of the more distinctive ideas here, because few retailers have such a direct, early signal of what customers are about to buy. Third, tiered fitting packages. A quick AI-led fit, a standard fit, and a full-bag fit with a senior fitter. That directly addresses the price objection and the free competition, and it lets the company serve the Priyas as well as the Marcuses.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, trade-ins. Club Champion has a partnership with a company called Next Round, which handles club trade-ins. In February 2026 they offered a fifty percent bonus on trade-in value for the month. In August 2026, there was an announcement that Next Round was expanding the partnership to complete a global rollout. We saw both at headline level. The opportunity, in our view, is to use trade-ins to lower the effective price of new clubs, and possibly to bring used clubs into fittings for value-conscious golfers.

**NORA:** Fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, launch fitting with brands. The McLaren deal is a template. New brands, and existing brands launching new lines, need credible fitting partners. Club Champion could build a repeatable launch program, and AI could help here too, by quickly learning how a new head performs across golfer types and feeding that into the Co-Pilot.

**NORA:** Any others?

**JAMES:** A couple. There's a 2026 PGA Partnership Program page on Club Champion's site, which we didn't read in detail but which suggests a channel through club professionals. Procurement scale across the American, Canadian and British operations is a margin lever. And the UK and Canada operations could be a test bed. Smaller networks, slightly different golfers, a good place to try something new before rolling it out across a hundred American studios. That last one is our suggestion, not anything the company has said.

**NORA:** What about personalised marketing? You know a lot about each customer.

**JAMES:** Yes, and I'd add it. If you know what was fitted, when, and how the golfer's swing speed has changed, you know when they're likely due for new wedges or a driver refresh. That's much more useful than generic promotions. It has to be done respectfully and with consent, but it's a classic retail AI use case with an unusually rich data set behind it.

### The best AI bet

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** I'd pair two things that share the same engine. AI-led remote fitting at the front, and component demand forecasting at the back. The Co-Pilot already works inside the core fitting step, so the marginal cost of extending it outward to golfers, and inward to purchasing, is relatively low. The value levers are fitter hours per fitting, new customer acquisition, and inventory cost across around a hundred studios and one assembly site. We can't size it from public data, but those are the right levers.

**NORA:** How would you run it? First hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: measure honestly. Pull the data on how fitters use the Co-Pilot today. How often do they follow its suggestions, how often do they override, and what happened when they did? Talk to fitters in a range of studios. And set a clear owner, one person with authority across fitting operations, technology and purchasing.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** Days thirty to sixty: two pilots. On the customer side, launch a simple pre-visit tool in one region, perhaps the UK or Canada, where golfers can get a preliminary recommendation and book a shorter fitting. Measure how many convert to a studio visit and a purchase. On the back end, build a simple forecast of component demand from Co-Pilot suggestions and recent fittings, and run it alongside the current purchasing process without acting on it yet. Shadow mode.

**NORA:** And days sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Compare. Did the pre-visit tool bring in new golfers, or just cannibalise people who would have booked anyway? Was the forecast more accurate than the current method? If yes, expand. If no, learn why. And in parallel, refresh the Co-Pilot itself. After three years, there will be newer modelling techniques and a much bigger data set to retrain on. Ship an updated version and tell golfers about it.

**NORA:** What would success look like at day one hundred?

**JAMES:** A clear answer, backed by data, to three questions. Does the Co-Pilot make fittings faster or better, and by how much? Does a remote front door bring new golfers in? And can the fitting data reduce inventory without hurting availability? If you can answer those, you have an AI story that's about the business, not about a feature.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** Fitter training and quality assurance using the fitting data. Less glamorous, but it protects the thing that matters most, which is a consistent, expert experience in every studio.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Club Champion?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, put AI where the value is created. Club Champion didn't start with a chatbot on its website. It put AI into the fitting, the moment the customer is paying for. Many businesses do the opposite, starting with peripheral AI, because it's easier. Starting at the core is harder but makes the AI matter.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, being first isn't a moat on its own. Launching early gave Club Champion a head start and a story. But others caught up. The lesson is that AI products need continuous investment, like any product. A three-year-old model in a fast-moving field is a depreciating asset unless you keep improving it.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, AI should raise the floor without flattening the ceiling. In expert-service businesses, the risk of AI is that management sees it as a way to replace expertise, and customers then feel the service has become generic. The better approach is to use AI to make every practitioner more consistent, while protecting and celebrating the human craft that justifies a premium.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, your recommendation engine is also a planning engine. Whenever your AI is suggesting what customers should buy, it's also telling you what you're about to sell. Connecting front-of-house AI to back-office planning is one of the most underused ideas I see. It applies to retailers, distributors, even service firms.

**NORA:** And a lesson about acquisitions?

**JAMES:** Yes, a fifth. Buying a beloved brand like TXG is quick, merging it is slow. Nineteen months from deal to merged brand, by our reading of the dates. If you're buying companies with loyal customers, plan for the brand transition as seriously as the systems integration, because customers' attachment to the old name is a real asset you can lose.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's run through the risks.

**JAMES:** One, brand-owned AI fitting pulling golfers and data toward the manufacturers. Two, free AI tools commoditising the basic recommendation. Three, big-box retailers offering fitting free with a purchase. Four, golf demand being cyclical and discretionary, though we found no specific data on that for Club Champion. Five, key-person and leadership continuity, given the turnover we've seen.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** Timing. Nearly eight years is a long hold. At some point a sale becomes likely, and a buyer will want to understand whether the Co-Pilot is still a differentiator, whether the international businesses are fully integrated, and whether the unit economics hold up against free competition. A refreshed, measurable AI story would help with all three.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any update to the Co-Pilot, a new version, new capabilities, or published evidence of results. Second, more brand partnerships like McLaren, which would show Club Champion is still valued as a channel even as brands build their own AI. Third, any move to cheaper or remote fitting tiers. And fourth, leadership news. A confirmed, stable leadership team would answer one of the open questions about this business.

**NORA:** Anything you'd want to learn that isn't public?

**JAMES:** Plenty. Revenue, studio count, average spend per fitting, how often golfers come back, and above all, any data on whether golfers fitted with the Co-Pilot play better or buy more. Those would tell you whether the AI is a feature or a genuine advantage.
