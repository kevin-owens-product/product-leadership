### THE Q3 BOARD PACK

**ASHA:** Six metrics, all at the end of Q3. First, portfolio ARR is three hundred forty-two million dollars. Second, portfolio LTM EBITDA is thirty-three million. Third, portfolio net debt is two hundred eighty-seven million. Fourth, the portfolio reports ninety-six thousand annualized hours saved. Fifth, only forty-two thousand of those hours survive workflow and finance review as usable capacity. Sixth, Keystone's AI-assisted claim-preparation task is forty-one percent faster, while median end-to-end claim-triage cycle time has improved only six percent.

**COLE:** The apparently positive number is the forty-one-percent task improvement. It is real inside the measured step. An examiner used to spend twenty-seven minutes collecting documents, matching policy evidence, and drafting the triage rationale. The assisted median is sixteen minutes. The eval set holds quality roughly constant, mandatory review remains, and the time study excludes failed runs. That is eleven minutes released per eligible claim.

**ASHA:** But the customer does not buy a faster draft. The customer buys a claim moving through a queue with the right evidence, reserve, communication, and exception handling. At Keystone, the draft now waits longer for a senior review because Martin kept the old approval bands. Intake volume also rose after two insurer clients moved more work into the managed service. Faster work entered a fuller bottleneck.

**COLE:** The ninety-six-thousand-hour total has three different measurement species hiding inside it. Thirty-eight thousand hours come from observed system events joined to completed work. Twenty-seven thousand come from manager estimates that multiply users by assumed minutes. Thirty-one thousand come from employee self-report. Those can all be useful signals. They do not deserve the same confidence, and none tells you whether payroll, outsourcing, hiring, backlog, revenue, or service level changed.

**ASHA:** The portfolio average is misleading in another way. Keystone needs a turnaround and has one hundred eighty million dollars of company debt. Vela is growing at exceptional speed and still carries negative LTM EBITDA of five million. Aegis is profitable and fortifying trust. One hour released at Keystone might remove an outsourcer invoice. At Vela it might absorb growth without a planned hire. At Aegis it might deepen evidence review. The hours cannot be swept into one fund-level savings account.

**COLE:** Nor can cash move casually among the borrowers. Northstar has two hundred thirty-five million dollars of uncalled capital after the Q1 proof call. Keystone cannot pay a Vela engineer with a portfolio average, and Vela's growth cannot cure a Keystone covenant. Any new equity must be an explicit call, allocation, and investment decision.

### FROM TASK TIME TO VALUE

**COLE:** Let us build the conversion chain. Task time is the active labor spent on one step. Queue time is the wait before that step begins. Cycle time is elapsed time from customer demand to completed outcome. Throughput is outcomes completed per period. Capacity is the volume a constrained system can reliably handle at a promised service level. They are related. They are not synonyms.

**ASHA:** If Keystone removes eleven minutes from document preparation but senior review is the bottleneck, total cycle time barely moves. The released examiner capacity has four possible destinations. Process more claims without adding labor. Improve quality or customer communication. Redeploy people into another constrained queue. Or remove paid capacity. Each destination has a different evidence standard and a different line in the value bridge.

**COLE:** Demand absorption comes first. Suppose a team has capacity for one hundred claims and demand for one hundred twenty. Automation raises theoretical capacity to one hundred thirty. The first twenty units reduce backlog; only the next ten create headroom. If management planned to hire for those ten, the benefit may be avoided cost. If demand is ninety, the same change creates forty units of unused capacity. Unless work or roles change, the P and L does not.

**ASHA:** Service level matters too. A claims queue cannot be staffed at one hundred percent theoretical utilization and still absorb Monday spikes, complex files, leave, and model exceptions. A capacity model needs arrival volume, mix, handle time, rework, availability, shrinkage, required response time, and a buffer. Calling the buffer waste is how a spreadsheet turns into missed claims and customer penalties.

**COLE:** Vela demonstrates the opposite pattern. Its agent gathers order context and drafts low-risk resolutions quickly. Active work per exception is down thirty-four percent. Yet growth increased the arrival rate by forty-six percent, and customer implementation added new supplier policies faster than the operations team standardized them. Total staff hours rose even as minutes per exception fell. That is productivity. It is not a cost reduction. It may be the reason Vela can grow from one hundred thirty million of ARR without hiring on the old curve.

**ASHA:** Aegis has a quality case. Its system assembles audit evidence and proposes remediations, but it may not touch an industrial control system. Analysts use some released time to investigate high-severity findings. Customer renewal interviews value the deeper review. Finance should not label that labor saving. It is a product and retention hypothesis that needs cohort evidence.

**COLE:** This is why the work-to-value map starts with demand, not tools. For each workflow, map customer trigger, arrival volume and mix, every step and queue, decision rights, active and waiting time, errors and rework, constrained resource, service level, cost, and financial destination. Then ask where AI changes the system. A glowing box around one task is not a redesign.

### WHAT THE EVIDENCE DOES AND DOES NOT SAY

**ASHA:** The external evidence is deliberately conflicting. Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's Generative AI at Work was published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics on February fourth, 2025. In a staggered deployment across five thousand one hundred seventy-two support agents, AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by fifteen percent on average. Less-experienced workers gained much more; the highest-skilled workers gained little.

**COLE:** That study gives us three useful disciplines. Measure completed work, not usage. Look for heterogeneous effects, not one workforce percentage. And examine quality alongside speed. It does not prove that claims examiners, supply-chain operators, or cyber analysts will see the same result. One firm, one occupation, and one tool cannot become Northstar's headcount formula.

**ASHA:** METR gives the counterweight. On July tenth, 2025, it published a randomized study of experienced open-source developers working in their own repositories with early-2025 AI tools. They took nineteen percent longer with the tools, despite expecting acceleration. A February 2026 update warned that later data were too affected by selection and measurement changes for a confident new estimate.

**COLE:** The lesson is not support agents good, developers bad. It is that task, worker expertise, environment, tool, and measurement design change the result. Vela's novice order coordinators may gain from encoded expert patterns while its most experienced supplier negotiators spend time correcting edge cases. Keystone's extraction step may accelerate while the full claim does not. Productivity must be observed at the operating unit you intend to change.

**ASHA:** The OECD's June fifth, 2026 review on AI and skills adds another constraint. Most workers affected by AI do not need to become advanced model builders. They need complementary digital, data, managerial, and human skills. The World Economic Forum's 2025 employer survey found reskilling high on corporate plans, but plans are not realized outcomes. Training attendance is no more a benefit than tool activation.

**COLE:** And the ILO–NASK exposure index published May twentieth, 2025 estimated that one in four jobs has some generative-AI exposure while emphasizing transformation over complete replacement. Exposure means a set of tasks may change. It does not mean one in four people should disappear. An operator who converts occupational exposure directly into a layoff target has skipped workflow, demand, quality, law, trust, and execution.

### AUDIT THE LABOR BRIDGE

**ASHA:** Julian Reed, the fund CFO, reconstructs the ninety-six thousand hours. Forty-two thousand are high-confidence usable capacity. Twenty-one thousand are plausible but need another quarter or a better comparator. Nineteen thousand duplicate benefits across functions. Fourteen thousand describe activity that moved to a different employee or increased review elsewhere. He does not call the lower number disappointing. He calls it usable.

**COLE:** Then he asks what forty-two thousand hours can become. At Keystone, nine thousand hours can reduce external claims-processing spend once two insurer queues are redesigned. Seven thousand can cover known attrition and frozen vacancies without replacement. Six thousand should move to the senior-review bottleneck and customer remediation. That is twenty-two thousand hours with three different financial treatments.

**ASHA:** At Vela, eleven thousand hours absorb forecast order growth and avoid part of the next hiring tranche. The benefit is not recognized on day one. Finance books avoided cost only when approved requisitions are cancelled or hiring moves below the prior capacity plan while service levels hold. Lina retains the right to decide which product workflow earns that capacity.

**COLE:** At Aegis, five thousand hours move from evidence assembly into complex finding review and customer assurance. Four thousand remain unallocated until demand and quality data settle. That work may create retention, risk reduction, or future cost. Today it is capacity with an owner, not a dollar benefit.

**ASHA:** Add severance and double-running. Removing a role does not create immediate annual salary in cash. Notice periods, severance, benefits, retention bonuses, system work, manager time, and parallel operations land first. If annual gross labor removal is four million and transition costs are two million, first-year cash benefit is not four. If the company also hires specialists, net benefit falls again.

**COLE:** Vacancy capture is cleaner but still needs precision. Cancelling a funded role prevents forecast cash. Cancelling an unapproved wish changes nothing. Attrition capture works only if the work is removed, automated, or deliberately absorbed. Otherwise the vacancy becomes overtime, contractor spend, service degradation, or a replacement wearing a different title.

**ASHA:** Redeployment is valuable when named. Move twelve examiners into the constrained review queue, change their training and authority, and measure completed claims and quality. Saying people will do higher-value work is not a plan. The labor bridge needs origin role, destination work, skills gap, manager, date, service target, incentive, and the old work that stops.

### THE LEADERSHIP PROBLEM INSIDE THE MODEL

**COLE:** Keystone's capacity model points to a management decision. Its product line has seven claim modules, three exception teams, and four approval layers. Two modules destroy retention and absorb experts. Martin agrees that AI saves time. He will not retire a module, merge a team, change supervisor authority, or tell a long-serving executive that the role no longer exists.

**ASHA:** That is not resistance to technology. It is a leadership-range mismatch. Martin's deep claims knowledge helped Keystone grow, but the next mandate is a leveraged turnaround: rebase customer promises, simplify products, redesign managed operations, protect cash, and surface bad news. The board must assess the leader against the next twenty-four months, not gratitude for the last ten years.

**COLE:** Priya Nwosu has spent Q2 and Q3 leading the proof contracts. In one insurer queue, she removed a redundant approval, created a daily exception huddle, retrained examiners, and tied the AI draft to a customer service level. The task saving became a seventeen-percent throughput gain with stable error rates. She also stopped a pilot when the comparison group was contaminated. Her evidence is operating behavior, not a transformation deck.

**ASHA:** Martin proposes that Priya lead a program office while he retains every decision. That would give her accountability without authority and leave the organization unchanged. Northstar must decide whether to replace a respected CEO during a fragile retention repair. The change will create customer, employee, and lender risk. Keeping the wrong range creates risk too; it just arrives more quietly.

**COLE:** Vela poses the inverse problem. Lina is the right product leader and a key-person risk. The portfolio AI team offered reusable specialists, then began setting her sequencing because central allocation looked efficient. A shared security standard is a control. A specialist guild is optional capacity. A roadmap is a company decision tied to Vela's customers, P and L, and founder accountability. Confusing those categories makes federation feel like confiscation.

**ASHA:** The talent operating model therefore needs two axes. What expertise is scarce enough to share? Eval design, red-team methods, identity patterns, model procurement, and benefits accounting qualify. What judgment must remain local? Customer workflow, ontology, product priority, staffing, action design, service level, and kill authority. Specialists advise or embed under a company sponsor; they do not become a shadow executive team.

### THREE CREDIBLE CHOICES

**ASHA:** Choice one is one central portfolio AI organization. Move specialists, workflow designers, and selected product managers under Northstar. Allocate them to the highest expected return across the fund. Standardize tooling, training, job architecture, and productivity targets. This could reduce duplication, deepen careers, and give Keystone talent it cannot recruit alone.

**COLE:** Its weakness is not centralization itself. It is ownership. The fund cannot operate three customer promises through one backlog. The central team would hold people while company CEOs held P and L consequences. Chargebacks could make cost visible, but not restore decision context. Value potential is medium-high; cash efficiency looks high; portfolio and key-person risk are high; evidence could arrive quickly; reversibility is medium if talent has not left.

**ASHA:** Choice two is to capture cost immediately. Use the verified forty-two thousand hours plus the credible portion of the next twenty-one thousand. Set a three point nine million annual labor target, reduce roles proportionally, and require each company to preserve service levels. It is decisive, helps Keystone's leverage, and tells the fund that measured productivity has consequences.

**COLE:** Also credible. Companies do need to harvest cost, and indefinite redeployment can become avoidance. But proportional reduction ignores bottlenecks and demand. Keystone could remove examiners while senior review remains constrained. Vela could cut ahead of growth. Aegis could weaken customer assurance. Cash evidence is fast after transition cost; operating damage may lag; risk is high; reversibility is low.

**ASHA:** Choice three is a federated bench plus company-by-company redesign. Share specialist guilds and minimum control standards. Make each CEO own a work-to-value map, capacity model, role blueprint, and labor bridge. Harvest cost only after work, queue, authority, service level, and transition are redesigned. Replace leadership where the mandate requires it. This is slower and it still removes roles.

**COLE:** It also demands the most management. The center must resist empire building. Companies must expose weak work design. Finance must validate avoided hires and released cash. Value potential is high but uneven. Near-term cash consumption is highest because Keystone needs double-running and leadership transition. Risk is bounded by workflow sequencing. Evidence comes in stages. Reversibility is medium for process and low for CEO and role decisions.

### THE DECISION MEMO

**ASHA:** Northstar calls fifteen million dollars, all into Keystone. Five million funds customer retention and service recovery in the weakest cohorts. Four million funds severance, double-running, and the leadership transition. Three million funds workflow, data, and queue redesign. Two million funds frontline skills and supervisor changes. One million is a controlled contingency released only through the turnaround review. The total is equity capital, not portfolio cash magically moved from Vela or Aegis.

**COLE:** Owner: Priya owns Keystone's integrated turnaround result; Lina, Omar, and Priya each own their company's local work-to-value plan; Julian validates every labor benefit and capital movement before it enters the fund ledger. The shared specialist bench advises under those company owners and does not acquire their roadmap authority.

**COLE:** The productivity target is a bridge, not an hour total. By Q4, Keystone must remove or avoid three point two million dollars of annualized labor and outsourcing cost, with at least one point four million contracted or visible in the next twelve months' cash forecast. It must redeploy capacity sufficient for a fifteen-percent increase in completed eligible claims, hold material error rates, and reduce the senior-review queue below one working day.

**ASHA:** Vela must absorb the Q4 volume plan without the old hiring ratio, but no saving is booked until approved requisitions or contractor commitments change. Aegis must show whether deeper finding review improves renewal evidence before finance values it. Across all three, no self-reported hour enters Julian's realized-benefit ledger without a system outcome and a financial event.

**COLE:** Guardrails: company CEOs retain product and workflow decisions; portfolio standards may veto unsafe identity, permission, eval, or incident designs but may not reorder a roadmap for convenience. Customer service levels and quality remain inside contract. No role exits before its work is removed, transferred, or deliberately stopped. No manager carries more redesigned direct reports without a capability and decision-rights review.

**ASHA:** Kill and reversal criteria: pause Keystone role removal if backlog, complaint, or error thresholds fail for two consecutive reviews. Stop a shared specialist service if a second company cannot adopt it without importing the first company's ontology or workflow assumptions. Replace the interim plan if Priya misses the ninety-day retention, cash, and organization gates without an evidenced recovery. If Lina's autonomy is breached, the operating principal—not Lina—owns correction.

**COLE:** Confidence is high that duplicated claims work and external spend can be reduced, medium on avoided hiring at Vela, and low on assigning dollar value to Aegis quality capacity. The forecast reflects those confidence levels. The fund does not average them into one productivity multiplier.

### REDESIGN THE WORK, THEN THE ORG

**COLE:** Priya starts with three Keystone workflows, not the whole company. Claim intake and evidence assembly. Senior triage and exception routing. Customer status and missing-document communication. For each, the team observes arrival patterns, maps active and queue time, marks every decision, and identifies the constrained role. They include model failures and manual fallbacks rather than timing only successful runs.

**ASHA:** The capacity model is built weekly by claim type. Demand, seasonality, complexity, productive hours, shrinkage, rework, service level, and buffer. If one hour of nominal capacity handles simple claims but a surge contains complex commercial files, the mix matters. Priya will not let a spreadsheet divide annual volume by annual hours and call the answer staffing.

**COLE:** The role blueprint follows. Examiners spend less time collecting evidence and more time resolving ambiguity and communicating with customers. Supervisors review only exceptions above risk thresholds rather than every assisted file. Operations analysts maintain queue rules and trace quality. A small workflow reliability role owns context freshness, fallback, and drift. Some existing roles change; some end; some new ones are funded from the call.

**ASHA:** Skills are attached to work. Evidence judgment, policy interpretation, exception diagnosis, customer communication, and agent supervision. Training has a proficiency test and an observed production period. Completion certificates do not prove readiness. Managers receive new decision rights, and their incentives include throughput, quality, service level, capacity realization, and employee transition—not raw tool use.

**COLE:** The management-equity reset matters. Priya's interim package rewards retained ARR, cash, EBITDA improvement, customer service, and durable workflow capacity. It does not pay separately for headcount reduction. Lina's package is updated for succession depth and operating-team development so Northstar does not solve founder dependence by undermining the founder. Omar's Aegis objectives include trust and control adoption without forcing roadmap uniformity.

**ASHA:** Change trust is operational. Keystone tells employees which work is changing, which decisions remain human, how quality will be monitored, when roles will be decided, and what support exists. Leaders do not invite everyone to co-design whether the turnaround happens. They give people truthful process, timely decisions, and a way to challenge unsafe assumptions.

### BUILD YOUR WORK-TO-VALUE PACK

**COLE:** Kevin, your operator drill has six connected pages. First, the work-to-value map. Choose one workflow with actual demand. Map the customer trigger, volume and mix, every task and queue, active and wait time, decisions, rework, failure paths, constrained resource, service level, and economic destination. Highlight what the AI changes and what it does not.

**ASHA:** Second, the demand and capacity model. Use weekly or daily arrival patterns, not annual averages. Show productive hours, shrinkage, quality rework, skills, utilization buffer, and required response time. Produce a base, upside, and downside. State whether released capacity reduces backlog, absorbs growth, avoids hiring, removes paid work, or improves quality.

**COLE:** Third, the role and skills blueprint. For every affected role: tasks removed, tasks changed, tasks added, decisions gained or lost, required proficiency, training evidence, manager, span, and transition date. Mark where separation of duties or human accountability prevents combining roles even when the hours appear to fit.

**ASHA:** Fourth, the labor benefit bridge. Opening payroll and outsourced cost. Gross removals. Vacancies truly funded. Attrition captured. Contractor exits. New specialist hires. Wage and location effects. Severance, notice, retention, training, and double-running. Then net P and L run rate and cash by month. Give finance an event that proves each row.

**COLE:** Fifth, the management and incentive reset. What mandate does the next twenty-four months require? What evidence says the current leader can execute it? What rights must move? Which outcome measures enter incentives? What key-person risk grows if the center overreaches? Write the succession and interim gates before personalities dominate the discussion.

**ASHA:** Sixth, the transition plan. Customer communication, employee sequence, works council or other required consultation where applicable, system changes, access removal, knowledge transfer, service guardrails, and weekly evidence. Employment obligations vary by jurisdiction; involve qualified HR and legal advisers. An operating model does not suspend them.

**COLE:** Run one red-team calculation. Take your biggest time-saved claim and assume demand rises twenty percent, exception rate doubles, and the bottleneck moves downstream. Does the saving still become cash? Then assume demand falls twenty percent. Which roles or contracts actually leave, by what date? If neither scenario changes the labor bridge, the hours were probably presentation inventory.

**ASHA:** The artifact is complete when the same unit of work connects telemetry, capacity, organization, customer outcome, P and L, cash, and the person who decides. If each page uses a different denominator, you have six sophisticated ways to avoid the decision.

### THE BOUNDARY WE DID NOT TEST

**COLE:** Priya's first month produces an uncomfortable success. Keystone adopts the common telemetry contract, Vela uses the specialist identity guild without surrendering its roadmap, and Aegis offers to host portfolio-wide control signals through its trusted monitoring service. Reuse finally looks real because a second and third company can adopt the interface.

**ASHA:** Then, at two thirteen on a Thursday morning, an Aegis analyst sees a machine identity enumerate metadata fields outside its own company namespace. No customer payload is visible. No destructive action occurs. The token should never have been able to ask the question.

**COLE:** Productivity forced us to map the work. The next decision forces us to map the boundary: which interfaces belong in a portfolio or ecosystem platform, which identity and data domains must remain local, and what happens when one shared credential turns efficiency into blast radius.

**ASHA:** Capturing productivity is not taking time away from people. It is converting a changed workflow into a customer, capacity, cost, cash, and talent decision—with the transition expense and leadership consequence left in the same picture.
