# DEADWATER
## Episode 5: "The Surveillance"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] The first rule of surveillance is patience. The second rule is that the subject must never know they are being watched. Not a flicker of it. Not a change in the rhythm of the world around them that their instincts might register without their conscious mind being able to name it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss had spent twenty years being careful. He had survived the initial investigation. He had survived the media coverage. He had survived a decade of the case going nowhere while he drove his truck to work and coached youth sports and went to bed at a reasonable hour. His caution was hardwired by that point. It was not a thought. It was a posture.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The task force knew all of this. Which is why the surveillance operation they built around him starting in March 2022 is one of the most methodical and low-footprint approaches I have encountered in twenty years of covering law enforcement. No unmarked cars sitting outside his house. No follows on his commute. Instead: digital. Everywhere and nowhere.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh briefs the task force on the decision to focus on Voss during the third week of March 2022. She has spent two weeks before that briefing building what she calls a "cold profile" - everything publicly available about Roland Voss. Property records. Business filings. Court appearances - a parking dispute from 2009, a contract disagreement from 2014, both minor. Voter registration. Social media presence, which is minimal. Photographs from public events.

[MARSH] By the time I stood up in that briefing room I had a three-inch binder on Roland Voss. And nothing in it would have raised a flag if you looked at any individual piece. He paid his taxes. He had no criminal record. He'd been married for twenty-two years. He went to church occasionally - not regularly, but occasionally. He was a member of a local civic organization. He was, on paper, exactly what he appeared to be.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] The only anomalies I found in those two weeks were things you had to know to look for. The vehicle registration at the time of the parking citation. The geographic connection - his firm had done work within the barrier island corridor. And something I found in a local newspaper archive from 2003: a letter to the editor, signed with his full name, objecting to proposed lighting upgrades in the access road parking areas. He objected because, he wrote, the lights would disturb the wildlife and alter the character of the natural landscape.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] He wanted those parking areas to stay dark.

[REESE] When Carol showed me that letter I just sat with it for a moment. And then I said: this is our guy. I don't usually say things like that until we have a lot more. But I was certain.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The digital surveillance begins in the last week of March 2022. The task force obtains a series of warrants - each one carefully scoped, each one argued before a judge who pushes back at several points and needs to be satisfied that the probable cause threshold is met.

[NARRATOR] DA Frank Tully handles the warrant applications personally.

[TULLY] I've been a DA for nineteen years and I've authorized a lot of surveillance applications. This one was different in that the predicate evidence was all circumstantial and all old. A parking citation. A witness account of a truck. A letter to the editor. None of it is direct. None of it is what you'd call solid. But the pattern was coherent and the lead detective had twelve years of case knowledge behind her and I trusted her read of it. We applied for narrow, specific warrants and we got them.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] The rule I gave the task force was: nothing moves fast. We build this brick by brick. One piece of admissible evidence at a time. If we rush this and it falls apart, those families never get justice. I was not prepared to let that happen.

[NARRATOR] The first thing they look at is cell phone data. Not Voss's personal phone - they don't have a warrant for that yet, and pulling it too early could alert him. Instead, they look at records for the area - which cell towers were active, which registered devices pinged them - during the windows of time when each of the victims was last known to be in contact with their families.

[DR. PARK] What we're looking for is a ghost. A pattern of connection that is consistent across multiple victims and multiple time periods but that doesn't belong to anyone in the victim's known network. An unregistered number, typically. A prepaid device - what's called a burner - that appears near the victim at the time of contact and then disappears from the record.

[NARRATOR] Dr. Nina Park is the task force's digital forensic lead, working alongside her role as the DNA specialist. Her background combines molecular biology with data analytics, which makes her, as Carol Marsh puts it, "the person in the room who understands how all the threads connect."

[DR. PARK] Across four of the confirmed victim cases, we identify the same unregistered number making contact in the window of time before the victim went missing. The number is never the same - it changes. Different prepaid SIM cards. But the device itself - the physical handset - shows a consistent hardware signature in the network metadata. Same phone, different SIMs.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] That phone, and the various SIM cards used in it, ping cell towers in two consistent areas: the barrier island corridor where the bodies were found, and a cluster of towers in Nassau County, on Long Island - approximately fifteen miles from Gilgo Beach.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] Massapequa Park is in that cluster.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The burner phone is a crucial link but it is not alone enough. They need to connect the physical device to Roland Voss specifically. This requires something investigators do not yet have: a direct intersection between Voss and the phone's location in a window that can be corroborated.

[REESE] We needed to put him and the burner at the same place at the same time, in a way that wasn't explainable by coincidence. And to do that without tipping him off, we needed to be patient. We needed to wait for him to go about his life and give us the moments we needed.

[NARRATOR] The surveillance team watches. Digitally, carefully, at distance. They pull records from his train commute - the commuter rail logs the time each card is swiped. They pull his E-ZPass records from the highway. They pull security footage from the station nearest to his firm's Manhattan office.

[MARSH] Every day for six months, we were building a map of his movements. Where he was. When. For how long. Cross-referencing it against the burner phone's location history. Looking for the overlap.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] In September 2022, we find it. Three separate occasions, spread over a six-week period, where the burner phone is active in the barrier island corridor and Roland Voss's E-ZPass places him on the highway within a fifteen-minute window. He is using a prepaid phone near the dump sites while driving the same road. In winter. When the park is closed.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] He is still going back. After everything. After twelve years. He is still going back to that road.

[REESE] That's when I stopped sleeping well. Because it told us something about who we were dealing with. This isn't someone who fled. This isn't someone who moved and started over. He stayed. He stayed fifteen miles from where those women were, and he kept going back. And I don't know exactly what that means psychologically, and I'm not sure I want to. But operationally what it meant is that he felt completely safe.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] He had been safe for twelve years. He had no reason to think that was changing.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The DNA evidence is the next pillar. Dr. Park runs a genealogical analysis on the male hair found on one of the victims in the early years of the investigation. She has been waiting for this moment - waiting for the technology, waiting for the databases to expand, waiting for the moment when she could run this analysis with confidence.

[DR. PARK] Forensic genealogy works by extracting genetic markers from a sample and running them against consumer DNA databases - the kind populated by people who do ancestry testing for personal reasons, without knowing they've potentially entered themselves into a law enforcement resource. From those matches, we build a family tree outward. We look for relatives of the unknown contributor and trace the family branches until we identify someone who fits the geographic, temporal, and physical profile.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] The analysis of the hair takes six weeks. When the family tree resolves, it points to a cluster of relatives with a common ancestor. When we trace the branches of that tree forward through the generations, one name appears at the intersection of all the relevant factors. Long Island. Male. The right age range. The right physical profile.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] Roland Voss.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The genealogical analysis is consistent with Voss being the contributor. It is not conclusive on its own - they need a direct sample to compare against the crime scene material. But it is enough to intensify the surveillance. It is enough to take to DA Tully for the next round of warrants.

[TULLY] When Nina brought me the genealogy results alongside the cell data and the E-ZPass records, I felt the case shift. I've had that feeling twice in my career. The feeling that you are standing on the edge of something. That the thing you've been building toward is finally close enough to touch.

[NARRATOR] Close. But not there yet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They still need the direct DNA comparison. They need Voss's sample. And they need to get it without him knowing. Without triggering the extreme caution of a man who has been careful for twenty years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They need to wait for him to throw something away.

[PAUSE]
