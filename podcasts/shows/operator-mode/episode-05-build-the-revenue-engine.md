### THE CONTRACT THAT COULD NOT GO LIVE

**ASHA:** You have three pricing choices: per seat, pure outcome, or annual workflow-volume commitments with overages. Each choice changes product telemetry, seller behavior, implementation, forecasting, margin, and customer trust. Price is not a number attached after product strategy. It is an operating design.

But thirty-five percent of the qualified dollars have no validated workflow volume, no data-readiness check, or no reserved implementation slot. If those opportunities cannot clear the conditions required to become live, they are not coverage. They are optimism stored in a CRM.

**COLE:** And live AI ARR is up eight hundred thousand, from three hundred thousand to one point one million, while signed backlog is one point eight million. The ratio says the sales motion can promise roughly twice what delivery has put into use. That is not automatically bad; enterprise software always has some backlog. The danger is that time to value is sixty-eight days and growing. The longer the delay, the less evidence exists before renewal and the more likely the customer treats Assist as an executive purchase rather than an operating habit.

**ASHA:** Q4 company economics are improving, but they leave little room for waste. Quarterly revenue is twenty-six point two-nine million dollars. ### START WITH THE BUYING AND USING UNIT

**ASHA:** Before pricing, define the customer. Harborline has twelve hundred accounts across commercial HVAC, fire safety, elevator, and industrial maintenance. The original ICP was effectively contractor with a budget. That is not enough. Which segment can adopt Assist now?

**COLE:** Multi-branch HVAC and industrial-maintenance contractors with at least twenty-five thousand service work orders a year, equipment identity above eighty-five percent, digital technician adoption above seventy percent, and an executive owner for first-time fix or quote cycle time. Fire safety remains a controlled segment because jurisdictional content is uneven. Elevator customers need more source coverage. Small contractors may love the feature but cannot support the implementation cost yet.

**ASHA:** That definition includes economics and readiness, not just industry. A customer with large theoretical volume but unresolved asset identity is not a near-term ICP. A customer with excellent data and three thousand annual jobs may not produce enough contribution to fund a high-touch launch. Segmentation is a capital decision.

**COLE:** The user, buyer, and beneficiary differ. The technician uses the draft. The service operations leader owns productivity and first-time fix. The finance leader may approve the spend. IT and legal govern data. The branch manager determines whether the workflow is actually adopted. Positioning has to connect those people: Assist turns authorized service history into a cited, technician-approved next action inside the existing work order, reducing search and quote effort without creating a parallel chat destination.

**ASHA:** That is stronger than AI copilot for field service because it names the workflow, the evidence, and the control. It also implies the billable unit. The economic event is an assisted work order, not a named seat. Technicians are seasonal, work across branches, and sometimes share devices. A seat is easy to meter and weakly connected to value.

**COLE:** Yet per-seat pricing is not foolish. Buyers understand it. Sellers can quote it. Procurement can compare it. It produces predictable invoices, and it encourages the vendor to make every user active. The problem is that one dispatcher may initiate thousands of assisted jobs while fifty occasional technicians initiate very few. Price would diverge from both Harborline's compute cost and customer benefit.

**ASHA:** Pure outcome pricing sits at the other extreme. Charge a percentage of avoided truck rolls, incremental accepted quote value, or verified technician hours saved. The alignment is attractive. So are the disputes. Weather, technician experience, parts availability, and customer mix all affect first-time fix. Quote revenue depends on local pricing and sales follow-up. Harborline would spend months auditing attribution before it could invoice.

**COLE:** Which is why the middle is credible: an annual commitment to assisted workflow volume, with overages and quality terms. It matches the operational unit, gives the customer budget certainty, gives Harborline a capacity signal, and keeps outcome evidence central without pretending every outcome is solely caused by the product.

### PRICE THE WORKFLOW, NOT THE NOVELTY

**ASHA:** Walk through the package. No mysterious enterprise tier.

**COLE:** Three annual volume bands based on eligible assisted work orders. The entry band commits twenty-five thousand orders. The growth band commits one hundred thousand. The scale band commits three hundred thousand or more. Higher commitments lower the unit rate. Overage rates are stated in advance. Across the expected mix, realized price averages four dollars per assisted work order, consistent with the workload P and L from the last episode.

**ASHA:** The annual commitment matters because Harborline reserves implementation and support capacity. But the contract cannot make the customer pay indefinitely for a service Harborline failed to activate. So the billing clock starts at the first production branch or a mutually agreed readiness date, not merely at signature. If the customer misses its data obligations after Harborline is ready, the contract defines the consequence. Accountability works both ways.

**COLE:** We also separate platform access from volume. Core Harborline remains priced under its existing contract. Assist includes the production service, model processing, citations, approval workflow, monitoring, and a committed volume pool. Professional services cover genuinely customer-specific migrations. We do not bury recurring inference inside one-time implementation fees or call recurring human operations software margin.

**ASHA:** Bessemer's 2026 AI pricing and monetization playbook is useful because it frames pricing as a match among value metric, predictability, and cost exposure. The practical lesson for Harborline is not that outcome pricing is always advanced. It is that the metric should be legible to the buyer, correlated with value, measurable by both parties, and survivable for the vendor under heavy use.

**COLE:** The packaging also protects learning. Every commitment includes a defined proof-of-value cohort before broad rollout: at least one thousand eligible jobs or thirty days, whichever creates enough evidence; baseline search and quote time; a branch-level comparison where feasible; acceptance and severe-error thresholds; and explicit data readiness. A demo does not become proof. A pilot does not become ARR until the service is live under the metric contract.

**ASHA:** Bessemer's July twenty-ninth, 2025 product-market-fit playbook puts unusual weight on retention and depth of use as evidence, which matters here. Harborline should not confuse executive enthusiasm with a workhorse product. The useful signal is whether technicians return to the workflow, branches expand eligible volume, and the customer renews after the novelty is gone.

**COLE:** One more pricing trap: discounting before learning. Sales wants a fifty-percent launch discount for the first year. That would increase signatures and corrupt willingness-to-pay evidence. Instead, design partners receive service credits tied to the extra feedback and controlled rollout work they provide. Discounts expire, are visible, and do not change the standard unit economics presented to the board.

### PIPELINE IS A CONVERSION SYSTEM

**ASHA:** Now connect demand to math. The next-quarter Assist booking target is two million dollars of annual commitments. At a twenty-six percent win rate from sales-accepted opportunity, Harborline needs about seven point seven million dollars of genuinely qualified pipeline. That is three point eight-five times coverage. The dashboard says four point one. Why am I still worried?

**COLE:** Because coverage is dollars divided by target, while conversion depends on stage quality, timing, and capacity. Of the eight point two million dollars labeled qualified, two point nine million lacks one of the activation prerequisites. Exclude it and real coverage is two point six-five times. If we keep the original target, sellers will either miss or pull forward poorly qualified contracts, worsening shelfware.

**ASHA:** So redefine stages. An opportunity enters qualified only when the ICP, economic problem, approximate eligible volume, decision process, data owner, and target production quarter are known. It enters proof when a baseline dataset and success criteria are agreed. It enters commit only when commercial redlines are substantially closed, data rights are clear, the implementation slot is reserved, and the buyer has confirmed the rollout owner.

**COLE:** That stage contract reduces the visible pipeline, which is politically painful and operationally useful. It also improves forecast language. Pipeline is possibility. Best case has a credible path but unresolved conditions. Commit has conditions substantially inside our control. Booked is signed. Live ARR is activated under the company's ARR definition. Revenue follows the contract and service period. Cash follows invoicing and collection. Five different events, five different dates.

**ASHA:** Let us add sales capacity. Harborline forms four vertical pods, initially weighted toward HVAC and industrial maintenance. Across account executives and expansion managers, ten quota carriers can sell Assist. Six are fully productive. Four are at roughly fifty-percent ramp. At one million dollars of annual quota per fully productive carrier, effective annual booking capacity is eight million, or two million per quarter.

**COLE:** Which means the booking target already assumes full use of current effective capacity. Adding pipeline alone will not create more closed business. A new seller hired today lowers productivity before increasing it because managers, solution engineers, and implementation teams absorb ramp. The capacity model needs start date, ramp curve, attrition, quota, attainment distribution, and support ratios—not headcount multiplied by average quota.

**ASHA:** Implementation is the tighter constraint. Four squads can each launch three average customers per quarter. At roughly one hundred twenty thousand dollars of average Assist ACV, theoretical activation capacity is about one point four-four million dollars of live ARR per quarter. Variability, large accounts, and data remediation reduce reliable capacity to about one point three million. Selling two million every quarter grows backlog unless scope or throughput changes.

**COLE:** So the integrated model has a conservation law: opening signed backlog plus new bookings minus cancellations equals activations plus ending backlog. If the equation does not reconcile, someone has hidden a delay. Harborline's Q4 backlog of one point eight million, plus a two million booking target, against one point three to one point five million activation capacity, would leave more than two million still waiting. Northstar is not an exception. It is the model.

### IMPLEMENTATION IS PART OF THE PRODUCT

**ASHA:** The common reaction is hire more implementation managers. Why is that incomplete?

**COLE:** Because forty-six percent of launch effort is data mapping repeated across customers, twenty-two percent is permission and contract clarification, eighteen percent is training and workflow configuration, and fourteen percent is project coordination. Only the last category scales mostly with headcount. The rest should produce reusable product capabilities: schema adapters, asset-resolution diagnostics, contract playbooks, in-product readiness checks, and role-based onboarding.

**ASHA:** In other words, implementation contains product discovery. But not every custom request deserves code. Harborline needs a standard launch path and a priced exception path. What is standard?

**COLE:** Four gates. Readiness: work-order and asset coverage meet the threshold. Authorization: contract, tenant, roles, and model-processing path are approved. Proof: the cohort clears outcome and safety criteria. Rollout: branches activate in waves with telemetry and named ownership. Each gate has an artifact, a responsible person, an exit criterion, and a maximum waiting time. Customers can see the same plan Harborline sees.

**ASHA:** Time to value becomes the interval from signature to the first successful assisted work order, and activation becomes the share of eligible work orders actually assisted in the live branches. Both need distributions. A median of forty-five days can hide a tail of customers at one hundred fifty. For renewal, the tail often matters more than the median because those accounts received the least evidence.

**COLE:** Sales compensation should reflect that without turning account executives into project managers. Today one hundred percent of the variable commission is earned at signature. The new design pays sixty percent at signature, twenty percent when the first production branch is live, and twenty percent when the account reaches sixty percent of agreed eligible volume within ninety days. The pod shares the activation component with implementation and customer success through team objectives.

**ASHA:** Finance will object that commission administration becomes complicated. Sales will object that downstream teams control forty percent. Both objections are real. The answer is shared service-level agreements and exception rules. If Harborline misses the reserved launch date, the seller is protected. If sales sold outside the readiness criteria, the activation portion is at risk. Incentives expose handoff quality; they do not replace decision rights.

**COLE:** The strongest onboarding work comes from the one point six million dollars already allocated in episode three. Harborline funds self-service readiness diagnostics, two common schema adapters, an asset-identity repair workflow, implementation templates, and enablement. We are not adding a new transformation budget. We are converting bespoke labor into repeatable capacity.

### ADOPTION, RENEWAL, AND EXPANSION

**ASHA:** Once a customer is live, what leading indicators tell us whether the ARR is healthy?

**COLE:** Seven signals feed customer health, though only the most decision-relevant reach the board. Time to first successful order. Percentage of live branches. Eligible-volume activation. Weekly returning technician teams. Recommendation acceptance and correction. Severe-error trend. Operational outcome movement such as search time or quote cycle. Add support burden and executive sponsor engagement, but do not let subjective sentiment overpower observed use.

**ASHA:** Health scores often become weighted soup. A customer can be green because it attended a business review while usage collapsed. How do we keep this one honest?

**COLE:** Use explicit states and falsifiable rules. Not ready means authorization or data gates are incomplete. Launching means the first cohort is running but evidence is immature. Healthy means at least sixty percent eligible-volume activation, quality inside the envelope, and one verified customer outcome. At risk means a material threshold missed for two periods. Recovery has a dated plan and owner. Human judgment can override the state, but the override is visible and expires.

**ASHA:** Expansion then follows evidence, not a generic upsell calendar. First expand branches within the same workflow. Then increase the volume commitment. Then add an adjacent job such as quote drafting after diagnosis is healthy. Do not sell fire-compliance generation to an HVAC customer because the quarter is light. The expansion path should increase context density and customer value without outrunning the envelope.

**COLE:** This is where vertical software can have structural advantage. Stripe and Tidemark's 2025 vertical SaaS benchmark found that broader product adoption is associated with stronger growth and retention in the companies studied. We should not claim that adding any module causes retention. Harborline's inference is narrower: an adjacent workflow can deepen retention if it shares data, reduces customer effort, and reaches sustained use.

**ASHA:** NRR is the lagging score. Opening recurring revenue from the cohort, plus expansion, minus contraction and churn, divided by opening recurring revenue. New logos do not enter. A customer who buys Assist and then downgrades core dispatch may still produce negative net expansion. We look at account-level and cohort bridges so AI expansion cannot camouflage core damage.

**COLE:** GRR remains the floor because it ignores expansion. Harborline entered at eighty-eight percent verified GRR and one hundred one percent NRR. By Q4 NRR is ninety-seven. The recovery target is not achieved by selling more Assist into a shrinking set of happy giants. We need broad retention improvement, then expansion. Concentration already matters: the top twenty customers are twenty-three percent of ARR.

### THE THREE PRICING CHOICES

[MUSIC STING]

**ASHA:** Decision gate. Choice one: per-seat pricing. It is simple, comparable, predictable, and easy to forecast. Harborline could bundle Assist into premium technician tiers, use the existing sales motion, and reduce metering arguments. It would likely accelerate signatures among customers already buying user licenses.

**COLE:** It would also encourage seat minimization and shared access. Compute and support rise with work-order volume, not named technicians. A dispatcher with a small seat count could generate enormous workload. The product would optimize monthly active users rather than successful jobs. Value created is moderate, cash consumption low, risk of misalignment high, speed to evidence fast, reversibility medium because customers anchor on the unit.

**ASHA:** Choice two: pure outcome pricing. Harborline charges for an avoided repeat visit, an approved quote, or a share of verified savings. It offers the strongest headline alignment and could support a higher price when the result is valuable. It also differentiates the product from generic software.

**COLE:** Attribution is not mature enough. First-time fix has multiple causes; approved quote value is not realized cash; customers will dispute counterfactuals; and long measurement windows delay billing and forecast. Value could be high, cash collection slow, risk high, speed to evidence slow, and reversibility low once contracts and systems encode contested outcomes. Outcome evidence should support selling, not yet define every invoice.

**ASHA:** Choice three: annual workflow-volume commitments with overages. The meter is the assisted work order. The customer gets predictable committed spend and transparent overages. Harborline gets a capacity signal and a unit linked to variable cost. Quality and activation terms protect both parties. The realized rate averages four dollars as volume mix grows.

**COLE:** This choice is not perfect. A rejected recommendation still consumes a unit unless the service credit rules say otherwise. Customers may undercommit. Volume can fluctuate seasonally. Metering must be auditable. But the unit is observable, connected to value, and close enough to cost. It gives us evidence needed to consider more outcome-linked pricing later.

**ASHA:** Kevin, choose. Then choose the commercial behavior your choice will reward. A pricing model without activation ownership and forecast definitions is only a rate card.

### THE CANONICAL CALL

**ASHA:** Harborline chooses annual workflow-volume commitments with overages. Average realized price remains four dollars per assisted work order across the mix. The contract includes an activation plan, customer readiness obligations, service-envelope terms, auditable metering, and credits for material Harborline-caused service failure. Outcome evidence proves value and guides expansion; it does not yet control the invoice.

**COLE:** We narrow the initial ICP. We create HVAC and industrial-maintenance pods first, with controlled fire-safety coverage and no broad elevator launch until source completeness improves. Each pod contains sales, solution engineering, implementation, customer success, and product analytics capacity. Account ownership remains clear even though the work crosses functions.

**ASHA:** We change sales compensation to sixty percent at signature, twenty at first live branch, and twenty at sixty-percent eligible-volume activation within ninety days, with protections when Harborline misses its obligations. Commit forecast requires data rights and an implementation slot. The CRO owns bookings and forecast accuracy. The COO owns launch capacity. Both own the signed-to-live conversion metric.

**COLE:** The team also stops selling the proof of value as an unbounded free pilot. It is a defined cohort with baseline, minimum evidence volume, quality gates, and an agreed conversion path. Product telemetry starts at readiness, not after launch, so we can distinguish not configured, configured but unused, recommendations rejected, and outcomes not sustained.

### BUILD YOUR INTEGRATED GTM MODEL

**COLE:** Kevin, the artifact is an integrated GTM model and quarterly ARR bridge. Begin at the end, not the top of the funnel. What live ARR must exist by quarter? What activation volume creates it? What signed backlog and implementation throughput are required? Only then calculate bookings, qualified pipeline, and lead generation.

**ASHA:** Use seven connected schedules. First, the ARR bridge: opening ARR, new logo, core expansion, AI or product expansion, contraction, churn, acquisition, ending ARR. Second, pipeline conversion by stage and segment. Third, sales capacity by named role, start date, ramp, quota, and attainment. Fourth, signed backlog and implementation capacity. Fifth, activation and adoption cohorts. Sixth, contribution and CAC payback. Seventh, forecast, invoice, cash collection, and variance.

**COLE:** Every schedule needs a conservation check. Opening pipeline plus created opportunities minus closed-won, closed-lost, and slipped equals ending pipeline. Opening signed backlog plus bookings minus cancellations equals activations plus ending backlog. Opening ARR plus the bridge components equals ending ARR. Opening cash plus receipts minus disbursements equals ending cash. If the rows do not reconcile, the model is storytelling.

**ASHA:** Make assumptions inspectable. Win rate is not one company average; split by ICP, source, seller maturity, and proof completion. Sales capacity is not seats times quota; apply ramp and expected attainment. Implementation capacity is not project managers times accounts; use work units and variability. CAC payback uses live contribution, not bookings. NRR excludes new logos.

**COLE:** Add three scenarios. Base assumes current conversion and the planned onboarding improvement. Downside holds bookings but delays activation, because that is the risk Harborline just experienced. Stress combines lower win rate, slower activation, and higher inference cost. Watch which constraint moves first. A robust plan reveals whether the next dollar belongs in demand, sellers, implementation, data readiness, or retention.

**ASHA:** Then create the operating view. Six metrics only: capacity-adjusted pipeline coverage, forecast accuracy, signed-to-live days, eligible-volume activation, cohort contribution and CAC payback, and NRR with its gross-retention floor. Each has a definition, source system, owner, review frequency, and decision threshold. More metrics may exist underneath; the meeting should not become a tour of the warehouse.

**COLE:** Finally, force one sentence for the pricing logic: we charge this customer this amount per this unit because it correlates with this value, costs us this much to deliver, can be measured this way, and creates these behaviors. If that sentence is evasive, the model will be evasive too.

**ASHA:** Next time, the sponsor sees the Q5 improvement and asks for six million dollars of immediate annual savings. Some of the requested cuts would remove the reliability, data, and implementation capacity that made the growth possible. Your choice will be broad layoffs, indiscriminate offshoring, or redesigning the operating system around drivers, decisions, and validated benefits.

**COLE:** A revenue engine is not complete when sales can sell. It is complete when the company can qualify, contract, activate, prove, renew, expand, collect, and forecast the work without hiding the queue between those verbs.
