### SEGMENT 1: The New Leadership Profile (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** So let us start at the top. When you walk into a company and assess whether their leadership team is equipped for an AI-native transformation, what are you actually looking for? What separates the leaders who can drive this from the ones who become obstacles?

**KEVIN:** I look for four capabilities, and I have learned the hard way that all four are necessary — you cannot trade one off against the others. The first is systems thinking. An AI-native leader has to hold the entire value chain in their head simultaneously. They cannot just optimize their silo. When the VP of Product is making decisions about what to automate, they need to understand the downstream revenue implications, the data pipeline dependencies, the customer trust dynamics, and the platform cost structure. If they are only thinking about feature velocity, they are going to make decisions that look great in their quarterly review and create chaos everywhere else.

**ALEX:** That makes sense conceptually, but I want to push on it. Systems thinking is one of those phrases that everyone nods at and nobody operationalizes. How do you actually test for it in a leader? How do you know the difference between someone who genuinely thinks in systems and someone who has just learned to say the right words in a leadership offsite?

**KEVIN:** You put them in a room with a real decision and watch what questions they ask. Here is a concrete example. You present a proposal to automate a customer onboarding workflow using an AI agent. A leader who lacks systems thinking will ask about cost savings and time-to-deploy. A systems thinker will ask: what happens to the customer data that currently gets captured by the human onboarding team during conversations? How does this change our ability to detect churn risk early? What is the fallback if the agent handles an edge case poorly and we damage a high-value account relationship? If the automation fails silently, how long before anyone notices? Those questions reveal whether someone is actually holding the system in their mind or just evaluating a feature request.

**ALEX:** That is a great litmus test. What is the second capability?

**KEVIN:** Technical-commercial fluency. And I want to be precise about what I mean because this is not the same as being technical. You do not need your Chief Revenue Officer to write Python. What you need is a leadership team where every senior person can reason about the mechanics of the systems that drive the business. The CRO needs to understand how the recommendation engine works well enough to know why certain pricing experiments will interact badly with the model's training data. The CTO needs to understand customer lifetime value well enough to make intelligent tradeoffs about where to invest platform reliability versus where to accept some brittleness. This is not about everyone becoming a generalist. It is about everyone having enough fluency in the adjacent domains that they can participate in the real decisions, not just the decisions that have been pre-filtered for their function.

**ALEX:** I have seen companies try to solve this with "translation layers" — hiring business analysts or technical program managers to sit between functions and interpret. Does that work?

**KEVIN:** It is a crutch, and like most crutches, it helps you walk but it also prevents you from building the muscle to walk on your own. Translation layers add latency to every decision, they introduce information loss at every handoff, and worst of all, they let leaders off the hook for developing their own fluency. In an AI-native operating model where decisions need to happen fast and the interdependencies are deep, you cannot afford a two-day turnaround while someone translates between engineering constraints and commercial opportunity. The leaders themselves need to be able to have that conversation directly.

**ALEX:** OK, so systems thinking, technical-commercial fluency — what are numbers three and four?

**KEVIN:** Third is decision velocity. Not speed for its own sake — reckless speed is worse than slow deliberation. What I mean is the ability to make high-quality decisions quickly with incomplete information, and to build organizational systems that distribute decision-making so you are not the bottleneck. In an AI-native org, the volume of decisions goes up dramatically because you have more systems generating more signals and more automated workflows that need governance judgments. A leader who needs to personally review everything, who cannot delegate with confidence, who insists on consensus before acting — that person becomes a chokepoint that paralyzes the entire operation.

**ALEX:** And that connects directly to the fourth one, I am guessing?

**KEVIN:** Exactly. The fourth is governance discipline. This is the counterweight to decision velocity. You need leaders who move fast but who also build and respect guardrails. In an AI-native organization, the consequences of ungoverned decisions compound quickly because automated systems amplify both good and bad choices. A leader with governance discipline establishes clear policies about what the AI systems can and cannot do autonomously, defines escalation thresholds, insists on monitoring and auditability, and treats compliance not as a burden but as an operating advantage. The leaders who scare me are the ones with high velocity and low governance instinct — they will move fast and break things in a context where "things" means customer trust, regulatory standing, and data integrity.

**ALEX:** So if you are a board member or a PE operating partner evaluating a leadership team, you are looking for all four of these in combination. What happens when you find a leader who is strong in two or three but weak in one?

**KEVIN:** It depends which one is missing, but in general, you have two options: develop or replace. Some of these capabilities can be developed — technical-commercial fluency, for example, can be built through structured exposure and cross-functional immersion. Governance discipline can be coached. Systems thinking is harder to develop because it requires a fundamental shift in how someone frames problems, but it is not impossible. Decision velocity is the trickiest because it is partly temperament. If someone is constitutionally risk-averse or consensus-dependent, that is very hard to rewire. My general rule is: you can coach capability gaps, but you cannot coach operating temperament. If the temperament is wrong, you need to make a change, and the longer you wait, the more expensive it gets.

### SEGMENT 2: Role Architecture by Layer (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us move from leadership traits to organizational structure. You have a framework for thinking about role architecture in layers. Walk us through that, because I think this is where a lot of companies get confused — they try to bolt AI responsibilities onto existing org charts and wonder why nothing works.

**KEVIN:** Right, and the reason bolting on fails is that AI-native operations require fundamentally different decision flows than traditional software companies. You cannot just add an "AI team" to the existing structure and expect integration. The org design itself has to change. I think about it in three layers: strategic, operational, and execution. Each layer has distinct responsibilities, distinct decision rights, and distinct accountability structures.

**ALEX:** Start with the strategic layer.

**KEVIN:** The strategic layer is about integrated executive ownership. And the keyword there is "integrated." In a traditional org, you might have a CEO, CTO, CPO, CRO, and CFO each owning their domain with coordination happening through executive team meetings and planning cycles. In an AI-native org, the strategic layer needs to function as a unified system because the decisions at this level — where to deploy AI capabilities, how to allocate platform investment, what customer experiences to automate versus keep human — these decisions cut across every function simultaneously. You cannot make them well in silos and then reconcile later.

**ALEX:** So what does that look like practically? Are you arguing for a single leader, or a different kind of executive team structure?

**KEVIN:** Neither extreme. I am not arguing for a dictator-CEO model where one person makes every call. What I am arguing for is shared strategic context and joint accountability at the executive level. Concretely, this means the executive team operates against a unified AI-native strategy that they co-own — not a "company strategy" that gets interpreted differently by each function. They have shared metrics that matter: autonomous value delivery rate, customer trust scores, platform cost efficiency, revenue per AI-assisted workflow. Every executive is accountable for these numbers, not just the ones that map to their function. When the VP of Engineering sees customer trust declining, that is their problem too, not just the CRO's problem. When the CPO sees platform costs spiking, they need to engage, not shrug and say that is infrastructure's concern.

**ALEX:** That sounds like it requires a level of executive team cohesion that is, frankly, rare. How do you build that?

**KEVIN:** You build it through operating rhythm, not through goodwill. People talk about executive alignment like it is a cultural outcome — "we need to trust each other more." That is true but insufficient. What actually creates alignment is a shared operating cadence where the executive team reviews the same data, debates the same tradeoffs, and makes joint decisions on a predictable schedule. Weekly system health reviews, monthly portfolio allocation decisions, quarterly strategy recalibration. And the data has to be the same data — not each function bringing their own dashboard that tells their own story. One source of truth, one shared view, joint accountability.

**ALEX:** OK, that is the strategic layer. Move us down to the operational layer.

**KEVIN:** The operational layer is where I see the most organizational innovation happening, and frankly where the most confusion exists. This is the layer that translates strategic intent into execution capability. In an AI-native org, I see three critical operational roles emerging. First, AI Product Operations — this is the function that owns the lifecycle of AI-powered products and features, from design through deployment through continuous optimization. It is not a traditional product management role because it requires deep understanding of model behavior, data pipeline management, and automated quality assurance. Second, Revenue Engineering — this is the function that owns the intersection of AI capabilities and revenue generation. They are responsible for things like pricing optimization engines, automated customer expansion workflows, and predictive churn intervention systems. This role did not exist five years ago because the technology to support it was not mature enough. Third, platform reliability leadership — this is the function that ensures the underlying AI infrastructure operates within defined performance, cost, and safety parameters. This is more than traditional SRE. It includes model monitoring, data quality governance, and automated rollback capabilities.

**ALEX:** These are genuinely new roles, not just relabeled versions of existing ones. Where do companies find people to fill them?

**KEVIN:** That is one of the hardest practical challenges, and we will get deeper into hiring strategy later. But the short answer is: you rarely find someone who already has the complete skill set. What you find is someone with strong foundational capability in one dimension — maybe a product manager who genuinely understands ML systems, or a revenue leader who has built data-driven optimization workflows — and then you invest heavily in developing the adjacent skills. The operational layer is where internal development is most critical because these roles require deep institutional knowledge combined with new technical capabilities. You cannot just hire them off the street.

**ALEX:** And the execution layer?

**KEVIN:** The execution layer is cross-functional squads with clear decision rights and defined policy budgets. Let me unpack that. A squad in an AI-native org is not just an agile team with a new label. It is a group that owns an end-to-end outcome — not a feature, not a component, but an outcome. The squad responsible for automated customer onboarding owns the entire pipeline from lead qualification through account activation through early engagement monitoring. They have engineering, product, data science, and customer success capabilities embedded in the squad. And critically, they have decision rights — meaning they can make changes to the AI workflows they own without escalating every modification. They also have a policy budget, which is a defined envelope of risk they are authorized to take. They can experiment within that envelope without approval from above. If they want to go outside the envelope, that triggers an escalation to the operational layer. This structure gives you speed at the edges while maintaining governance at the center.

**ALEX:** The policy budget concept is interesting. How granular does that get? What does it actually look like in practice?

**KEVIN:** Very granular, and it has to be because vague policy budgets are useless. A concrete example: a squad that owns an AI-powered pricing recommendation system might have a policy budget that says they can adjust pricing suggestions within plus or minus five percent of current levels, they can run A/B tests on up to ten percent of traffic, and they can modify the model's feature weights but cannot change the model architecture. Anything outside those bounds requires operational layer approval. The policy budget is reviewed and adjusted quarterly based on the squad's track record. Squads that demonstrate good judgment get wider budgets. Squads that make mistakes that should have been caught get tighter budgets temporarily. It is a trust-but-verify system that scales decision-making capacity without scaling risk.

### SEGMENT 3: Skills and Capability Matrix (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Now let us get into the capability side of this. You are a big advocate for what you call a "skills and capability matrix." I know you have strong opinions about how most companies approach AI training, so let us start there. What is wrong with the typical approach?

**KEVIN:** The typical approach is to send everyone through the same generic "Introduction to AI" course, declare the organization upskilled, and move on. It is box-checking masquerading as capability development. The problem is not that people do not understand what AI is — most professionals have a basic conceptual grasp at this point. The problem is that different roles need fundamentally different AI capabilities, and a one-size-fits-all training program develops none of them to the depth required for actual job performance. It is like trying to make everyone in a hospital better at medicine by sending them all to the same anatomy lecture. The surgeon, the nurse, the radiologist, and the hospital administrator all need different things.

**ALEX:** So walk us through the capability matrix. What are the dimensions, and how do they map to different roles?

**KEVIN:** I work with five core capability dimensions. The first is AI literacy — and by this I do not mean "understanding what a neural network is." I mean the ability to reason about AI system behavior in the context of business operations. For a product manager, AI literacy means understanding why a model might perform well on average but fail catastrophically on a specific customer segment, and being able to ask the right questions of the data science team. For a finance leader, it means understanding how AI-driven variable costs differ from traditional fixed cost structures and what that means for financial planning. Same term, completely different operationalization depending on the role.

**ALEX:** That distinction is subtle but important. What is the second dimension?

**KEVIN:** Workflow design — the ability to look at a business process and identify where AI can add value, where humans should remain in the loop, and how to design the handoffs between automated and human steps. This is arguably the most practically important capability because it is where strategy meets execution. A lot of AI initiatives fail not because the technology does not work, but because the workflow was designed poorly. Someone automated a step that required human judgment, or they kept a human in the loop on a step that was pure computation and just added latency. Good workflow design requires understanding both the technology's capabilities and limitations and the nuances of the business process being transformed.

**ALEX:** And this is a skill that almost nobody has been formally trained on, right? It is an entirely new discipline.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And that is why targeted development matters so much. This is not a skill you can develop through a two-hour webinar. It requires hands-on practice — actually redesigning real workflows, testing them, observing where they break, and iterating. The companies that are doing this well are running internal workflow design labs where cross-functional teams spend real time — not theoretical time, real time with real processes — learning how to think about human-AI workflow architecture.

**ALEX:** What about the other three dimensions?

**KEVIN:** Third is data fluency. Not data science — fluency. The ability to work with data as a core part of your daily job, regardless of your function. This means understanding data quality issues and their downstream consequences, being able to interpret model outputs critically rather than accepting them as truth, and knowing enough about data governance to handle sensitive information responsibly. Fourth is risk handling — the ability to identify, assess, and manage AI-specific risks. This includes bias risk, reliability risk, security risk, and regulatory risk. Different roles face different risk profiles, so the development has to be role-specific. A customer-facing team needs to understand different risks than an infrastructure team. Fifth is experimentation discipline — the ability to design and run rigorous experiments, interpret results correctly, and make evidence-based decisions about whether to scale, iterate, or kill an AI initiative. This one is deceptively hard because most organizations have deeply ingrained habits of making decisions based on intuition, authority, or anecdote rather than evidence.

**ALEX:** So once you have mapped these five dimensions against your roles, how do you actually build the upskilling program? What does targeted development look like compared to the generic training you criticized earlier?

**KEVIN:** You start by assessing where each role currently sits on each dimension — not each person, each role. You define what "good enough" looks like for each role on each dimension. A product manager needs deep workflow design capability but may only need moderate data fluency. A platform engineer needs deep data fluency but may only need moderate workflow design capability. Then you identify the gaps between current state and target state for each role, and you build development paths that close those specific gaps. The development itself should be a mix of structured learning and applied practice. Maybe thirty percent classroom or course-based learning and seventy percent on-the-job application with coaching. And you measure progress with practical assessments — can this person actually redesign a workflow competently? — not with quiz scores or completion certificates.

**ALEX:** How long does this take? If a PE firm is looking at a two to three year hold period and they need the org to be AI-native by exit, what is a realistic timeline for capability development?

**KEVIN:** The honest answer is that you can get meaningful capability improvement in six months if you are focused and disciplined, but full organizational capability transformation takes twelve to eighteen months. The first six months gets you a critical mass of people — maybe thirty to forty percent of the roles that matter most — operating at a competent level. The next six to twelve months is about deepening that capability, expanding it to the rest of the organization, and building the internal development infrastructure so it becomes self-sustaining rather than dependent on external programs. If you are in a PE context with a tight hold period, the implication is that capability development has to start in the first hundred days, not after the technology is deployed. You cannot wait until you have built the AI systems and then figure out whether people can use them. The people development has to run in parallel with — or ideally slightly ahead of — the technology deployment.

### SEGMENT 4: Incentives and Performance Systems (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** This brings us to one of my favorite topics, and one that I think is chronically under-discussed: incentives. You can hire the right people, train them brilliantly, and give them beautiful org structures, and it will all be meaningless if the incentive system is still rewarding the old way of working. How broken are most incentive systems when it comes to AI-native transformation?

**KEVIN:** Profoundly broken, and in ways that are subtle enough that leadership often does not realize it. Here is the core problem: most incentive systems were designed for a world where functional output was the unit of measurement. Engineering is measured on shipping velocity. Sales is measured on bookings. Support is measured on ticket resolution time. Each of those metrics makes sense in isolation, and each of them becomes actively destructive in an AI-native operating model.

**ALEX:** Give me the concrete version of that. How does a reasonable-sounding metric become destructive?

**KEVIN:** Take engineering velocity — story points completed, features shipped, whatever flavor your company uses. In a traditional model, more output is generally better. In an AI-native model, the highest-value engineering work might be building an automated system that replaces the need for fifty future features. The team that builds that system ships one thing instead of fifty, and under the old metric, they look like low performers. Meanwhile, the team that keeps shipping manual features at high velocity looks like stars, even though they are actively working against the transformation. Or take sales bookings. If your comp plan rewards total contract value and your AI systems are enabling a usage-based pricing model that generates more lifetime revenue but lower initial contract values, your sales team is financially incentivized to fight the AI-native pricing model. They will push customers toward legacy contract structures because that is what their comp plan rewards.

**ALEX:** So what should the incentive system look like instead?

**KEVIN:** You need to align compensation and performance evaluation to AI-native outcomes, which means system-level metrics rather than functional output metrics. Let me give you the specific metrics I think matter most. First, autonomous value delivery — what percentage of customer value is being delivered through AI-automated workflows without human intervention? This measures actual transformation progress, not activity. Second, trust retention — are customers maintaining or increasing their engagement and spending as AI systems take over more of the interaction? This is the quality check on autonomous delivery. You can automate everything and destroy the customer relationship, which is why you need both metrics. Third, cost efficiency ratio — what is the fully loaded cost of delivering a unit of customer value, and how is that trending over time? This captures whether the AI investment is actually generating operating leverage.

**ALEX:** How do you handle the transition? You cannot flip the incentive system overnight without creating chaos.

**KEVIN:** You absolutely cannot, and the companies that try usually create a revolt. The transition has to be staged over two to three quarters. In the first quarter, you introduce the new system-level metrics alongside the existing metrics. People still get paid primarily on the old metrics, but the new metrics are visible and discussed. In the second quarter, you shift the weighting — maybe sixty percent old metrics, forty percent new. By the third quarter, you are at full weighting on the new system, but you have also built in a transition guarantee that ensures nobody takes more than a defined percentage hit to their total compensation during the shift. The guarantee costs money, but it buys you organizational buy-in, which is worth far more than the guarantee costs. The biggest mistake I see is companies that try to be "fair" by making the transition purely meritocratic from day one. That sounds principled, but in practice it means people who were excellent performers under the old system suddenly look like poor performers under the new system through no fault of their own, and they leave. You lose your best people first because they have the most options.

**ALEX:** That is a really practical insight. Protect the transition so you do not lose the people you need most to make the transformation work.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And I will add one more thing: the incentive redesign has to be transparent. People need to understand why the metrics are changing, how the new metrics connect to the company's strategy, and what specific behaviors the new system is trying to encourage. If people feel like the comp plan is being changed to save the company money under the guise of transformation, you will get cynicism and resistance. If they understand that the new metrics genuinely reflect how value is created in the AI-native model and that they have a clear path to earning more under the new system, you get engagement.

### SEGMENT 5: Hiring Strategy and Talent Acquisition (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us talk about hiring. We have discussed developing the people you have, but most companies also need to bring in new talent to fill the gaps. When you are hiring for an AI-native organization, what are you looking for that is different from traditional hiring?

**KEVIN:** The single biggest shift is that you are hiring for learning velocity and cross-domain capability rather than deep single-domain expertise. In a traditional hiring model, you look for the person who has done the exact job before — ten years of product management experience, five years in the specific industry, proven track record with exactly this kind of product. In an AI-native hiring model, that person might actually be a worse choice than someone with seven years of experience across product, engineering, and data science who has never done the exact role but has demonstrated the ability to learn fast, operate across boundaries, and deliver under ambiguity.

**ALEX:** That is a hard sell for hiring managers. How do you convince someone to take a bet on a cross-domain generalist over a proven specialist?

**KEVIN:** You reframe what "proven" means. A specialist who has done the exact job before is proven in a world that no longer exists. The role they are being hired for will change significantly within twelve months as AI capabilities evolve. The specialist's deep experience becomes a liability if it makes them rigid. The cross-domain generalist, if they have genuine learning velocity, will adapt to whatever the role becomes. I am not saying domain expertise does not matter — it does. But it should be a secondary filter, not the primary one. The primary filter should be: can this person learn? Can they operate across functional boundaries? Can they make good decisions with incomplete information? Have they shipped something real under conditions of genuine ambiguity?

**ALEX:** Where are you finding these people? Is there a talent pool that is particularly rich for AI-native roles?

**KEVIN:** Several places. First, look at people who have worked in early-stage companies that shipped AI-powered products — not the big tech AI research labs, but the scrappy startups where one person had to be the product manager, the data scientist, and the customer success lead because there was nobody else. Those people have cross-domain muscle memory that is incredibly valuable. Second, look at people coming out of operational roles in companies that have already undergone significant automation — logistics, fintech, adtech. These people understand what it means to work alongside automated systems because they have been doing it. Third, and this is underrated, look at people from consulting or professional services who have worked on AI transformation engagements. They have seen multiple organizations go through this transition, they understand the patterns and the pitfalls, and they often have the cross-functional fluency that comes from having to understand every part of a client's business.

**ALEX:** What about the build versus buy question? How much of this talent should you develop internally versus hiring from outside?

**KEVIN:** My general rule is seventy percent develop, thirty percent hire, but the thirty percent you hire has to be strategically chosen. You hire externally for roles where you have a critical capability gap that cannot be closed quickly through development — usually the operational layer roles we discussed earlier, where you need someone who has actually built AI Product Operations or Revenue Engineering functions before. You develop internally for everything else, because internal talent has institutional knowledge that external hires take six to twelve months to build, and in a transformation context, you do not have six months to wait for someone to learn how the business works. The worst pattern I see is companies that hire a bunch of external AI talent, parachute them into the organization, and expect them to transform things. Those people do not understand the existing systems, the political dynamics, the customer relationships, or the institutional history. They make confident recommendations based on incomplete understanding, alienate the existing team, and leave within eighteen months having accomplished very little.

**ALEX:** That is a vivid and unfortunately common pattern. So the hiring strategy is really about targeted external injection combined with aggressive internal development.

**KEVIN:** Yes, and one more thing: when you do hire externally, optimize for cultural integration ability. The most technically brilliant AI hire in the world is useless if they cannot work effectively within the existing organization. I would rather hire someone who is eighty percent as technically strong but who has demonstrated the ability to earn trust in unfamiliar organizations, build relationships across functions, and bring people along rather than bulldozing them.

### SEGMENT 6: The Frozen Middle and Managing Resistance (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Now we need to talk about what is probably the most politically sensitive topic in AI transformation: the frozen middle. For listeners who have not encountered this term, it refers to the layer of middle management that often becomes the primary bottleneck in organizational change. They are too senior to ignore, too numerous to replace, and too embedded to easily move. Kevin, why is the frozen middle such a persistent problem in AI transformations specifically?

**KEVIN:** Because AI transformation threatens middle management's core value proposition more directly than almost any previous wave of organizational change. Think about what middle managers do in a traditional organization. They aggregate information from the teams below them and synthesize it for the leaders above them. They translate strategic direction from above into operational instructions for below. They manage resource allocation, resolve conflicts, and make judgment calls that require organizational context. Now think about what AI systems are increasingly capable of doing: aggregating information, synthesizing it, translating between levels of abstraction, optimizing resource allocation, and making context-aware judgments. The overlap is enormous. And middle managers can see this, even if nobody is saying it out loud. Their resistance is not irrational — it is a perfectly rational response to a perceived existential threat.

**ALEX:** So how do you deal with it? Because I have seen two extreme responses, and both seem wrong. One is to pretend the problem does not exist and hope middle managers voluntarily get on board. The other is to do a mass layoff of middle management and replace them with AI systems. Neither works.

**KEVIN:** Both of those are terrible, and you are right that they are the two most common approaches. The first — denial — just delays the reckoning and gives the frozen middle time to entrench their resistance through passive sabotage. The second — mass replacement — destroys institutional knowledge, creates a culture of fear that makes everyone else resist the transformation, and usually fails operationally because the AI systems are not ready to absorb all the tacit work that middle managers were doing. The right approach is what I call "role evolution with a safety net." You are honest with middle managers about how their role is going to change. You invest genuinely in helping them develop the new capabilities they need. And you provide a meaningful transition period where their compensation is protected while they are learning.

**ALEX:** What does the evolved middle manager role look like in an AI-native org? If information aggregation and translation are being automated, what is left?

**KEVIN:** Quite a lot, actually, but it is fundamentally different work. The evolved middle manager becomes what I call a "system operator." They are not aggregating information — the AI does that. They are interpreting anomalies, edge cases, and novel situations that the AI flags but cannot resolve. They are not translating strategy into tasks — the AI can decompose objectives into workstreams. They are providing the contextual judgment about which workstreams are actually feasible given the team's current state, the political dynamics, and the customer relationships that do not show up in any data system. They are not managing resource allocation through spreadsheets and meetings — the AI optimizes that. They are making the human judgment calls about team development, interpersonal conflict, motivation, and organizational health that AI systems cannot handle. The role shifts from information processing to judgment application. That is a meaningful, valuable role. But it requires a completely different skill set than most current middle managers have.

**ALEX:** How do you handle the ones who cannot or will not make the transition? Because let us be honest — some percentage of middle managers are going to resist regardless of what support you provide.

**KEVIN:** You have to be honest about that, and you have to plan for it. In my experience, middle managers in a transformation context sort into roughly three groups. About thirty percent are what I call "eager adapters" — they see the change coming, they are excited about learning new skills, and they lean in. These people are your transformation champions, and you should invest heavily in them because they will pull others along. Another forty percent are "cautious followers" — they are nervous but willing to try if they see a clear path and feel supported. These are the people that the safety net is designed for. With the right development program and incentive protection, most of them will make the transition successfully. The remaining thirty percent are "active resisters" — they either cannot develop the new capabilities or have made a deliberate choice not to. For this group, you need a humane but firm exit process. Generous severance, outplacement support, reasonable timelines. But you cannot let them stay in roles where they are blocking the transformation, because the cost of their resistance compounds over time.

**ALEX:** How do you identify which group someone is in? Because I imagine a lot of active resisters are smart enough to look like cautious followers until they have thoroughly sabotaged the initiative.

**KEVIN:** That is exactly what happens, and it is why you need objective signals rather than relying on what people say in meetings. The signals I look for are behavioral, not verbal. Are they actively using the new tools and workflows, or are they finding workarounds? Are they sending their team members to development programs, or finding reasons why the timing does not work? When a pilot shows positive results, do they engage with the data, or do they immediately find reasons why it "would not work at scale" or "is not applicable to my team"? Are they asking questions about how to make the transformation work, or only asking questions about why it might fail? Behavior tells you everything. People who are verbally supportive but behaviorally resistant are the most dangerous because they provide cover for inaction while appearing aligned. You have to watch what people do, not what they say.

**ALEX:** This is hard, uncomfortable organizational work. How do you keep the transformation moving forward while managing all of this human complexity?

**KEVIN:** Speed and momentum are your best friends. The longer a transformation drags on, the more time resistance has to organize and entrench. This does not mean being reckless — it means being decisive. Identify your transformation champions quickly. Get visible wins within the first ninety days. Make the benefits of the new model tangible and undeniable, not theoretical. When people can see with their own eyes that the AI-native workflow is producing better outcomes with less drudgery, the emotional calculus shifts. It is much harder to resist a change that is clearly working than one that is still hypothetical. The companies that get stuck are the ones that spend eighteen months in planning and piloting mode, giving the frozen middle ample time to argue that the transformation is "not ready" for full deployment.

### SEGMENT 7: Culture Design for AI-Native Organizations (9 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us close with culture, which I know you consider the foundation that everything else rests on. We have talked about structure, skills, incentives, hiring, and change management. How does culture tie all of that together, and what does a healthy AI-native culture actually look like?

**KEVIN:** Culture is the set of shared beliefs and norms that determine how people behave when nobody is watching. In an AI-native organization, the culture has to support three things that are in tension with how most organizations operate today. First, genuine experimentation — not "innovation theater" where you have a lab that runs safe experiments, but real willingness to try things that might fail, in production, with real customers, at meaningful scale. Second, radical transparency — making the performance of AI systems visible to everyone, including when they fail, so that the organization builds collective judgment about what is working and what is not. Third, learning velocity — the speed at which the organization converts experience into improved capability. These three elements create a reinforcing cycle: experimentation generates data, transparency makes the data visible, and learning velocity turns the data into better decisions, which improves the next round of experiments.

**ALEX:** Let us take those one at a time. Genuine experimentation first. Most companies claim to value experimentation, but in practice they punish failure. How do you actually change that?

**KEVIN:** You change it by changing what gets rewarded and what gets punished. In most organizations, the implicit reward structure is: success gets celebrated, failure gets investigated. That is deeply anti-experimental because it makes the rational individual choice to avoid any action that might fail. In an AI-native culture, the reward structure needs to be: well-designed experiments that generate learning get celebrated regardless of outcome, and the thing that gets investigated is not failure but failure to experiment. If a team runs a rigorous experiment, it fails, and they extract clear learnings that inform the next iteration — that should be recognized and rewarded just as much as a successful experiment. If a team sits on their hands for a quarter because they were afraid of failure, that should be a performance concern. Practically, this means leaders have to model the behavior. The CEO needs to publicly discuss experiments that failed and what was learned. The executive team needs to allocate budget specifically for experiments that might not work. And when an experiment goes badly, the first question should be "what did we learn?" not "who is responsible?"

**ALEX:** Radical transparency is the second element. What does that mean concretely for AI systems?

**KEVIN:** It means making the performance, behavior, and limitations of AI systems visible across the organization, not just to the team that built them. In most companies today, AI system performance is a black box to everyone except the ML engineering team. The rest of the organization interacts with the outputs but has no visibility into accuracy rates, failure modes, confidence levels, or known limitations. In an AI-native culture, this information is shared openly. There are dashboards that anyone can access showing how every AI system is performing against its defined objectives. When a model degrades or produces unexpected results, that is communicated broadly rather than quietly fixed. The reason this matters is that an organization cannot build collective judgment about AI systems if only a small group has visibility into how they actually work. You need the product team to know when the recommendation engine is struggling so they can adjust the user experience. You need the sales team to know when the lead scoring model's accuracy drops so they can compensate with human judgment. Transparency creates organizational intelligence. Opacity creates organizational fragility.

**ALEX:** And learning velocity — the third element. How do you accelerate how fast an organization learns?

**KEVIN:** Learning velocity is a function of three things: the quality of the feedback loops, the speed at which information moves through the organization, and the willingness to update beliefs based on new evidence. On feedback loops — every AI system, every automated workflow, every experiment needs to have clear, fast feedback mechanisms that tell you whether it is working. Not quarterly reviews. Not annual assessments. Real-time or near-real-time signals that are monitored continuously. On information flow — when one team learns something about how an AI system behaves in their context, that learning needs to propagate to every other team that might benefit from it within days, not months. This requires deliberate mechanisms: internal learning repositories, cross-team knowledge sharing sessions, published post-mortems that are actually read. On updating beliefs — this is the hardest one because it requires people to admit they were wrong and change course. Organizations that have strong hierarchies and cultures of deference struggle with this because updating a belief might mean contradicting a senior leader's previous position. AI-native cultures need to make it safe and normal to say "the data shows our previous assumption was wrong, and here is what we should do differently."

**ALEX:** How does this cultural foundation interact with everything else we have discussed today? The leadership profile, the role architecture, the incentive system?

**KEVIN:** They are all interdependent, and that is actually the most important point I want to leave listeners with. You cannot have genuine experimentation culture if the incentive system punishes failure. You cannot have radical transparency if the leadership team does not model it. You cannot have learning velocity if the role architecture creates silos that block information flow. You cannot have effective AI-native leaders if the culture rewards authority over evidence. Everything we have discussed today — the leadership traits, the org layers, the capability development, the incentive redesign, the hiring strategy, the change management — these are not separate workstreams that you can execute independently. They are a system. You have to evolve them together, or the pieces that you do change will be pulled back toward the old equilibrium by the pieces you did not. That is why AI-native transformation is fundamentally a leadership operating model upgrade, not a technology project. The technology is the easy part. The human system design is where the real transformation happens.

**ALEX:** That is a powerful place to land. Let me try to synthesize the key takeaways for listeners. One: the new leadership profile requires systems thinking, technical-commercial fluency, decision velocity, and governance discipline — all four together. Two: role architecture needs three layers — strategic with integrated executive ownership, operational with new roles like AI Product Ops and Revenue Engineering, and execution with empowered squads that have clear policy budgets. Three: capability development has to be targeted by role across five dimensions, not generic AI training for everyone. Four: incentive systems need to shift to system-level metrics like autonomous value delivery and trust retention, with a protected transition period. Five: hiring should prioritize learning velocity and cross-domain capability, with a seventy-thirty split favoring internal development. Six: the frozen middle requires honest engagement — role evolution with a safety net, not denial or mass replacement. And seven: culture design around experimentation, transparency, and learning velocity is the foundation that makes everything else work.

**KEVIN:** That is an excellent summary. And I would add one final thought: urgency matters. Every month you delay on the people side of AI-native transformation is a month where your technology investments are underperforming because the organization cannot fully exploit them. The companies that win will be the ones that treat leadership and talent transformation with the same rigor and investment they apply to their technology platforms. If you would not deploy a mission-critical system without proper architecture and testing, you should not deploy an AI-native operating model without proper organizational design and capability development. The human system is the mission-critical system.
