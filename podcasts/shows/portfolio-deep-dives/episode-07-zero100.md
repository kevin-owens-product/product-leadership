### The problem Zero100 solves

**NORA:** Let's get to the customer. What's actually hard about being a chief supply chain officer?

**JAMES:** It's one of those jobs that went from back office to front page in a few years. When supply chains work, nobody notices. When they break, it's on the news. Think about the last several years in general: a pandemic, shipping disruption, shortages, tariffs, pressure to cut emissions. All of that lands on the supply chain leader's desk.

**NORA:** And the board starts asking questions.

**JAMES:** Right. And they're hard questions. How resilient are we? What would happen if this supplier failed? Are our inventory levels sensible compared with others in our industry? Should we be spending more on automation, or on AI? How do we find and train people for jobs that didn't exist five years ago? A trade headline we found talks about an AI talent dash in supply chain, with companies scrambling to upskill staff.

**NORA:** So where does a supply chain leader go for answers today?

**JAMES:** A few places, in general. Their own teams and data, which only tell them about themselves. Big consultancies, which are expensive and project-based. Analyst firms, which publish research and run benchmarks. Software vendors, who have a point of view but also something to sell. And their peers, which is often the most valuable source and the hardest to access in a structured way.

**NORA:** Peers being other people doing the same job at other companies.

**JAMES:** Yes. The head of supply chain at a consumer goods company would love to know how her counterpart at another consumer goods company handled a port closure, or how they structured their planning team. Those conversations happen at conferences, over dinner, through personal networks. But they're informal, patchy and hard to turn into numbers.

**NORA:** And Zero100's pitch is to make that structured.

**JAMES:** That's how I'd put it. It combines three things. A peer network, so leaders can meet and talk. Community benchmarks, so the network's data can be compared. And research, so analysts can turn that data and those conversations into guidance. The homepage promises, quote, personalized insights in days, not quarters. And it describes its guidance as operator-led and driven by AI.

**NORA:** "Days, not quarters." That's a speed claim.

**JAMES:** It's a direct shot at traditional research, where a big report might take months to produce. And it's exactly where AI should help, because the slow part of research is gathering, cleaning and writing, which is analyst time.

**NORA:** You said the buyer is senior. Who actually signs the cheque?

**JAMES:** We don't have direct evidence, but our inference from the client list is that a chief supply chain officer or COO buys it from an enterprise budget, much like other executive memberships. And there's a second type of buyer. In February 2025, Körber Supply Chain Software, a supply chain software vendor, announced that it had joined the Zero100 community. A trade headline also says Puma joined, though we couldn't confirm the date. So the community includes both big operating companies and vendors who sell to them.

### Inside the membership

**NORA:** Let's go deeper on what a member actually gets. Walk me through it.

**JAMES:** With a caveat that Zero100 doesn't publish a full menu or prices, here's what the public material describes. First, research. Zero100 publishes analysis on supply chain and operations topics, and its homepage highlights AI and transformation strategy as a theme. Second, surveys and benchmarks. For example, it promotes a State of the COO 2026 survey of one hundred operations leaders. Third, the community and events. Its homepage mentions events in places like Phoenix, San Diego and Dublin, on top of its London and New York base. And fourth, guidance, which sounds like more personalised advice for members.

**NORA:** A survey of a hundred people. Is that a lot?

**JAMES:** It's modest in statistical terms, but in this world it can be valuable, because it's a hundred very senior people. A survey of a hundred chief operating officers can tell you things a survey of ten thousand random workers can't. The trick is being honest about what a sample that size can and can't support.

**NORA:** And the benchmarks. What are they, in plain terms?

**JAMES:** A benchmark is a comparison. Say a member wants to know whether their planning team is the right size for a company of their revenue and complexity. If enough members share how they're structured, the research team can show the range: here's the typical size, here's the spread, here's where you sit. Zero100 describes these as community benchmarks, which suggests the data comes from members themselves.

**NORA:** Which is a nice loop. Members give data and get insight back.

**JAMES:** It's a powerful loop when it works. The more members, the better the benchmarks. The better the benchmarks, the more valuable membership is. Economists call that a network effect. But it only works if members trust that their data is handled carefully, and if enough of them actually contribute.

**NORA:** And the events?

**JAMES:** Events do two jobs. They're part of the value, because senior people want to meet peers. And they're a sales engine, because a great event is how you persuade the next company to join. The Estée Lauder gathering of about forty leaders is a good example of the format: a member company hosts, Zero100 convenes, and peers spend a day on shared problems.

**NORA:** So if I had to describe the product, it's part research firm, part club, part data business.

**JAMES:** That's a very good summary. And the balance between those three matters a lot for the AI story. A club is hard to automate. Research is easier to speed up. A data business is where AI can really multiply value.

### A member's quarter, a hypothetical

**NORA:** Let's make this concrete. Invent a member for me. Not a real person or company.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's call her Priya. She's the chief supply chain officer at a made-up mid-sized consumer goods company. They make household products, sell through big retailers, and have factories in two countries and a lot of contract manufacturers. She has a team of several hundred people and a chief executive who has just come back from a board meeting with three questions.

**NORA:** What are the three questions?

**JAMES:** One: our inventory has crept up, is that normal for our industry right now? Two: our competitors are talking about AI in planning, are we behind? Three: we're struggling to hire planners, what are others doing about it?

**NORA:** And without something like Zero100?

**JAMES:** Priya asks her team for an internal analysis, which tells her about her own company. She calls two or three peers she knows, who are friendly but busy. She might commission a consultancy, which takes weeks and costs real money. Or she reads analyst reports that are useful but not specific to her situation. She ends up giving the board a thoughtful answer built mostly on her own judgment.

**NORA:** And with a membership?

**JAMES:** In the model Zero100 describes, she can go to its research and benchmarks first. She looks at what the community data says about inventory trends in consumer goods. She reads its work on AI in planning, which is the kind of topic its homepage highlights. She asks the Zero100 team for a personalised view, which is the "days, not quarters" promise. And at the next event she sits at a table with three other chief supply chain officers who have the same planner shortage, and hears what's actually working.

**NORA:** So she walks into the next board meeting with comparisons, not just opinions.

**JAMES:** That's the value. "Here's where we sit relative to peers, here's what leaders in our industry are doing, here's my recommendation." For a senior executive, that's career-relevant. It makes her look prepared and makes her decisions more defensible.

**NORA:** And where does it fall short in that story, today?

**JAMES:** In my view, the bottleneck is the personalised piece. If Priya's question needs an analyst to dig through data and write a tailored answer, that takes analyst time. If fifty members ask similar questions in the same week, the research team becomes the constraint. Which is exactly where an AI research assistant could change the economics, and we'll come back to that.

**NORA:** And I suppose the community piece doesn't scale in the same way either.

**JAMES:** Right. You can't automate the dinner where Priya meets her peers. But you can make sure that when she gets back to her desk, the follow-up data and research are waiting for her, rather than in a queue.

### The market, competitors and the economics of research

**NORA:** Let's talk market. How big is the market for supply chain research and executive networks?

**JAMES:** We didn't find a reliable figure, so we're not going to give one. It's part of a broader world of business research, advisory and executive networks, which is large in general, but carving out the supply chain slice with a number we trust wasn't possible from public sources.

**NORA:** Fair. Who does Zero100 compete with?

**JAMES:** Our research here was thinner than usual, so I'll separate what's sourced from what's general knowledge. Sourced: a business database page compares Zero100 with Manhattan Associates, which is a supply chain software company. But that's a comparison page, not a competitor list, and Manhattan sells software, not peer networks. Körber, as we said, is a member, not a rival.

**NORA:** And the general knowledge?

**JAMES:** Speaking generally and cautiously, the obvious large competitor for this kind of business is Gartner, the big research and advisory firm, which publishes supply chain research and runs executive programmes and peer communities. Then there are the large consultancies, which produce benchmarks and thought leadership partly to win consulting work. There are other analyst firms. And there are executive membership organisations in general. We didn't find a sourced list of direct peer-network rivals, so I won't name specific ones.

**NORA:** So Zero100 is a young company competing, in part, with some very big and established names.

**JAMES:** Yes. And that's both a challenge and an opportunity. Big incumbents have scale, brand and huge research libraries. A young company can move faster, focus tightly on one executive role, and build its research process around AI from the start rather than retrofitting it. That's the theory, at least.

**NORA:** What about vendors?

**JAMES:** That's a really interesting dynamic. Software vendors increasingly want access to buyer communities, so they join or sponsor them, like Körber did. But vendors also run their own user groups and customer communities. If the big software companies build strong communities of their own, an independent network has to offer something they can't, which is neutrality. A chief supply chain officer trusts a peer comparison more if it isn't run by someone selling her software.

**NORA:** So neutrality is a selling point that also limits how much you can lean on vendor money.

**JAMES:** That's a good way of putting it. Vendor members are useful revenue, but if the community starts to feel like a sales channel, the executives may drift away. Getting that balance right is one of the key judgments in this business.

**NORA:** I want to spend a few minutes on how research businesses make money, because I think it explains the AI opportunity.

**JAMES:** Agreed. In general terms, research and membership businesses tend to sell subscriptions. A company pays a yearly fee for access, often for a set number of people, sometimes with extra fees for events, advisory time or custom work. Zero100 doesn't publish prices, so I'm describing the category, not its actual terms.

**NORA:** And the costs?

**JAMES:** Mostly people. Analysts who research and write, community managers who organise events and look after members, and sales staff who bring in new members. In a business with more than fifty employees whose main product is research and guidance, our inference is that analyst time is both the biggest cost and the biggest limit on growth.

**NORA:** So if you want more members, you need more analysts.

**JAMES:** In the traditional model, roughly, yes. Each new member brings more questions, more requests for tailored work, more expectations of attention. That makes research firms grow in a fairly straight line: revenue and headcount rising together. That's fine, but it's not the kind of growth investors get most excited about.

**NORA:** And the other thing that matters is retention.

**JAMES:** Huge. A subscription business lives or dies on renewals. If members renew year after year, each new member adds to a growing base. If they drift away after a year, you're constantly refilling a leaky bucket. And what drives renewal in executive memberships, in general, is perceived value: did I use it, did it help me, did my boss notice?

**NORA:** Which comes back to Priya walking into the board meeting prepared.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Every time a member gets a useful answer quickly, renewal gets more likely. Every time they forget they even have the membership, it gets less likely.

**NORA:** So where does AI fit into these economics?

**JAMES:** In two places. On the cost side, AI can make each analyst far more productive: finding data, drafting analysis, summarising what members are saying. That breaks the straight line between headcount and revenue. On the value side, AI can let members get answers themselves, at any time, which increases how often they use the membership. More usage, in general, means better renewal. Both of those are inferences, but they're the logic of the opportunity.

**NORA:** And the danger, of course, is that AI does the same thing for everyone else.

**JAMES:** That's the threat in a nutshell. If a general AI tool can answer a decent share of benchmark-style questions for free, a paid research product has to be clearly better. Which, in my view, means it has to rest on data the free tools don't have.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about the challenges. What do you think keeps Zero100's leadership up at night?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, scale. More than fifty employees is still small, and it's competing for the attention of executives who are approached by everyone. Every year it has to prove it deserves a place in their calendar and their budget.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, the research model itself is under pressure from AI. If a supply chain leader can ask a general AI assistant to summarise trends in inventory management, some of the value of a written research report gets squeezed. That doesn't kill the business, but it raises the bar. Research has to be more specific, more data-driven and more personal to justify a premium.

**NORA:** Third.

**JAMES:** Third, proving the AI claim. As we said, the positioning is ahead of the visible product. In the short term that's fine. In the long term, when the next investor or buyer does diligence, they'll ask what AI product exists, how many members use it and what it does to retention.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**JAMES:** Key people. In research businesses, members often follow particular experts. If a small number of research leaders carry the brand, losing one is a real risk. Building a bench, and building systems that capture the firm's knowledge rather than leaving it in people's heads, is important. Interestingly, AI can help with that too.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Transparency. Zero100 publishes no pricing and no member numbers. For a private company that's normal, and I'm not criticising it for that. But it means outsiders, including potential members, can't easily judge value. Our inference is that sales depends heavily on reputation and word of mouth, which works well in a tight executive community, but has limits as you try to grow into new regions and segments.

**NORA:** There's also the small brand thing you mentioned.

**JAMES:** Yes, Zero100 versus Z100 Community Limited. Minor, but in a world where people search for you, consistency helps. It's an easy fix.

**NORA:** And the time question for the owners.

**JAMES:** Right. LLCP has been involved for under two years. We don't know its intended holding period. But in general, private equity investors want to see a value creation plan show results within the first few years. So there's a natural push to move from promise to evidence fairly quickly.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's move to opportunities. If you were on Zero100's board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** The biggest one, in my view, is an AI research assistant built on Zero100's own research, benchmarks and survey data, offered to members as a self-serve product. Members ask questions in plain language and get answers grounded in the community's data, with the sources shown. We'll spend a chapter on that.

**NORA:** What else?

**JAMES:** Second, turning benchmarks into a product of their own. Today benchmarks seem to be part of the research and events. They could become subscription data feeds or dashboards, where a member can see where they sit against peers at any time. That moves value from one-off moments to something members use every week.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Pricing tiers. If there's currently one main membership, there's room for options between event-only access and full advisory membership, perhaps with usage-based access to benchmarks or the assistant. That lets Zero100 sell to more of each member company: not just the chief supply chain officer, but the planning director, the procurement lead and so on. All of this is our inference, because we don't know today's structure.

**NORA:** And vendors?

**JAMES:** A formal sponsor programme for vendors is a natural step. Körber already joined. A structured tier, with clear rules on what vendors get and don't get, could bring in revenue while protecting neutrality.

**NORA:** Geography?

**JAMES:** Zero100 already has events in the United States and Europe, in places like Phoenix, San Diego and Dublin, on top of London and New York. Expanding to the Asia-Pacific region and to the American mid-market, meaning operations leaders at companies smaller than the giants on the client list, are both plausible. An AI assistant makes that more affordable, because you can serve more members without a matching rise in analysts.

**NORA:** And acquisitions?

**JAMES:** None so far. But a tuck-in of a survey or benchmarking data business would widen the data asset, which feeds the AI opportunity. That's our view of a sensible target, not anything the company has said.

**NORA:** You mentioned talent earlier, the scramble to upskill. Is there an opportunity there?

**JAMES:** I think there's a real one, although it's our inference rather than anything Zero100 has announced. If supply chain teams need to learn new skills, especially around AI and data, then a trusted community for those leaders is a natural place to offer learning. Short programmes for planners and analysts, peer cohorts for new managers, briefings for leaders on what AI can and can't do in operations.

**NORA:** That would widen the audience beyond the top executive.

**JAMES:** It would. Today the visible buyer is the chief supply chain officer. Learning products could reach the hundreds of people who work for her. And that has a nice side effect. When a whole team uses the membership, not just one person, it becomes much harder to cancel.

**NORA:** Is there a danger of spreading too thin?

**JAMES:** Definitely. A company of fifty-odd people can't do everything at once. If I had to rank these, I'd put the AI research assistant and benchmark products first, because they build on the core data asset. Pricing tiers and vendor sponsorship next, because they're mostly commercial design. Learning and geography after that. And acquisitions only when there's a clear data business that fits.

**NORA:** And cross-selling advisory work?

**JAMES:** Possibly. Members who trust the research may want help putting it into practice, for example on digital transformation. The care point is that advisory work is people-heavy, so it can pull the company back towards the straight-line growth model. I'd treat it as a complement, not the core.

### The best AI bet and the first 100 days

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months, what is it?

**JAMES:** The member research assistant. It plays directly to the strongest asset, the proprietary community data and research. It speeds up the "days, not quarters" promise towards minutes. It reduces pressure on analysts. And it gives members a reason to use their membership every week, which should help renewal. I want to be clear that we haven't seen any numbers on hours saved or revenue, so the size of the prize is unknown. But the logic is strong.

**NORA:** How is that different from just using a general chatbot?

**JAMES:** Three ways. The data: it would answer from Zero100's benchmarks and member surveys, which no general tool has. The context: it would know the member's industry and size, so answers are relevant. And the trust: it would show where each answer comes from, and flag when the data is thin. A general tool might give a confident answer with no basis. A good research assistant should say, here's what our community data shows, here's the sample, and here's where you should talk to an analyst.

**NORA:** So how would you run it? Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: get the foundations in order. Inventory all the research, benchmarks and survey data. Decide what can be used and under what permissions, because members gave that data on certain terms. Write a clear data and AI policy for members. And pick a small group of design partner members, perhaps eight to ten, who are keen and will give honest feedback.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Build an internal version first. Give Zero100's own analysts the assistant and let them use it to answer real member questions, checking every answer before it goes out. That does two things. It speeds up the analysts straight away. And it shows where the assistant is wrong, which you want to find out in private, not in front of a chief supply chain officer at a household-name company.

**NORA:** The shadow mode idea again.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Then days sixty to a hundred: open it to the design partner members, with clear labelling, sources on every answer and an easy route to a human analyst. Measure three things: how often they use it, how fast they get answers compared with before, and how they rate the answers. At day one hundred, decide on wider rollout and on how to price it, based on evidence.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** One person, ideally a product leader who understands both research and technology. Right now the public leadership is research-led, so this may mean a new hire. And they need the chief research officer's backing, because the research team's trust in the tool is what makes it credible to members.

### The assistant, step by step

**NORA:** Let's replay Priya's board questions with the assistant in place. Still hypothetical.

**JAMES:** Sure. It's Monday afternoon. Priya opens the Zero100 member portal and types: how have inventory levels moved for consumer goods companies of our size over the past year, and what are leaders doing about it?

**NORA:** And what comes back?

**NORA:** So the assistant becomes a front door to the community, not just to documents.

**JAMES:** That's the bigger idea. The best version doesn't replace the people part of the membership, it routes members into it more effectively. Ask a question, get the data, and get introduced to the right peer or analyst.

**JAMES:** A lot. Every question members ask is a signal of what they care about. If two hundred members ask about planner shortages in a month, that's the next research report and the next event theme. So the assistant also becomes a sensor for the research agenda. ### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Zero100?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, if you sell expertise, your data is your defence against AI. General AI tools are trained on public information. Anything you know that's not public, your members' benchmarks, your surveys, your case history, is what lets you stay ahead. Businesses selling knowledge should ask: what do we know that nobody else does?

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, make sure the AI claim and the AI product are on the same timeline. Saying AI-native on your homepage is easy. Every competitor will say it soon. The winners will be the ones who can show a member a working tool. If your positioning is ahead of your product, close the gap before a buyer, a customer or a competitor points it out.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, use AI to scale people, not replace them. In a community business, the human relationships are the product. The smart move is to let AI handle the searching, summarising and drafting, so that the experts and community managers spend more time with members. That's true of a lot of professional services, from law to accountancy to consulting.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, trust is a feature. When customers share data with you, how you handle it is part of what they're buying. A clear, published policy on data and AI, and the security credentials to back it up, can win business. It's especially true when your customers are big, cautious companies with lawyers who read the fine print.

**NORA:** I'd add one from the Priya story. Think about what makes your customer look good to their boss.

**JAMES:** That's a great addition. Executive products succeed when they make the buyer more effective in front of the people they report to. If your product helps your customer walk into a board meeting with answers, they'll fight to keep it in the budget.

**NORA:** Is there a lesson here for investors too?

**JAMES:** I think so. When you invest in a business whose positioning includes AI, ask early what AI exists today, what's planned, and how you'll know it's working. Build the measures in from the start, so that by the time of any sale, the story is backed by evidence.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks. Give me the list.

**JAMES:** One, AI disruption of the research model. General tools answer more benchmark-style questions over time, and analyst-written content loses pricing power. Two, commoditised benchmarks. Consultancies and analyst firms often publish survey data cheaply or for free to win other business. Three, vendor-run communities crowding out independent ones. Four, dependence on a few key research leaders. And five, execution: building a credible AI product with a small team, while running events and research at the same time.

**NORA:** Is there a data risk?

**JAMES:** Yes. If the company builds an AI assistant on member data and gets the permissions or security wrong, that's a serious trust problem. In a tight community of senior executives, news like that travels fast. So I'd put data governance not just under risks, but at the start of any AI plan.

**NORA:** Walk me through how that could go wrong, concretely.

**JAMES:** Imagine, hypothetically, a member asks the assistant a question about inventory benchmarks, and the answer is so specific that another member's data can be worked out from it. Say there are only three companies in a segment, and the assistant shows the average. Two of them could, in principle, work out the third. That's a known problem in benchmarking in general, and it gets easier to trip over when an AI is generating answers on the fly.

**NORA:** So how do you prevent it?

**JAMES:** Standard techniques exist. Minimum group sizes, so you never show a figure built from fewer than a set number of companies. Rounding and ranges instead of exact numbers. Clear rules on what the assistant is allowed to reveal. And testing, where your own team deliberately tries to make the assistant leak information before any member sees it. None of this is exotic, but it has to be designed in from day one.

**NORA:** And the other side of governance, the question of whether member data trains AI models.

**JAMES:** That's the question every big company's lawyers will ask. The cleanest answer, in my view, is that member data is used to answer member questions within Zero100's own system, under clear terms, and is not used to train outside models. Whatever the answer is, it should be written down, published and easy to explain.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** Timing and proof. A growth investment is a bet that the company can scale. The proof points a future investor or buyer would look for, in our view, are growth in members, strong renewal, more revenue per member, and a product, AI or otherwise, that makes the business less dependent on adding analysts for every new client.

**NORA:** So what should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, whether Zero100 launches a named AI product for members, and whether it publishes any usage numbers. Second, any confirmed detail on the King's Award, which would be a nice validation. Third, whether it makes its first acquisition, and whether that's a data business. And fourth, whether it publishes a data and AI policy for members, which would signal it's taking the trust side seriously.

**NORA:** And if none of those happen?

**JAMES:** Then the company may still be growing well, but quietly. Private companies don't owe the public updates. But for one that leads with AI, I'd expect to see evidence surface within the next year or so.
