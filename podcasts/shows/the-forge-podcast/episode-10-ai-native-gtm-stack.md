### SEGMENT 1: From Linear Funnel to Learning Loops (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** The traditional GTM model that most B2B software companies run on is essentially a funnel. Marketing generates awareness and leads at the top, sales qualifies and closes in the middle, and customer success handles onboarding and retention at the bottom. It has been the dominant mental model for twenty years. Kevin, why does that model break down specifically in the context of AI-native products?

**KEVIN:** The funnel assumes a one-directional flow and discrete handoffs. A lead becomes an opportunity, an opportunity becomes a customer, a customer becomes a renewal. Each stage has its own team, its own metrics, and its own tooling. The problem with AI-native products is that the product itself is generating signals and creating value at every single one of those stages simultaneously. A free-trial user who hits a high-autonomy workflow in week one is telling you something enormously valuable about their willingness to pay, their use case fit, and their expansion potential -- and that signal does not fit neatly into a funnel stage. The product is not waiting for the sales team to qualify the lead. The product is qualifying the lead through behavior.

**ALEX:** So what replaces the funnel? You have talked before about learning loops. Can you walk through what that actually looks like in practice?

**KEVIN:** Instead of a linear funnel, think of five interconnected loops: acquire, activate, automate, expand, and refine. Each loop feeds the others. In the acquire loop, you are using product intelligence -- not just firmographic data -- to identify and attract accounts that match your highest-value usage patterns. In the activate loop, you are measuring not just whether someone logged in, but whether they reached a meaningful autonomy threshold where the AI is actually doing work for them. The automate loop is where you track whether the customer is letting the system operate with increasing independence, which is where the real value compounds. Expand is about identifying moments where the customer's trust and usage patterns suggest they are ready for more -- more workflows, more seats, more autonomous capability. And refine is the feedback mechanism where everything you learn from downstream loops improves your upstream loops. What you learn from expansion patterns makes your acquisition targeting better. What you learn from automation adoption makes your activation playbooks sharper.

**ALEX:** That is a significant structural shift. It means your marketing team needs to understand product telemetry, your sales team needs to understand autonomy curves, and your CS team needs to be feeding intelligence back to both. How do you actually instrument this without drowning in data?

**KEVIN:** You start with what I call commercial-product events -- the handful of signals at each loop that actually predict revenue outcomes. In the activate loop, it might be three or four specific behaviors that correlate with conversion. In the automate loop, it might be the percentage of a workflow that the customer has delegated to the AI versus doing manually. You do not need to track everything. You need to track the signals that connect product behavior to commercial outcomes, and you need to make those signals visible across all three GTM functions. The mistake most companies make is instrumenting product analytics for the product team and commercial analytics for the revenue team and never connecting them. In an AI-native GTM stack, those are the same data set.

**ALEX:** So the learning loop model is not just a philosophical shift, it is an instrumentation and data architecture decision from day one.

**KEVIN:** Absolutely. And the teams that get this right early have a compounding advantage, because every cycle through those loops makes every other loop more efficient. The teams that do not get it right end up with marketing optimizing for volume, sales optimizing for close rate, and CS optimizing for retention -- three functions optimizing three different objectives with three different data sets, none of which reflect what the product is actually doing for the customer.

### SEGMENT 2: The Unified Revenue Graph (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** This brings us naturally to the data model underneath all of this. You have a concept you call the Unified Revenue Graph. Before we get into the details, can you explain why the standard CRM-plus-product-analytics stack is insufficient here?

**KEVIN:** Traditional CRM systems like Salesforce were designed around accounts, contacts, and opportunities. Product analytics tools like Amplitude or Mixpanel were designed around users, events, and sessions. The problem is that the entity relationships between these two worlds are either nonexistent or stitched together with brittle integrations. An account in your CRM might have fifty users in your product, each with different roles, different usage patterns, different autonomy levels. The CRM does not know which of those users is the champion driving adoption and which is the skeptic who has not logged in. The product analytics tool does not know which of those users is attached to a seven-figure renewal or which is on a team that just got budget approval for expansion. You end up with two partial pictures of reality and no coherent view of the commercial relationship.

**ALEX:** So the Unified Revenue Graph is the answer to that fragmentation. Walk us through the entity model. What are the core nodes and relationships?

**KEVIN:** The graph connects six primary entity types. First is the account -- the commercial entity, the company you have a contract with. Second is the user role -- not just the individual person, but their function within the account. A finance analyst and a finance director at the same company have different value profiles and different decision-making authority. Third is the workflow -- the specific job or process the product is being used for. This is critical because AI-native products often serve multiple workflows within a single account, and each workflow can be at a different maturity stage. Fourth is the autonomy level -- a measure of how much the AI is doing versus the human for each workflow. This is a dynamic property that changes over time and is one of the strongest predictors of expansion readiness and retention risk. Fifth is trust indicators -- the signals that tell you how much confidence the users have in the AI's outputs. Are they accepting recommendations or overriding them? Are they expanding the scope of delegation or pulling back? Sixth is revenue events -- every commercial action from initial contract through expansion, renewal, and churn.

**ALEX:** That is a rich graph. Let me make sure I understand the power of it. You are saying that by connecting all six of those entity types, you can answer questions that neither CRM nor product analytics can answer alone. Can you give me a concrete example?

**KEVIN:** Here is one that comes up constantly. Which accounts have high usage and high autonomy but are commercially under-monetized? In a traditional stack, you would need someone to manually cross-reference product data with contract data, and by the time they did, the moment might have passed. With a revenue graph, that is a standing query. You can also answer the inverse: which accounts are paying at a premium tier but showing declining trust indicators and falling autonomy levels? That is a churn risk that no NPS survey will catch in time, but the behavioral data is screaming it months before the renewal conversation. Another one: which user roles within expanding accounts are most correlated with successful land-and-expand patterns? That tells your marketing team exactly who to target, your sales team exactly who to multi-thread into, and your CS team exactly who to invest onboarding resources in.

**ALEX:** How does an organization actually build this? Are we talking about a custom data warehouse, a graph database, a layer on top of existing tools?

**KEVIN:** The implementation varies, but the architecture usually involves a shared event schema that both product and commercial systems write to, a processing layer that resolves entity relationships -- matching users to accounts, workflows to revenue lines -- and a query layer that GTM teams can access without needing to file a data engineering ticket every time they want an insight. Some companies build this on top of a warehouse like Snowflake or BigQuery with a semantic layer. Some use purpose-built customer data platforms. The technology matters less than the commitment to a single shared entity model. The worst outcome is when marketing has its own data model, sales has its own in the CRM, and product has its own in the analytics tool, and someone is trying to reconcile them in a spreadsheet every quarter. That is where most companies are today, and it is untenable for AI-native GTM.

**ALEX:** And the graph is not static -- it is continuously updating as product behavior evolves.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. The autonomy levels change. The trust indicators shift. New workflows get adopted. Revenue events fire. The graph is a living representation of your commercial relationships, and every GTM motion should be reading from it and writing back to it. When a sales rep logs a conversation that surfaces a new use case, that should update the graph. When the product detects a new workflow being configured, that should update the graph. When CS identifies a risk signal in a quarterly business review, that should update the graph. It is the single source of truth for your entire revenue operation.

### SEGMENT 3: AI in Demand Generation and Pipeline Building (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us move up the stack to demand generation and pipeline. This is where a lot of companies first encounter AI in their GTM -- using AI tools for email generation, ad targeting, lead scoring. But I suspect your view of AI-native demand gen goes quite a bit deeper than bolting ChatGPT onto your outbound sequences.

**KEVIN:** It does, and I want to be direct about the current state. Most companies using AI in demand gen are using it as a content accelerator -- generating more emails, more ad copy, more blog posts, faster. That is fine as far as it goes, but it is a productivity play, not a strategic one. AI-native demand gen means three things. First, segmentation that is grounded in product behavior, not just firmographic attributes. Instead of targeting companies with 500 employees in financial services, you are targeting companies whose usage patterns in adjacent products suggest they have the specific workflow complexity that your AI excels at. Second, personalization that reflects actual value delivery, not just persona assumptions. When you reach out to a prospect, the message is informed by what you know about how similar companies achieved specific outcomes, because you have that data from your revenue graph. Third, qualification that uses product engagement as the primary signal, not self-reported interest.

**ALEX:** That third point is huge. Traditional lead qualification is essentially asking people how interested they are and hoping they tell the truth. Product-led qualification flips that entirely.

**KEVIN:** Right. In an AI-native product with a freemium or trial motion, the product is the qualification engine. You do not need to ask a prospect whether they have budget or authority or need -- you can observe whether they engaged with a high-value workflow, whether they configured the AI for their specific use case, whether they invited colleagues, whether they reached an autonomy threshold that suggests the product is delivering real value. Those behavioral signals are dramatically more predictive than any BANT qualification framework. And this is where quality gates become essential. Just because AI can generate a thousand personalized outbound emails does not mean it should. You need gates that ask: does this prospect match a validated ideal customer profile based on behavioral data? Has the personalization been reviewed for accuracy and tone, especially for enterprise prospects where a poorly calibrated message can burn a relationship permanently? Is the timing aligned with a product engagement signal, or are we just spraying and praying with better grammar?

**ALEX:** Talk to me about the quality gates in more detail. I think this is where a lot of teams go wrong -- they get excited about volume and lose discipline on quality.

**KEVIN:** There are three quality gates I recommend. The first is a fit gate: does this account or contact match a pattern that has historically converted to high-value customers? This is where the revenue graph pays dividends, because you can define fit based on behavioral similarity to your best accounts, not just demographic overlap. The second is a readiness gate: is there a signal suggesting this prospect is actively in a buying or evaluation window? Product engagement is the strongest readiness signal, but it can also be hiring patterns, technology changes, or funding events. The third is an appropriateness gate: is the proposed outreach contextually appropriate? This is where human review is most important, particularly for enterprise accounts. AI can draft the message, but a human should validate that the framing, the references, and the ask are calibrated to the relationship stage and the stakeholder level. The companies that skip that third gate in pursuit of speed end up with high activity metrics and terrible pipeline quality.

**ALEX:** How do you think about the handoff from AI-generated pipeline to human-driven pipeline? Is there a point where the AI should step back and the human should step in?

**KEVIN:** The handoff should be triggered by signal quality, not by stage labels. When a prospect shows high-intent behavior -- configuring a complex workflow, running the product against their own data, engaging multiple stakeholders -- that is a signal that a human relationship can add value that the AI channel cannot. The human brings judgment about organizational dynamics, competitive positioning, and deal strategy that AI is not good at yet. Conversely, for lower-intent signals -- someone downloading a white paper, attending a webinar -- the AI-driven nurture sequence is probably more efficient and more consistent than a human SDR follow-up. The mistake is treating the handoff as a binary, one-time event. In practice, there should be fluid movement between AI-driven and human-driven engagement based on the evolving signal from the product and the relationship.

### SEGMENT 4: AI-Native Sales Execution (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us get into the sales motion itself. In a traditional SaaS company, the sales rep is the primary vehicle for communicating product value, building relationships, and negotiating commercial terms. In an AI-native company where the product is demonstrating its own value in real time, how does the sales role change?

**KEVIN:** The sales role shifts from value narrator to value orchestrator. In the old model, the rep tells the prospect what the product can do and asks them to believe it. In the AI-native model, the product is already showing the prospect what it can do, often before a rep is even involved. So the rep's job is not to explain the product -- the product is explaining itself. The rep's job is to understand the customer's strategic context well enough to connect the value the product is already demonstrating to the business outcomes the customer cares about. That is a fundamentally more consultative and higher-judgment role. It requires reps who can read product engagement data, understand what it means when a prospect's autonomy level is rising or falling, and translate that into a commercial conversation about how to structure a deal that aligns incentives.

**ALEX:** So what does that look like day to day? Give me the picture of a sales rep working in this model.

**KEVIN:** The rep's morning starts with a dashboard that shows product-led signals across their book of business. Not a list of tasks from the CRM -- a live view of what is happening inside accounts. They see that a mid-market prospect who started a trial last week just configured a second workflow and the AI autonomy score jumped from 20 percent to 65 percent. That is an activation signal that says this account is getting real value fast. The rep reaches out -- not with a generic check-in, but with a specific message acknowledging the workflow they configured, referencing a similar customer who expanded from that starting point, and proposing a conversation about how to extend that value to other teams. Compare that to the traditional model where the rep is calling to ask if the prospect had a chance to look at the product, with no idea what actually happened. The signal-driven approach respects the buyer's time, demonstrates that you understand their usage, and positions the rep as a strategic advisor rather than a quota-chasing stranger.

**ALEX:** What about expansion within existing accounts? That is where a lot of AI-native companies see their biggest revenue growth.

**KEVIN:** Expansion is where product-led signals are most powerful, and it is also where the relationship between sales and customer success needs to be tightest. The traditional model is that CS owns the relationship post-sale and sales comes back in when there is an upsell opportunity. In an AI-native model, expansion signals are continuous. A new team within an account starts using the product. An existing team delegates a higher-stakes workflow to the AI. A power user starts training colleagues. These are all expansion triggers, and the question of whether sales or CS acts on them should be determined by the nature of the expansion, not by an arbitrary organizational boundary. For usage-based expansion -- more of the same -- CS can often handle that through guided self-service. For strategic expansion -- new use cases, new business units, new buyer personas -- that typically requires a sales-led conversation because the commercial structure might need to change.

**ALEX:** You mentioned trust-based expansion. Can you unpack that concept?

**KEVIN:** Trust-based expansion is the idea that a customer's willingness to buy more is directly correlated with their trust in the AI's outputs, and that trust is something you can measure and influence. If a customer is using your product for low-stakes, low-complexity tasks and consistently overriding the AI's suggestions, they do not trust it enough to expand to higher-value workflows. Pushing an upsell at that point is premature and potentially damaging. But if a customer is steadily increasing the autonomy level, accepting AI recommendations without modification, and expanding the scope of what they delegate -- that is trust in action, and it is the strongest possible buying signal. Your expansion playbook should be gated on trust indicators, not just usage volume. High usage with low trust is a customer who is working hard because the AI is not helping enough. High usage with high trust is a customer who is ready to go deeper. Those require completely different commercial approaches.

**ALEX:** That is a much more nuanced view of expansion than "they hit their seat limit, time to upsell."

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And it requires sales teams who understand the product well enough to read those signals and have conversations about trust and autonomy, not just pricing and packaging. That is a capability gap in a lot of sales organizations today, and it is one that training alone cannot solve. You need to hire and develop salespeople who think like product strategists.

### SEGMENT 5: Customer Success as Continuous Value Engineering (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us talk about customer success, which in many SaaS companies has become primarily a retention and renewal function. In the AI-native model, you are describing something that sounds significantly more ambitious. What does customer success become?

**KEVIN:** Customer success becomes continuous value engineering. The traditional CS model is oriented around preventing churn -- monitoring health scores, doing quarterly business reviews, escalating when sentiment turns negative. That is a defensive posture, and it is always reactive. In an AI-native model, the CS team's primary job is to optimize the value the customer is getting from the product on an ongoing basis. That means monitoring autonomy gains -- is the customer delegating more to the AI over time, or have they plateaued? It means tracking outcome quality -- are the AI's outputs meeting or exceeding the customer's quality thresholds? It means identifying new workflows where the AI could deliver value but the customer has not yet adopted it. The CS manager is not calling to ask if everything is okay. They are calling to say, "We noticed your team in the London office is still doing this particular process manually, and based on what your New York team achieved when they automated it, here is the outcome improvement you could expect."

**ALEX:** That is a proactive, data-driven conversation. But it requires CS to have access to product telemetry and the analytical capability to interpret it. That is a significant upgrade from the typical CS tool set.

**KEVIN:** It is, and it is one of the reasons the revenue graph is so important. CS needs to see not just account-level health, but workflow-level health, user-role-level health, and autonomy-level trends over time. They need to know that a specific team within an account has been declining in trust indicators for the past three weeks, even if the overall account usage looks fine. That granularity is what enables proactive intervention. The other major shift is that CS becomes the primary feedback loop to the product team. When CS observes patterns across accounts -- a workflow where autonomy consistently stalls, a use case where trust indicators plateau -- that is product intelligence that should drive roadmap decisions. In the learning loop model, CS is not just protecting revenue; they are generating the insights that improve the product for everyone.

**ALEX:** How does this change the way you measure CS performance?

**KEVIN:** The metrics shift from lagging indicators like renewal rate and NPS to leading indicators like autonomy trajectory, time-to-value for new workflows, trust indicator trends, and outcome improvement rates. You still care about renewal and expansion revenue, obviously, but those become outputs of the leading indicators rather than the primary things you manage to. A CS team that is driving steady autonomy gains and expanding workflow coverage will naturally produce strong renewal and expansion numbers. A CS team that is just monitoring sentiment and scrambling when things go wrong will always be playing catch-up. The other metric that matters enormously is what I call the value gap -- the difference between the value the customer is currently capturing and the value they could be capturing based on what similar customers have achieved. Closing that value gap is the CS team's strategic mandate.

### SEGMENT 6: Marketing Content and Positioning for AI-Native Products (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us circle back to marketing, specifically content strategy and positioning. When you are marketing an AI-native product, you are not marketing a set of static features. You are marketing a system that learns and improves. How does that change the way you position and create content?

**KEVIN:** The fundamental shift is from selling features to selling outcomes, and from promising capabilities to demonstrating trajectories. In traditional software marketing, you say "our product has these features" and you list them. In AI-native marketing, the relevant message is "customers who use our product for this workflow see this outcome improve by this amount over this time period." That is a much harder marketing message to craft because it requires specificity, it requires evidence, and it requires honesty about the conditions under which those outcomes are achievable. But it is also a much more compelling message because it speaks directly to the buyer's business objectives rather than asking them to imagine how features might translate into results.

**ALEX:** That sounds like it demands a much closer relationship between marketing and the product and CS teams than most organizations have.

**KEVIN:** It demands exactly that. Your marketing team needs access to outcome data, which lives in the product and in CS records. They need to understand autonomy curves so they can accurately describe the journey from initial adoption to full AI-enabled operation. They need to know what trust indicators look like so they can set appropriate expectations and not overpromise. The worst thing an AI-native company can do is market outcomes that the product cannot reliably deliver. That erodes trust faster than any competitor could. The content strategy should reflect the maturity model of your product. Early-stage content should focus on the specific problems you solve and the evidence you have from early adopters. Mid-stage content should showcase the compounding value that comes from sustained usage and increasing autonomy. Late-stage content should make the case for enterprise-wide adoption by demonstrating cross-workflow and cross-team impact.

**ALEX:** What about the challenge of marketing a product that is genuinely different from what buyers have seen before? AI-native products often do not fit neatly into existing categories, which makes it hard for buyers to evaluate them using their normal frameworks.

**KEVIN:** This is one of the biggest positioning challenges. If you are too far ahead of the buyer's mental model, they cannot process your value proposition. If you anchor too much to existing categories, you get compared on feature checklists that do not capture what makes your product different. The approach I recommend is what I call a "bridge positioning" strategy. You start from a problem the buyer already understands and already has budget for. You anchor to that familiar territory. Then you show how your approach to solving that problem is fundamentally different -- not just faster or cheaper, but structurally different in a way that compounds over time. You make the AI-native advantage concrete through specific examples and outcome data, not through abstract claims about intelligence or learning. Nobody cares that your product uses AI. They care that their team processes invoices 80 percent faster with 95 percent accuracy after 90 days of usage, improving steadily as the system learns their patterns. That is the kind of positioning that converts.

### SEGMENT 7: Attribution, Measurement, and the Integrated GTM Tech Stack (9 minutes)

**ALEX:** We are approaching the final segment, and I want to tackle two interconnected topics. First, the measurement and attribution challenge. In a traditional GTM stack, you can at least pretend you know which marketing campaign generated which pipeline and which pipeline turned into which revenue. In an AI-native GTM model where product behavior is driving commercial outcomes, how do you even think about attribution?

**KEVIN:** Attribution in AI-native GTM is genuinely hard, and I would argue that most companies should stop trying to do last-touch or even multi-touch attribution and instead invest in understanding contribution patterns. Here is why. When a customer converts, was it the marketing content that brought them in, the product experience that demonstrated value, the AI-driven nurture sequence that kept them engaged, or the sales conversation that closed the deal? The honest answer is it was all of them, in a specific sequence that is unique to that customer, and trying to assign fractional credit to each one is an exercise in false precision. What is more useful is understanding which combinations of touchpoints and product engagement milestones correlate with the highest-value outcomes. Not "this email generated this pipeline" but "customers who read this content, then activated this workflow, then had a sales conversation within two weeks of reaching this autonomy threshold convert at four times the rate and have three times the lifetime value." That is a contribution pattern, and it is actionable in a way that fractional attribution is not.

**ALEX:** That requires a different analytical framework than most marketing ops teams are set up for.

**KEVIN:** It does, and it requires the revenue graph we discussed earlier. You need to be able to trace the full journey from first touch through product engagement through commercial milestones, and you need enough data to identify which journeys produce the best outcomes. The other measurement challenge that is specific to AI-native GTM is measuring the performance of the AI itself within the GTM stack. If you are using AI to score leads, personalize outreach, and prioritize accounts, you need to measure whether that AI is actually improving outcomes compared to the counterfactual. Are AI-scored leads converting at higher rates than they would under the old model? Are AI-personalized messages generating better response rates than template-based ones? That requires controlled experiments and a commitment to measuring real outcomes, not just activity metrics. A lot of teams fall into the trap of measuring the AI's output volume -- it generated more emails, it scored more leads -- without validating that the output quality actually improved commercial outcomes.

**ALEX:** Let us close with the practical question that every operator listening to this is asking: what does the integrated GTM tech stack actually look like? What tools, what data flows, what governance?

**KEVIN:** The foundational layer is the shared data infrastructure -- your revenue graph. Whether that is built on a warehouse, a CDP, or a custom graph database, it needs to be the authoritative source for account, user, workflow, and commercial data. On top of that, you have your system of engagement -- typically your CRM for commercial workflow and your product analytics platform for usage data. The critical integration is between those two, and it needs to be bidirectional and near-real-time, not a nightly batch sync. Then you have your AI execution layer -- the tools that use data from the graph to drive segmentation, personalization, lead scoring, and signal routing. These might be embedded in your marketing automation platform, your sales engagement tool, or purpose-built applications. The governance layer is often the most neglected and the most important. You need clear policies on what data the AI can use for commercial outreach, what quality thresholds must be met before automated actions fire, who reviews AI-generated content before it goes to enterprise prospects, and how you handle data privacy across product telemetry and commercial systems.

**ALEX:** What about the people side? Who owns this stack?

**KEVIN:** The integrated GTM stack needs a single owner, and in most organizations that is either a Revenue Operations leader or a GTM-focused Chief of Staff who reports to the CRO or the CEO. The worst pattern is distributed ownership where marketing ops owns the marketing tools, sales ops owns the CRM, and product owns the analytics -- because then nobody owns the integrations, nobody owns the revenue graph, and nobody is accountable for whether the whole system is producing better outcomes than the sum of its parts. The operator who owns this stack needs to understand product telemetry, commercial workflows, data governance, and AI capabilities. That is a rare profile today, but it is becoming one of the most important hires in a scaling AI-native company. Their mandate is simple: make sure the GTM system learns as fast as the product does.

**ALEX:** Kevin, that is a powerful framing to end on. The GTM system has to learn as fast as the product. For everyone listening, the core message of this episode is that AI-native products require AI-native go-to-market -- not as a nice-to-have, but as a structural requirement. The funnel gives way to learning loops. Siloed data gives way to a unified revenue graph. Volume-driven outreach gives way to signal-driven engagement. And customer success shifts from retention defense to continuous value engineering. Thank you for listening to The Forge. We will see you next time.

**KEVIN:** Thanks, Alex. And for anyone starting this work, remember: the gap between your product's intelligence and your GTM's intelligence is a revenue leak. Closing that gap is the highest-leverage investment most companies are not making yet.
