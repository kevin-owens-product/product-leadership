# DEADWATER
## Episode 1: "The Find"
### Season 1

**Duration:** ~44 minutes

---

[NARRATOR] There is a stretch of highway on the south shore of Long Island that most people drive through without really looking.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It runs along the coast between two barrier islands - a thin ribbon of asphalt with the Atlantic Ocean pressing in on one side and a wide, flat, brackish marsh pressing in on the other. In summer the traffic slows to a crawl. Families load their cars with coolers and beach chairs and the radio plays and kids argue in the back seat and everyone is looking at the water, at the distance, at the weekend ahead. In winter the highway empties almost completely. The wind comes off the ocean at an angle that makes it feel colder than the thermometer says. The reeds in the marsh stand perfectly still on days when everything else is moving. The parking areas along the barrier island access points are gated and dark. Signs tell you which facilities are closed for the season, as if anyone is planning to stop.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is a beautiful stretch of road, in a bleak and very particular way. The beauty of places at the edge of things - where the inhabited world runs out and something older and less interested in human beings takes over. Marshland has its own logic. Its own slow time. Its own way of holding what is placed inside it and keeping it through seasons and years with no particular urgency about being found.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Someone knew this. Someone had driven this road many times in the winter months, in the cold and the flat gray light, and had understood something about what that marsh could hold and for how long.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] My name is Sam Wilder. I'm an investigative journalist, based in New York for the past twenty-two years. In that time I have covered financial fraud, political corruption, environmental disasters in three states, and two wars in countries where I had absolutely no business being. I say all of this not to establish credentials but to establish context. Because what I'm about to tell you is the most significant investigation I've ever worked on. I do not say that lightly. I have never said that before.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] For three years, I have been reporting on what locals call the case. What investigators called Deadwater, after the stretch of water behind the barrier island. What the national press eventually named after the beach where the first remains were found. A case that began in December of a year I'll keep slightly vague for ongoing legal reasons, and that ended - in a courtroom, with a confession, with a man naming eight women out loud into the official record - approximately thirteen years later.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Some names in this series have been changed. The victims are referred to by first name only, with the explicit permission of their families, who reviewed this series before its release. The investigators who worked this case appear under their own names, as do the families of the victims. The killer - Roland Voss - is referred to by name throughout, because he has entered a guilty plea in open court, because his identity is fully a matter of public record, and because there is something important in saying a name clearly and without euphemism. He does not deserve to be an abstraction. And neither, especially, do the women he killed.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] December. The specific kind of cold that settles into coastal Long Island in winter - the cold that comes off the water and finds its way through whatever you're wearing, that makes the whole world feel emptied out and waiting. The sky is the color of old pewter. The vegetation along the highway corridor has died back to gray-brown stalks. The marsh is wide and dull and flat, and if you drove past at speed you might not give it a single thought.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A search party is working through the marsh grass along the south shore access road. Twenty-three people, volunteers coordinated by a family services nonprofit that runs these operations several times a year in Suffolk County. They are looking for a missing woman named Karen. Karen is twenty-eight years old. She was last seen four days earlier leaving a motel approximately four miles from this location, a motel where she had been working. She told her roommate she would be back by morning. She left her phone charger plugged into the wall.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A tip came in to the relevant missing persons line two days ago: a caller thought they might have seen Karen near the highway corridor. The search coordinator marked the grid on a laminated map and sent the volunteer call-out on Thursday evening. Twenty-three people came to the staging area at seven in the morning on a gray Saturday.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] By eight-fifteen, the search for Karen has effectively ended. Not because she has been found. Because something else has been found instead - something that will change the nature of everything happening on that stretch of road, and will continue to change it for the next thirteen years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The volunteer who makes the initial discovery is a retired schoolteacher named Phil who has been doing search-and-rescue work for eleven years. He has found two people alive in those eleven years and four people who were not. He knows immediately, from the shape of what he is seeing through the marsh grass at the edge of the access trail, what he is seeing. He does not approach. He calls out to the search coordinator. He stands very still, looking at the ground, and he waits.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The bundle in the grass contains human remains. An adult woman. She has been there for some time - the medical examiner will later determine that the decomposition is consistent with a minimum of twelve months, possibly considerably longer. She is bound. Her wrists and ankles are secured with a specific type of material that the first responding officers note carefully in their reports and that will become, over the following days, a detail of enormous investigative significance.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She has been placed here. That is the word that every investigator who looks at this scene uses, in their reports, in their interviews, in the years of testimony and analysis that follow. Placed. Not dumped, not discarded, not abandoned in haste. Placed. There is a deliberateness to her position that is visible even to untrained eyes - she has been set back from the road at a specific distance, in a location that shows clear knowledge of exactly how visible this particular spot is and is not from passing traffic. Close enough to the road that a methodical searcher might eventually find her. Far enough that anyone driving past at highway speed never would.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Detective Sergeant Carol Marsh gets the call at eight forty-seven in the morning. She is at her desk, second coffee still warm, working through paperwork on a residential burglary investigation that she has privately decided is going nowhere and will never go anywhere. Her phone rings. It is a patrol supervisor she knows well. He tells her there is a crime scene on the barrier island access road and that she should come immediately and bring her partner.

[MARSH] My first thought was Karen. That made sense contextually - we had been running the Karen missing persons file for four days, and the tip had positioned her in that area. So I grabbed my case bag and I went to Danny's desk and said we had a body call. Danny asked if it was Karen. I said probably. We drove out there already in the mental state you put yourself in for a victim notification - already preparing for the conversation with Karen's family, already thinking about what you say.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I have done a lot of those conversations over the years. You don't get fully comfortable with them but you develop a preparation for them, a way of approaching the emotional weight of it. I was in that mode when I pulled into the access road. And then I walked up to where my colleagues were standing and I saw the scene and I understood immediately that this was not Karen, and that the mental mode I was in was completely wrong for what I was actually walking into.

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh has been a detective with the South Shore Police Department for fourteen years at the time of this call. Before homicides she worked narcotics. Before narcotics she walked a beat in one of the department's rougher districts for four years. She is forty-one years old, methodical by both temperament and training, and in possession of what Danny Reese - her partner on this and many other cases - will describe to me as an almost inhuman ability to be calm in situations where calm is nearly impossible.

[MARSH] When I reached the scene, there were three evidence markers already placed in the grass. Three. And two of my colleagues were standing at a distance from those markers that told me immediately what we were dealing with. When a scene is what you expect - when it is the thing you came to find - officers crowd it. When it is something larger than what you came prepared for, something that requires a moment to absorb, you see people step back. Not from protocol. From instinct. From the need to put a small physical distance between yourself and what you are looking at so you can begin to process it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] They were stepping back. I walked up and I looked at what was in the grass. And I said to the patrol supervisor: how many markers total? He said three in this immediate area. Possibly more as the search continues. I said: pull the volunteer search team. Right now. Get them out of the grass and back to the road. This is a crime scene and they are in it.

[NARRATOR] The decision to pull the volunteer search team immediately is one that Carol Marsh will later identify as among the most consequential of the entire investigation. Untrained personnel moving through potential evidence is a contamination risk that cannot be undone. The nonprofit coordinator responds immediately and without argument. Twenty-three volunteers are back on the road inside eight minutes.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] By four in the afternoon, there are four sets of remains in the grass. By the following morning, seven. By the end of the fourth day of organized searching - state police, county crime lab, cadaver dog teams, a helicopter with infrared - the total is ten.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Ten victims. All adult women. All bound with the same or very similar material. All placed with the same deliberate care. All of them had, at some point, advertised escort services through online platforms. All of them had been reported missing by someone who loved them. In most cases those reports had generated minimal sustained follow-up.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This last fact is not a detail. It is one of the central realities of this case. And we will return to it throughout this series.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The discovery generates immediate and massive media attention. Television news trucks are on the highway within hours of the first official statement. By the second day there are journalists from national outlets, international wire services, true crime publications that didn't exist a decade earlier. Helicopters circle the search corridor. Photographers with long lenses line up on the highway shoulder.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh holds the first press briefing on the afternoon of the second day. She stands at a department lectern in the SSPD parking lot and tells assembled media that investigators have found human remains in the barrier island corridor, that those remains appear to be those of multiple individuals, that an active investigation is underway, and that she has no further information to share at this time. She takes seven questions. She deflects all seven without providing a single detail that could compromise the investigation.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She has prepared for forty-five minutes for a seven-minute briefing. This is not unusual for her. What is unusual is the scale of what she is preparing for. In fourteen years of detective work, she has never stood in front of a camera assembly this size for something this significant.

[MARSH] I understood in the first hours that this was not going to be an ordinary case. Not the scale of it - the number of victims was extraordinary from day one. Not just the scale though. The nature of it. The deliberateness of the placement. The consistency of method across what we could already see was a long timeline. This was not someone who made a terrible choice once. This was someone who had developed a practice. Who had returned to the same location repeatedly. Who had built this into their life in a way that required years of sustained effort and sustained concealment.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] That understanding shapes how you investigate. It tells you that you are looking for extraordinary self-control. For the ability to maintain two completely separate lives indefinitely. For someone who presents to the world as entirely unremarkable while privately sustaining something that would end everything they have built if it ever came to light.

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese joins the investigation in the second week, brought in by Marsh specifically because of his skill in what he calls geographic analysis - the work of building a spatial picture of a crime that can point, eventually, toward a person.

[REESE] I've been doing this kind of analysis for most of my career. My thesis in it - the thing I come back to constantly - is that geography is biography. The way a person uses space, the places they return to, the routes they take and the ones they avoid, the distance they operate from their home base - all of that tells you who they are as surely as any database. People are territorial. They have centers of gravity. And a killer who is this deliberate about location is not choosing randomly. They are choosing from knowledge. From familiarity. From a specific relationship to this particular place.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] My first job was to build a picture of that relationship. Not who yet. What kind of knowledge of this corridor, accumulated over how long, would produce the choices we were seeing in the crime scenes.

[NARRATOR] The answer Danny Reese arrives at - and it takes weeks of systematic work to get there - is precise. The killer has extensive, intimate knowledge of the barrier island corridor that predates the killings by years, possibly decades. They know the access points, the camera coverage, the seasonal patterns of human presence and absence. They know which section of the marsh grass grows tallest and where the road is not visible from any occupied structure. This is not the knowledge of someone who scouted the location once. It is the knowledge of someone who has spent a great deal of time here over a long period.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That knowledge profile - someone with deep, long-term familiarity with a very specific stretch of the south shore - is one of the most distinctive things about this case. It tells you that whoever you are looking for grew up here, or worked here, or had some sustained connection to this exact geography. Which narrows things. Not as much as you'd want, but it narrows things.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey is at her kitchen table in upstate New York on the morning the news breaks. She is making breakfast. Her two children are getting ready for school. The television is on in the background, volume low, set to the local news. She hears the words "unidentified female remains" and "Long Island" and she puts down the spatula in her hand and she stands very still.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Her sister Renee has been missing for sixteen months. Renee disappeared from a motel on Long Island where she had been working. She called Donna two days before she disappeared and they talked for forty minutes about nothing in particular - their mother, a television show they both watched, whether Renee would make it up for Christmas. She said she'd try. She sounded fine. Two days later she was gone and Donna never spoke to her again.

[DONNA] I can tell you exactly where I was standing. At the counter between the stove and the sink. Spatula in my hand. My daughter complaining about something in the other room. And I heard those words and my whole body just went still. Like everything went on pause.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I don't know how to explain what I knew in that moment. I just knew. Sixteen months of hoping it was something else, something explainable, something she chose - and I stood in my kitchen with a spatula in my hand and I knew it wasn't any of those things. That was Renee. Whatever they had found on that beach.

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey is speaking to me in the living room of her house in a small town north of Albany. It is the spring of last year. She is fifty-one years old. She has about her the particular quality of stillness that develops in people who have sustained a very large weight for a very long time - not peace, not resignation, but something more active than both of those things. The stillness of someone who has decided, consciously and repeatedly, to hold themselves together and keep going.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She called the tip line the SSPD set up within hours of the initial public statement. She waited forty minutes on hold. When someone answered, she gave Renee's full name, date of birth, last known location, and the case number for the missing persons report she had filed sixteen months earlier. She was told that her information would be logged and that someone would be in touch.

[DONNA] Nobody called for three weeks. I'm not saying this for dramatic effect. It is what happened. Three weeks. I understand the volume they were dealing with - thousands of tips, dozens of families, an investigation of unprecedented scale with nowhere near enough people to run it properly. I understand all of that now. But I had been waiting sixteen months, calling regularly, being told Renee was a priority, and I waited three more weeks before anyone called me back.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] When they did call, it was a junior detective who clearly had not read the file before picking up the phone. He asked me to describe Renee's appearance. I had sent a photograph sixteen months earlier. I described her anyway.

[NARRATOR] Six weeks after the initial discovery, Renee is identified. She is twenty-six years old. She has been dead, the medical examiner estimates, for between ten and fourteen months. She has been missing for sixteen. The timeline means she was almost certainly dead before her missing persons report generated a single field investigation visit.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I found that out later. Not immediately - immediately I was dealing with the identification, with telling my mother, with trying to figure out what to tell Marcus. Marcus is Renee's son. He was three years old. He came to live with me permanently after the identification. He's fourteen now. He's doing well. I want people to know that. He's doing really well.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She pauses. Looks toward the window.

[DONNA] I held him that night and I didn't tell him anything specific - he was three, you don't explain that to a three-year-old. I just held him and thought about Renee and thought: I am going to find out what happened. I made myself that promise. I did not yet know that Carol Marsh had made the same promise, on the same day, to me.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna drives to Long Island to make the formal identification. She meets Carol Marsh for the first time in a corridor outside the medical examiner's office on a Thursday afternoon in January, six and a half weeks after the initial discovery.

[MARSH] I made a point of being present for every family notification I could manage in those first weeks. Protocol doesn't require it. But I had been walking through that search corridor for six weeks looking at what this person had done, and I needed to be in front of the families. Not as a gesture. As a reminder to myself. These are the people this is for. Not the case number. These people.

[DONNA] She shook my hand and she looked directly at me and she said: I'm Detective Sergeant Carol Marsh. I'm leading this investigation. I am going to find out what happened to your sister.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] Not we'll do everything we can. Not I'll keep you updated. I am going to find out what happened to your sister. Personal. Present tense. Direct. And I stood there in that corridor outside the medical examiner's office, where I had just identified my sister's remains, and I believed her. I still don't fully understand why I believed her immediately. Given how many times I'd been let down over sixteen months, I should have been more cautious. But I believed her.

[MARSH] I meant it. I want to be clear about that, because it would be easy for it to sound like something you say. When I looked at those markers in the marsh grass and understood what I was looking at - the scale of it, the years of it, the deliberateness of it - I had an absolute clarity about one thing. Whoever did this believed they had gotten away with it. Believed they were finished. And I was not going to allow that.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Twelve years later, in a courtroom in Riverhead, New York, Roland Voss pleaded guilty to the murder of eight women. He was not finished. He was found.

[NARRATOR] She gives Donna her card, the cell number handwritten on the back, and tells her to call anytime.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I called her forty-seven times over the next twelve years. She answered every single time.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The early investigation is massive, complicated, and - in ways that will matter enormously later - fundamentally imperfect. At peak, more than four hundred people across multiple agencies are involved in some aspect of the case. The SSPD is effectively reorganizing itself around Deadwater while trying to maintain every other function it is responsible for. Tips flood in at a rate that no one is equipped to process - nine thousand in the first month. The forensic work is extensive and meticulous, the geographic analysis is rigorous, and the victim identification process is painstaking.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And there are things that fall through the cracks.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A witness account in the second week: a woman walking her dog along the access road in the early morning hours, on a winter day some time before the bodies were found, who saw a large man getting out of a dark-colored pickup truck at one of the closed parking lots. The man looked at her as she walked past. The call is logged. An interview is scheduled and rescheduled and never completed.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A parking citation in the barrier island lot database: issued to a vehicle registered to a Manhattan business address, on a winter day, in a lot that should have been empty. Never cross-referenced against the Deadwater vehicle profile because the analyst running the database query uses a geographic filter - local county registrations only - and a Manhattan business address falls outside it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The citation sits in the database for nine years. The vehicle is a dark green Chevrolet Avalanche pickup truck. The business address is the Manhattan office of Roland T. Voss and Associates, Architects.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He was there. Six months before the bodies were found, he was at that parking lot in his truck. A patrol officer wrote him a ticket and filed it and moved on. For nine years, nobody connected it to anything.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This is how it goes cold. Not through one dramatic failure. Through the accumulation of small, defensible, reasonable-in-the-moment decisions that together produce an outcome no one would have chosen.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In the early months, investigators build a profile of the killer. Organized, methodical, almost certainly male, deep local knowledge of the south shore corridor, likely older given the apparent timeline of killings that extends back years before the discovery. The profile says: look for someone who appears completely normal. Who has built a life - employment, relationships, community presence - that generates no friction, no flags, no reasons for anyone to look twice.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The profile is accurate. Roland Voss matches it completely. He is also not on any list. He has no criminal history. He is not in any database. He is invisible to every filter the investigation is running.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He commutes to Manhattan from Massapequa Park every weekday on the same train. He works at a mid-size architecture firm where he has been employed for twenty-one years. His colleagues find him patient, reliable, meticulous. His neighbors find him quiet but considerate. He coaches youth sports. He volunteers at the fire department. He shovels his elderly neighbor's sidewalk without being asked.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is, to everyone who knows him, exactly what he appears to be. The performance is that complete.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] While investigators work through a suspect pool that will eventually top five hundred names, while Donna Kelsey starts calling Carol Marsh and never stops, while Danny Reese opens the notebook that will eventually contain forty-seven entries - Roland Voss goes to work and comes home and reads the news coverage of the hunt for himself and understands, over months and then years, that he is safe.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is not done. The case is not done. The people looking for him are not done.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It will take twelve years. It will take the full weight of every person in this series and some institutional changes that required legislation to make happen. But it will not take forever.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: Ocean Parkway. As the victims are identified and the timeline of the killings is built from forensic evidence, investigators begin to understand the geography of what they are dealing with - and the specific, intimate local knowledge it would take to use this stretch of road the way someone has been using it for decades.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] I want to stop and stay with the early investigation for a moment, because there is something important that tends to get compressed in the way this story is usually told. The narrative arc of the case - bodies found, investigation struggles, task force formed, suspect identified, arrest made, guilty plea - has a shape that implies forward motion. It implies that events led logically and consistently toward resolution. They did not. The early investigation is not a story of steady progress. It is a story of immense effort producing insufficient results, and understanding why that happened matters enormously to understanding the full scope of what went wrong and what eventually made it right.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh ran thirty-seven interviews in the first month alone. She worked every tip that came into the line with any substance to it. She ran background checks on registered sex offenders in the target geographic area. She ran checks on men who had been employed along the barrier island complex going back twenty-five years. She was building what she called a candidate list - a roster of people who, for one reason or another, had the geographic familiarity and access that the crime scene profile required.

[MARSH] At its peak the candidate list had four hundred and twelve names on it. Four hundred and twelve people who, by some combination of geography, employment history, vehicle ownership, or behavioral flag, warranted some level of investigative attention. My job, and Danny's job, and the job of the team that worked with us in those early months, was to work that list down. To eliminate people who could be eliminated and to focus resources on the people who couldn't be.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Roland Voss was not on the list. He should have been. Looking back, I know exactly why he wasn't - the database queries we were running weren't configured to surface his specific profile, because his vehicle was registered to a Manhattan business address rather than a local residential address, and our filter was set for local registrations. A small technical parameter set slightly wrong. But at the time, I didn't know what I was missing. You can't see the shape of what isn't there.

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese's early work focuses on what he calls the geographic architecture of the case. Not the physical search - that is the state police and crime lab's domain, and they are doing it thoroughly. Danny is building a spatial analysis: where each victim was last known to be before she disappeared, what routes connect those last-known locations to the dump site, and what that geometry tells you about the person who made those journeys.

[REESE] The first thing I establish is that the killer is not ranging widely. Every victim's last confirmed contact - phone activity, financial transaction, witness account - originates within a fairly tight geographic area relative to the dump site. We're talking about a twenty-two-mile radius. He's not driving three hours with victims. He's operating locally. He's hunting close to home.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] The second thing I establish is that the dump site itself requires very specific knowledge to use the way it's been used. The particular stretch of grass, the specific depth of the grass at that location versus adjacent sections, the visibility from the road - this is not something you identify by driving past once. You identify it by being in this place, on foot, at different times of year, in different weather. You identify it by knowing this landscape the way you know your own backyard. And that tells you something about the relationship this person has with this place. It's not tactical reconnaissance. It's intimate familiarity. This is their territory.

[NARRATOR] The word territory will come up repeatedly in the years of investigation. It is the word that best captures what investigators come to understand about Roland Voss's relationship to the south shore barrier island corridor. Territory in the specific behavioral sense - a defined geographic area that a person knows deeply, uses regularly, and experiences as in some fundamental way their own.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He had been using this territory for decades before the investigation discovered it. He would continue to return to it even after the investigation began. Even after the bodies were found and the search teams walked through it and the news helicopters circled overhead. He went back. He kept going back. That is, for the investigators who eventually piece this together, one of the most psychologically revealing details of the entire case.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In the second month of the investigation, the medical examiner's office begins producing more detailed reports on the individual victims. The reports establish, among other things, the timeline of the killings. When investigators compile the timeline - when they order the estimated dates of death against each other and against the years of missing persons reports on file - what they see stops them.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The oldest confirmed killing does not date to the years just before the discovery. It dates to 1993. Partial remains found in a wooded area in a neighboring county in the year 2000, previously unidentified, are matched through DNA comparison to the Deadwater evidence profile. The victim - eventually identified as Sandra, thirty-one years old, mother of two daughters - has been dead for seven years when her remains are found, and she has been dead for seventeen years when her identity is finally established.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The timeline now spans from 1993 - when a woman named Sandra disappeared and was murdered and placed in a wooded area that is eventually connected to the same killer as the Gilgo remains - through to the most recent victims, who disappeared in the period just before the discovery. That is a span of seventeen years. Seventeen years of killings by the same person.

[MARSH] When the timeline extended back to '93, I had to stop and recalibrate completely. I had been operating on an assumption about when this started that was wrong. And the implication of the correct timeline is not just operational - it's psychological. You are not looking for someone who snapped at some point in the recent past. You are looking for someone who has been doing this, systematically, as a component of their life, for seventeen years. Who got up every morning during all of those years, went to work, maintained relationships, participated in their community, and did what we know they did. Who sustained that for seventeen years without anyone around them noticing anything wrong.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] That person exists. I knew they existed because the evidence told me they existed. But understanding that they existed and finding them are completely different things. And in those early months, I was still very far from finding them.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The medical examiner's reports also produce one crucial piece of physical evidence that will become, a decade later, the cornerstone of the case against Roland Voss.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A hair. Specifically: a single male hair, recovered during the detailed forensic examination of one of the victims. The hair is intact. It contains a follicular attachment - the root end, which provides significantly more genetic material for analysis than a hair shaft alone. It is, by the standards of what investigators were hoping to find, exceptional.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The hair is processed immediately against the national criminal DNA database. No match. This is disappointing but not surprising - the national database, while extensive, contains only the profiles of people who have previously been processed through the criminal justice system. The killer has no criminal history. He was never going to be in the database.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The lead forensic analyst who handles the hair notes in her report that the sample is in exceptional condition and recommends that it be stored with the highest possible care under conditions that will maintain its integrity indefinitely. She notes that DNA analysis technology is advancing rapidly and that techniques currently in development may allow for investigative uses of partial DNA samples that are not yet operationally available.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Her name is Dr. Nina Park. She is thirty-four years old. She has a specific, personal interest in a technique she has been studying in the academic literature - a technique called forensic genealogy, which uses partial DNA to trace family trees through consumer ancestry databases and potentially identify suspects whose profiles do not exist in any law enforcement database.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The technique does not yet exist in operationally usable form. It is theoretical and experimental. The databases it relies on are not yet large enough. The analytical methods are not yet refined enough. The legal framework governing its use has not yet been developed.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Dr. Park preserves the hair. She stores it correctly. She notes in her lab records that she considers it a high-value evidence item that should be revisited as the field develops.

[PAUSE]
