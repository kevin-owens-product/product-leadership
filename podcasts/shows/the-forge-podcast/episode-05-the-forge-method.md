# Episode 5: The Forge Method
## "A Systematic Framework for Transforming Software Companies from Legacy to AI-Native"

**Duration:** ~60 minutes
**Hosts:** Alex (Interviewer) & Kevin (Expert)

---

### INTRO (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** Welcome back to The Forge Podcast. I am Alex, and as always I am joined by Kevin. This is episode five, and if you have been following along, you know that the first four episodes have been building toward something. In episode one, we unpacked how PE firms actually create and destroy value through technology. In episode two, we went deep on multi-agent architecture, how you design systems where AI agents collaborate on complex business problems. Episode three covered platform power dynamics and data moats. And episode four was about what AI-native product management actually looks like in practice, how the PM role fundamentally changes when AI is the foundation rather than a feature. Today, we are tying all of that together with what Kevin calls the Forge Method. Kevin, before we dive in, give people the context. Why does a framework like this need to exist in the first place?

**KEVIN:** Thanks, Alex. The short answer is that almost every software company I talk to is handling the AI transformation badly, and they are handling it badly in one of two predictable ways. The first way is what I call bolt-on AI. The company sees the competitive pressure, the board is asking about their AI strategy, so they take their existing product and they glue some AI features onto it. They add a chatbot here, an auto-summarize button there, maybe some predictive analytics on a dashboard. The architecture stays the same, the team stays the same, the operating model stays the same. They get to check the AI box in their next board deck, but nothing fundamental has changed. The product is not meaningfully better for customers, and the company has not built any durable competitive advantage.

**ALEX:** And the second failure mode?

**KEVIN:** The second is paralysis. The leadership team looks at the scope of what a genuine AI transformation would require, rethinking the architecture, retraining the team, redesigning the product, reworking the operating model, and they freeze. They form a committee. They commission a study. They run a six-month pilot that never leaves the sandbox. Meanwhile, their competitors are shipping real AI capabilities and the gap widens every quarter. The Forge Method exists because neither of those paths leads anywhere good. What companies need, and especially what PE-backed software companies need, is a structured, repeatable framework that takes them from wherever they are today to a genuinely AI-native operating model. It needs to be fast, because PE hold periods are finite. It needs to be measurable, because boards need to see progress. And it needs to create real value, because that is ultimately what drives returns. That is what the Forge Method is.

### SEGMENT 1: THE FORGE METHOD OVERVIEW AND WHY PE CONTEXT MATTERS (7 minutes)

**ALEX:** So let us start with the thirty-thousand-foot view. What are the phases, how do they connect, and why is the PE context so important to how this framework is designed?

**KEVIN:** The Forge Method has four phases: Discover, Design, Build, and Operate. They are sequential, meaning you do not skip ahead, but they are also iterative, meaning insights from later phases feed back into earlier ones. Discover is about understanding where you are. Design is about deciding where you are going and drawing the map. Build is about executing the transformation. And Operate is about sustaining and compounding the gains after the initial transformation is complete. Each phase has specific inputs, specific activities, specific deliverables, and specific decision gates that you must pass before moving to the next phase.

**ALEX:** And you mentioned this is designed for the PE context. What does that mean concretely? How would this framework be different if it were designed for, say, a publicly traded enterprise software company running its own transformation?

**KEVIN:** Three things make the PE context different, and all three shaped how the Forge Method works. First, time compression. A public company can run a five-year digital transformation program. A PE-backed company typically has a three-to-five-year hold period, and the first year is usually consumed by the acquisition itself. So you might have eighteen to thirty months to execute a transformation that materially impacts the exit multiple. That means the framework has to front-load value creation. You cannot spend twelve months in discovery and planning. The Discover and Design phases together should take six to ten weeks, not six to ten months.

**KEVIN:** Second, measurability. PE operating partners and board members need to track progress with the same rigor they apply to financial metrics. Saying "the team is making good progress on AI" is not sufficient. They need quantitative metrics at every phase, ideally metrics that correlate with the value creation levers we discussed in episode one: revenue growth, margin expansion, and multiple expansion. The Forge Method bakes measurement into every phase, not as an afterthought, but as a core structural element.

**ALEX:** And the third difference?

**KEVIN:** Third, repeatability across a portfolio. A PE firm with fifteen or twenty software companies needs a framework that can be deployed across all of them with predictable results. Each company is different, different domain, different codebase, different team, different competitive dynamics. But the transformation process itself needs to be consistent enough that the firm can build institutional capability around it. The operating partner who runs the Forge Method at company three should be meaningfully better at it than they were at company one, because the framework captures learnings and the playbooks improve with each deployment. This is directly connected to what we discussed in episode one about how the best PE firms build repeatable value creation engines.

**ALEX:** That repeatability point is interesting because it gets at something we hear a lot, which is the idea that every company is a special snowflake and no framework can apply broadly. How do you handle that tension?

**KEVIN:** The framework is consistent but the application is bespoke. Think of it like a medical protocol. Every patient who comes into the emergency room gets triaged using the same framework, vital signs, symptom assessment, risk stratification. But the treatment plan that comes out of triage is specific to the patient. The Forge Method works the same way. The Discover phase uses the same assessment instruments at every company, but the findings are unique. The Design phase follows the same architectural principles, but the target architecture reflects the specific product and market. The Build phase uses the same engineering practices, but the agents and capabilities are domain-specific. The consistency is in the process, not in the output.

### SEGMENT 2: PHASE 1 -- DISCOVER (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us go deep on each phase. Start with Discover. What happens, how long does it take, and what comes out the other end?

**KEVIN:** Discover takes two to four weeks, and the goal is to build a rigorous, quantitative understanding of the company's current state across four dimensions. I want to emphasize the word quantitative, because the biggest trap in discovery work is producing a beautiful narrative report full of qualitative observations that nobody can act on. "The codebase is complex and could benefit from modernization" is useless. "The codebase has 47 microservices across 12 repositories with 14 percent average test coverage, a median deployment frequency of once every three weeks, and no shared API gateway" is actionable. That specificity is what Discover needs to produce.

**ALEX:** Walk us through those four dimensions.

**KEVIN:** The first dimension is codebase assessment. You are scanning every repository to understand the technology stack, the dependency graph, the test coverage, the coupling between components, the deployment pipeline maturity, and the overall code quality. You are looking at things like: how many repositories exist and how are they organized? What languages and frameworks are in use? How tightly coupled are the services? Is there a CI/CD pipeline and how mature is it? What is the release cadence? How much technical debt is embedded in the architecture? This is not a deep audit, it is a rapid scan, but it gives you a clear picture of the technical starting point and, critically, it tells you how much infrastructure work is needed before you can start building AI capabilities.

**ALEX:** That connects directly to what we discussed in episode two about multi-agent architecture, right? You need to understand whether the existing architecture can even support the kind of agent-based systems we described.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. If the codebase is a monolith with no API layer and no service boundaries, you cannot just drop AI agents into it. You need to understand that constraint upfront so the Design phase accounts for the infrastructure work required. The second dimension is workflow mapping. You are documenting every significant workflow in the product, end to end, from the user's perspective. For each workflow, you are capturing the steps involved, the data touched, the decisions made, the handoffs between systems or people, the error rates, and the time spent. This is where you start to see the AI opportunity, because every workflow that involves repetitive human judgment, manual data entry, pattern matching, or rules-based routing is a candidate for AI augmentation or full automation.

**KEVIN:** The third dimension is what I call quick-win identification, and this one matters more than people realize. You are looking for places where a relatively simple AI capability could deliver visible, measurable value within the first few weeks of the Build phase. Maybe it is auto-classifying support tickets. Maybe it is generating first-draft responses to common customer questions. Maybe it is automating a data reconciliation process that currently takes someone four hours a week. These quick wins are strategically important not because they are transformative on their own, but because they build organizational momentum. They show the team, the board, and the customers that this transformation is real and producing results.

**ALEX:** So quick wins are as much about psychology and organizational buy-in as they are about direct value creation?

**KEVIN:** One hundred percent. I have seen transformations stall not because the technology was wrong or the strategy was flawed, but because the organization lost faith. People go back to their old habits. The skeptics feel vindicated. The board starts questioning the investment. Quick wins prevent that. They create a flywheel of confidence that sustains the harder, longer-term work. This connects to what we discussed in episode four about how AI-native product management requires new ways of measuring success. If you can show the board that an AI agent is now handling forty percent of tier-one support tickets with a ninety-two percent satisfaction rate after just six weeks, you have bought yourself the organizational patience to tackle the more ambitious capabilities.

**ALEX:** You mentioned the AI Readiness Score. Tell us about that.

**KEVIN:** The fourth dimension, and the primary quantitative deliverable from the Discover phase, is the AI Readiness Score, or ARS. This is a composite score across six sub-dimensions: architecture readiness, which measures how well the codebase supports AI integration; data readiness, which measures the quality, accessibility, and structure of the company's data assets; team readiness, which assesses the engineering and product team's current AI capabilities and learning velocity; infrastructure readiness, which evaluates the deployment, monitoring, and operations foundation; product readiness, which measures how well the current product is positioned for AI-powered features; and organizational readiness, which assesses leadership commitment, cultural receptiveness, and process maturity for this kind of transformation.

**KEVIN:** Each sub-dimension is scored on a one-to-ten scale using specific, documented criteria, not vibes. A seven on data readiness means something precise and consistent across companies. The composite ARS gives you a single number that represents the starting position, and that number becomes the baseline against which all transformation progress is measured. When we get to the Operate phase, you will see how the ARS evolves over time and how it correlates with business outcomes.

**ALEX:** What are the common failure modes in the Discover phase? Where do teams go wrong?

**KEVIN:** Three big ones. First, they take too long. Discover should be two to four weeks, not two to four months. The goal is decision-quality information, not perfect information. Teams that try to boil the ocean during Discover lose momentum and burn through the organizational patience they need for the Build phase. Second, they do not involve the right people. Discover requires deep access to the engineering team, the product team, the customer success team, and ideally real customer data. If the Discover team is working from slide decks and second-hand information, the findings will be superficial. Third, and this is the most dangerous one, they let the Discover findings become an excuse for inaction. A low ARS score does not mean "we are not ready for AI." It means "we have a clear picture of what needs to change and in what order." Every company I have assessed has had areas of weakness. The ones that succeed are the ones that treat the ARS as a roadmap, not a verdict.

### SEGMENT 3: PHASE 2 -- DESIGN (9 minutes)

**ALEX:** Alright, the Discover phase has given us an ARS score and a clear picture of where the company stands. Now we move to Design. What happens here?

**KEVIN:** Design takes three to six weeks and runs in parallel toward the end of Discover, so in practice there is some overlap. The goal of Design is to answer four questions. What does the target architecture look like? Where should AI operate autonomously versus with human oversight? What data do we need to make this work? And what is the sequenced plan for getting from here to there? Those four questions map to the four workstreams of the Design phase: target architecture, autonomy boundaries, data requirements, and the integration plan.

**ALEX:** Start with target architecture. How do you decide what the end state should look like?

**KEVIN:** The target architecture defines how the product will work when the transformation is complete. This is where episode two becomes directly relevant, because the multi-agent architecture patterns we discussed there are the blueprint for the target state. You are defining which AI agents the system needs, how they communicate, what orchestration patterns connect them, and how they share context and memory. But here is the critical design principle: you do not design the target architecture in a vacuum. You design it as an evolution of the current architecture, not a replacement. The companies that fail at this phase are the ones that draw a beautiful future-state diagram on a whiteboard and then realize it would require rewriting seventy percent of the codebase. That is not a transformation, that is a rebuild, and it will take three times as long and cost five times as much as anyone estimated.

**KEVIN:** Good target architecture design identifies the minimal set of architectural changes needed to support AI capabilities. Maybe you need an API gateway to enable agent-to-service communication. Maybe you need to extract a few critical services from a monolith so agents can interact with them independently. Maybe you need a shared data layer so agents can access the information they need without going through twelve different interfaces. You make the smallest structural changes that unlock the biggest AI opportunities.

**ALEX:** The second workstream is autonomy boundaries. This sounds like what we discussed in episode four about progressive autonomy in AI-native product management.

**KEVIN:** It is the same concept applied at the system design level. For every AI capability in the target architecture, you need to define three things. First, the autonomy spectrum, which is the range from fully human-controlled to fully autonomous for that specific capability. Second, the starting position on that spectrum, which should always be more conservative than you think necessary. Third, the criteria for moving along the spectrum, meaning what evidence must the system produce before it earns more autonomy. This is where the platform power dynamics from episode three also come into play. Autonomy decisions are not just technical decisions. They have customer trust implications, regulatory implications, and competitive positioning implications. An AP automation product that processes payments autonomously needs a very different autonomy boundary framework than a content recommendation engine. Getting autonomy boundaries wrong is one of the most expensive mistakes in AI product design, because if the AI does something consequential without authorization, you lose customer trust, and trust is much harder to rebuild than it is to earn in the first place.

**ALEX:** Tell us about data requirements. This seems like it could be a major blocker.

**KEVIN:** It frequently is, and that is why it gets its own workstream. For every AI capability in the target architecture, you need to map the data it requires, assess whether that data exists and in what condition, and define the pipeline needed to deliver that data to the AI system in the right format, at the right freshness, with the right quality. The most common finding in this workstream is that the data technically exists but it is scattered, inconsistent, or inaccessible. Customer interaction data lives in Zendesk. Product usage data lives in Mixpanel. Financial data lives in the ERP. Transaction data lives in the product database. None of these systems talk to each other, and none of them expose their data in a format that an AI agent can consume efficiently. Mapping these gaps during Design, rather than discovering them during Build, saves enormous time and prevents the demoralizing experience of building an agent that does not work because it cannot access the data it needs.

**ALEX:** And then the integration plan ties it all together?

**KEVIN:** The integration plan is the sequenced roadmap that connects the current state from Discover to the target state from the architecture and autonomy workstreams. It defines what gets built in what order, with explicit dependencies between workstreams. It specifies milestones at thirty, sixty, ninety, and one-hundred-eighty-day intervals, each with measurable success criteria. And it includes resource requirements at each stage: what team capacity is needed, what infrastructure must be in place, what training needs to happen, and what budget is required. The integration plan is the decision gate document for moving from Design to Build. If the board or the operating partner is not confident in the plan, you iterate until they are. Rushing past this gate is a classic failure mode. I have seen teams eager to "start building" skip rigorous integration planning, and then they spend the first eight weeks of Build undoing decisions they made in haste.

**ALEX:** What about timeline expectations? How should teams think about the overall Discover-plus-Design duration?

**KEVIN:** Combined, Discover and Design should take six to ten weeks. If it is taking longer than that, something is wrong, either you are trying to achieve too much certainty before you start, or you have a scoping problem. You do not need to design every agent and every capability before you start building. You need to design the foundational architecture, the first wave of capabilities, and the infrastructure that supports them. The later waves can be designed in more detail as you learn from the early ones.

### SEGMENT 4: PHASE 3 -- BUILD (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Now we are at the part everyone is excited about. The Build phase. This is where AI capabilities actually come to life. Walk us through it.

**KEVIN:** Build takes twelve to twenty-four weeks depending on scope and starting position, and it has four parallel workstreams that I will walk through. But first, let me emphasize something that gets lost in the excitement of building: the Build phase is where the most expensive mistakes happen. Not because people build the wrong thing, although that happens too, but because they build the right thing in the wrong order, or they build without the evaluation infrastructure needed to know whether what they built actually works. Discipline during Build is what separates a successful transformation from an expensive science project.

**ALEX:** What are the four workstreams?

**KEVIN:** The first workstream is agent development. This is the core engineering work of building the AI agents defined in the Design phase. Each agent needs a well-defined scope, a carefully crafted system prompt, a specific set of tools it can use, and clear boundaries on what it can and cannot do. In episode two, we talked about agent boundary design based on cognitive specialization, tool boundaries, autonomy levels, and feedback loops. All of that design thinking from episode two comes to life here. The first agents you build should be the quick wins identified during Discover, because they deliver immediate visible value while giving the team a relatively low-risk opportunity to build their muscle memory for AI development.

**KEVIN:** One thing I want to stress is that building a good AI agent is not primarily a machine learning problem. It is a systems design problem and a product design problem. The LLM is the engine, but the system prompt, the tool definitions, the guardrails, the error handling, the fallback behavior, the user interface for human-in-the-loop scenarios, all of that is what determines whether the agent actually works in production. I have seen teams hire expensive ML engineers to build agents and then wonder why the agents do not perform well, when the issue was not the model but the system design around the model. The AI-native PM skills we discussed in episode four are critical here.

**ALEX:** The second workstream is evaluation frameworks. Tell us why this deserves its own workstream rather than being part of agent development.

**KEVIN:** Because evaluation is the single most underinvested area in AI product development, and it is arguably the most important. Here is the problem: traditional software testing is deterministic. You write a test that says "given input X, expect output Y." Either the test passes or it fails. AI systems are probabilistic. Given the same input, you might get a slightly different output every time. And "correct" is often not binary but exists on a spectrum. So you need a fundamentally different approach to evaluation.

**KEVIN:** The eval framework has three layers. The first is offline evals, which are test suites that run against a curated dataset before any agent reaches production. These catch obvious failures and regressions. The second is online evals, which monitor agent performance in production using a combination of automated metrics and human review. You are tracking things like accuracy rates, confidence calibration, latency, cost per interaction, and error patterns. The third is user-driven evals, which capture the signals that only real users can provide: acceptance rates, override rates, correction patterns, and satisfaction scores. All three layers need to be in place before you deploy an agent to production. Not after. Before. The teams that skip eval infrastructure and ship agents based on vibes and demo impressions are the ones that end up with production incidents that destroy customer trust.

**ALEX:** The third workstream is deployment pipelines. How does deploying AI capabilities differ from deploying traditional software?

**KEVIN:** Several important ways. First, you need model versioning and rollback capabilities that are distinct from code versioning. When you update a system prompt or swap an underlying model, you need to be able to track that change and revert it independently of code changes. Second, you need gradual rollout mechanisms, what some people call canary deployments or feature flags, but with AI-specific dimensions. You might roll out a new agent to five percent of traffic, compare its performance against the existing system, and only expand if the metrics are better. Third, you need cost monitoring at a granularity that traditional software does not require. Every LLM call has a cost that varies by model, input length, and output length. A poorly designed agent can rack up enormous API bills before anyone notices if you do not have real-time cost monitoring and alerting built into the deployment pipeline.

**KEVIN:** Fourth, and this connects back to the multi-agent architecture discussion from episode two, you need orchestration-aware deployment. When agents depend on each other, you cannot just deploy one in isolation and hope the system still works. The deployment pipeline needs to understand agent dependencies and ensure that updates to one agent do not break the contracts that other agents rely on. This is analogous to API versioning in microservices, but with the added complexity that agent behavior is probabilistic rather than deterministic.

**ALEX:** And the fourth workstream is feedback loops. This came up repeatedly in episode four when we talked about how AI-native products improve over time.

**KEVIN:** Feedback loops are what turn a static AI feature into a compounding intelligence system, and they are what create the durable competitive moat we discussed in episode three. There are three types of feedback loops you need to build during the Build phase. The first is correction feedback, which captures every instance where a user overrides or corrects an AI decision. The agent classified an invoice as a marketing expense, but the user reclassified it as a travel expense. That correction is training data. The system needs to capture it, store it in a structured way, and use it to improve future classifications.

**KEVIN:** The second is implicit feedback, which captures user behavior signals that indicate satisfaction or dissatisfaction without explicit correction. Did the user accept the AI's suggestion without modification? That is a positive signal. Did the user view the AI's output and then immediately perform a manual action that contradicts it? That is a negative signal. These implicit signals are higher volume but noisier than explicit corrections, and designing the system to capture and interpret them correctly is a meaningful engineering challenge. The third is system feedback, which comes from the eval framework monitoring agent performance over time and identifying trends, patterns, and drift. If an agent's accuracy on a particular type of task is declining week over week, the system feedback loop should surface that to the team before it becomes a customer-facing problem.

**ALEX:** What are the biggest failure modes during Build?

**KEVIN:** There are three that I see repeatedly. The first is building agents without eval infrastructure. I know I have said this already, but it is so common and so damaging that it bears repeating. Teams get excited about the agent, they demo it internally, everyone is impressed, they ship it to production, and then they have no reliable way to know whether it is working well or degrading. By the time customers complain, the trust damage is done. Second, over-scoping the first wave. The temptation is to build everything in the target architecture at once. Resist this. The first wave should be three to five agents targeting the highest-impact, lowest-risk opportunities. Get those right, learn from the experience, and then expand. Third, treating Build as purely a technical phase. Build requires deep involvement from product management, customer success, and domain experts, not just engineers. The PM defines the agent's purpose and success criteria. Customer success provides insight into the workflows the agent is augmenting. Domain experts validate that the agent's outputs make sense in context. Engineers who build agents in isolation build technically impressive systems that do not solve real problems.

### SEGMENT 5: PHASE 4 -- OPERATE (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** The fourth phase is Operate. This starts once the initial Build phase is complete, but from what you are describing, it sounds like it never really ends.

**KEVIN:** That is exactly right. Operate is not a phase with a defined end date. It is the ongoing operating model for an AI-native product organization. The transition from Build to Operate is not a hard boundary. It is a gradual shift as the initial agents stabilize in production and the team's focus moves from building new capabilities to sustaining and improving existing ones while selectively adding new ones. Operate has four workstreams, and they run continuously.

**KEVIN:** The first is monitoring. This is the operational backbone, the system that tells you whether your AI capabilities are working correctly, efficiently, and safely at all times. It includes real-time dashboards tracking agent performance metrics: accuracy, latency, cost, throughput, error rates, and confidence calibration. It includes alerting for anomalies, drift detection when agent performance starts degrading, cost spikes when an agent starts consuming more API tokens than expected, and safety triggers when an agent approaches or exceeds its autonomy boundaries. Good monitoring means you find problems before your customers do. Great monitoring means you find the early signals of problems before they become problems at all.

**ALEX:** How does the continuous improvement workstream work? What does it look like week to week?

**KEVIN:** Continuous improvement is where the feedback loops built during the Build phase start generating compounding returns. Every week, the team reviews the data from all three feedback loop types: corrections, implicit signals, and system feedback. They identify the most impactful improvement opportunities, which usually fall into three categories. Category one is prompt and configuration tuning, which is adjusting system prompts, temperature settings, tool definitions, or guardrail thresholds to improve agent performance on specific task types. These are low-effort, high-frequency improvements that add up over time. Category two is data pipeline improvements, which is adding new data sources, cleaning existing data, or restructuring how data flows to agents to improve the quality of their inputs. Category three is architectural evolution, which is more significant changes like splitting an agent that has become too broad into two specialized agents, adding a new orchestration pattern to handle a workflow that was not well-served by the initial design, or integrating a new model that handles a specific task better than the original choice.

**KEVIN:** The key discipline in continuous improvement is maintaining a rigorous before-and-after measurement practice. Every change, no matter how small, should be evaluated against the same metrics used during Build. This prevents the common failure mode where a team makes dozens of incremental changes and then cannot explain why performance got better or worse, because they never isolated the impact of each change.

**ALEX:** The third workstream is the scaling playbook. What does scaling look like once the initial deployment is stable?

**KEVIN:** The scaling playbook answers two questions. First, how do we expand the AI capabilities we have already built to handle more volume, more complexity, and more use cases? Second, how do we systematically identify and build the next wave of AI capabilities? For the first question, scaling usually involves expanding autonomy boundaries as the system earns trust through demonstrated performance. An agent that started at fifty percent autonomy, meaning it handled half of cases independently and escalated the rest to humans, might earn its way to eighty percent autonomy over six months as the eval data shows consistent high performance. Scaling also involves extending agents to new customer segments, new geographies, or new product modules where the same patterns apply but the specifics differ.

**KEVIN:** For the second question, the team maintains a rolling AI opportunity backlog that is continuously refreshed based on insights from the Operate workstreams. Customer feedback reveals new automation opportunities. Agent performance data reveals adjacent capabilities that would be straightforward to build. Market dynamics and competitive moves reveal strategic imperatives. The product management practices from episode four, particularly the AI-first discovery approach and the new prioritization frameworks, are the operating system for managing this backlog effectively.

**ALEX:** And the fourth workstream is value measurement. How does this connect back to the PE value creation levers from episode one?

**KEVIN:** Value measurement closes the loop between the transformation work and the financial outcomes that PE firms care about. You are measuring at three levels. At the operational level, you track the direct impact of AI capabilities on product metrics: processing times, accuracy rates, automation rates, customer satisfaction scores, and support ticket volumes. These are the leading indicators that tell you the AI is working. At the business level, you track how those operational improvements translate to the financial metrics: revenue growth from new AI-powered features or improved win rates, margin expansion from reduced cost-to-serve, and retention improvements from better customer experience. At the valuation level, you track how the business improvements map to the exit multiple drivers. This is where the AI Readiness Score comes full circle. We established the baseline ARS during Discover. Now, during Operate, you track how the ARS has improved and you correlate that improvement with business performance. Over time, this correlation data becomes one of the most valuable assets in the Forge Method, because it lets you predict, with increasing accuracy, how much business value a given amount of ARS improvement will generate at the next portfolio company.

**ALEX:** What is the most common failure mode in the Operate phase?

**KEVIN:** Declaring victory too early and pulling resources. I have seen this pattern multiple times. The initial agents are deployed, they are working well, the board is happy, and leadership decides the transformation is "done" and redirects the team to other priorities. Within six months, the AI capabilities start degrading because nobody is monitoring the feedback loops, nobody is tuning the agents, and nobody is addressing the gradual drift that is inevitable in any AI system operating in a dynamic environment. The Operate phase is not overhead. It is the phase where the investment compounds. A well-operated AI system gets better every month. An unattended AI system gets worse every month. The difference between those two trajectories is the difference between building a durable competitive moat and building a depreciating asset.

### SEGMENT 6: HOW THE PHASES CONNECT AND REAL-WORLD TIMELINES (7 minutes)

**ALEX:** We have now walked through all four phases individually. Let us talk about how they fit together as a system. What is the overall timeline, and how do the phases interact with each other?

**KEVIN:** Let me paint the picture of a typical engagement. Weeks one through three are Discover. You are on-site, embedded with the team, running the codebase assessment, mapping workflows, identifying quick wins, and building the ARS. By the end of week three, you have a quantitative picture of where the company stands and a clear view of the opportunity landscape. Weeks three through eight are Design, with some overlap at the beginning where Discover findings are still being finalized while Design workstreams are starting. By the end of week eight, you have the target architecture, autonomy boundaries, data requirements, and a fully sequenced integration plan.

**KEVIN:** Weeks eight through twenty-four are Build, with the first agents shipping to production as early as week twelve if the infrastructure work is straightforward, or week sixteen if more foundational work is needed. By week twenty-four, the first wave of agents is in production, the eval framework is operating, the deployment pipeline is mature, and the feedback loops are generating data. Week twenty-four onward is Operate, which continues indefinitely. So the total time from kickoff to first agents in production is roughly three to four months, and the total time to a mature operating model is roughly six months.

**ALEX:** That is aggressive. Is that realistic?

**KEVIN:** It is aggressive but achievable, and the aggressiveness is intentional because of the PE context. The key is that you are not trying to do everything in six months. You are trying to get the first wave of capabilities into production and the operating model established in six months. The system then continues to expand and improve during the Operate phase, which runs for the remainder of the hold period. I should also note that these timelines assume reasonable organizational support, meaning the leadership team is committed, the engineering team is engaged, and the necessary access to systems, data, and people is provided. I have seen timelines stretch significantly when the transformation team has to fight for every code review, every data access request, and every hour of subject matter expert time.

**ALEX:** How do the phases inform each other? You mentioned earlier that insights from later phases feed back into earlier ones.

**KEVIN:** Here is a concrete example. During Build, the team discovers that a workflow they planned to automate has a data quality issue that was not fully apparent during Discover. The transaction records they need for training and evaluation are inconsistent because three different systems have been writing to the same database with slightly different formatting conventions over the past five years. This discovery triggers a feedback loop to the Design phase. The integration plan needs to be updated to include a data remediation step. The target architecture might need a data normalization service that was not in the original design. And the timeline for that particular agent shifts by a few weeks.

**KEVIN:** This kind of feedback is normal and expected. The Forge Method does not pretend that you can perfectly plan everything upfront. It provides the structure to handle discoveries and course corrections without losing momentum. Each phase has regular checkpoints, weekly during Build and Operate, biweekly during Discover and Design, where the team reviews progress, surfaces blockers, and adjusts plans. The integration plan is a living document, not a stone tablet. What must not change is the overall direction and the success criteria. How you get there can and should adapt as you learn.

**ALEX:** You have worked with multiple companies on this. What is the typical trajectory of the ARS score through the phases?

**KEVIN:** A company that starts with an ARS of four out of ten, which is typical for a PE-acquired software company that has not yet invested in AI capabilities, will usually see the score move to five or five-and-a-half by the end of Design, because the assessment process itself surfaces improvements that the team can make immediately, things like consolidating repositories, setting up basic CI/CD, or establishing a data catalog. By the end of Build, the ARS typically reaches seven or seven-and-a-half, reflecting the infrastructure improvements, the new AI capabilities, and the team skill development. During the Operate phase, the score continues to climb, and companies that execute well typically reach eight or above within twelve months of starting the transformation. The correlation between ARS improvement and business performance is strong. In the companies I have worked with, every full point of ARS improvement has correlated with measurable improvements in at least two of the three PE value creation levers.

### SEGMENT 7: OVERCOMING COMMON OBJECTIONS AND CLOSING (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us close with the objections. When you present the Forge Method to a PE operating partner or a portfolio company CEO, what pushback do you hear, and how do you address it?

**KEVIN:** Three objections come up almost every time. The first is "we have too much technical debt to take on AI right now." This is actually a reason to start the Forge Method sooner, not later. The Discover phase will quantify the technical debt, and the Design phase will integrate the necessary infrastructure modernization into the transformation plan. You are not layering AI on top of a broken foundation. You are fixing the foundation and building AI capabilities as a single coordinated effort. The companies that say "let us fix our tech debt first and then think about AI" end up spending two years on infrastructure modernization with no AI to show for it, and by then the competitive window has closed.

**KEVIN:** The second objection is "our team does not have AI skills." This is addressed by design. The Forge Method includes skill development as a core activity in every phase. During Discover, the team learns assessment methodologies. During Design, they learn architecture patterns. During Build, they learn agent development, evaluation, and deployment through direct hands-on work. During Operate, they learn continuous improvement practices. By the end of the process, the team has built the skills organically through doing the work, not through abstract training programs. You may need to bring in two or three specialists, but the core team evolves rather than being replaced.

**ALEX:** And the third objection?

**KEVIN:** "AI is moving too fast, whatever we build will be obsolete in six months." This sounds reasonable but it is fundamentally wrong, and it is the most dangerous objection because it provides intellectual cover for inaction. The Forge Method is explicitly designed to be model-agnostic. The architecture, the agent framework, the eval infrastructure, the orchestration patterns, the feedback loops, none of these are tied to a specific model or model provider. When a better model comes out, and better models will keep coming out, you swap the model layer and everything above it benefits. The companies that are waiting for the "right moment" to start their transformation are accumulating two kinds of debt simultaneously. They are accumulating technical debt because their architecture is not evolving. And they are accumulating competitive debt because their rivals who started earlier are building compounding advantages through institutional memory, feedback loops, and team capability that cannot be replicated by simply plugging in a better model later.

**ALEX:** Kevin, this has been a thorough walkthrough. If someone listening to this wants to start applying the Forge Method, what is the first concrete step?

**KEVIN:** Run a lightweight version of Discover on your own product. Take one week. Scan your codebase and honestly assess its AI readiness. Map your three most important customer workflows end to end. Identify one workflow where a relatively simple AI capability could deliver measurable value within a month. And score yourself on the six ARS dimensions, even if the scoring is rough. That exercise alone will give you more clarity about your AI transformation opportunity than six months of strategy committee meetings. The Forge Method is a system, but every system starts with a first step, and the first step is understanding where you are right now with unflinching honesty.
