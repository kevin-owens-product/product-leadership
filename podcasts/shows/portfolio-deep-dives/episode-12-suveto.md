### How it got here

**NORA:** Let's do ownership and history.

**JAMES:** Levine Leichtman invested in October 2020, from its Fund VI, and lists the investment as current. The seller isn't named on the firm's page. PitchBook also lists Morgan Stanley and Morgan Stanley Private Credit among Suveto's investors, and estimates total funding of about three hundred and fifteen million dollars across five rounds. **JAMES:** Right. It's an early-stage platform investment, in a sense. The firm wasn't buying a mature business with decades of history. It was backing a model and a team that planned to grow by bringing independent hospitals into the network.

**NORA:** How fast has it grown?

**JAMES:** We have one older data point. In February 2023, Suveto announced that more of its hospitals had earned accreditation from the American Animal Hospital Association, a voluntary quality standard in the profession, and that announcement refers to twenty-eight hospitals in seventeen states. It's not completely clear from what we read whether that was the whole network at the time or just the accredited group. If it was the whole network, then going from twenty-eight to seventy-five plus in about three and a half years is fast growth.

**NORA:** And add-on acquisitions? With most companies in this series we can list them.

**JAMES:** Here we can't. The private equity firm lists no add-ons for Suveto. **NORA:** Let's zoom out for a second. Why has veterinary care become such a favourite for private equity in the first place?

**JAMES:** I'll give you the general background, not anything Suveto-specific. Animal hospitals have a few features investors like. Demand is steady, because pets get sick in good economies and bad ones. Many families treat their pets as members of the family and are willing to spend on their care. And the industry was historically very fragmented, with thousands of small, owner-run practices. Fragmentation plus steady demand is the classic recipe for a roll-up.

**NORA:** And the roll-up logic is buy many small ones and run them better together.

**JAMES:** Right. Buy practices at a price that reflects their size, combine them, share the costs of things like payroll, purchasing and marketing, and the combined group is, in theory, worth more per dollar of profit than the pieces were alone. That gap is where a lot of the private equity return comes from.

**NORA:** So Suveto is a roll-up with a twist.

**JAMES:** That's how I'd describe it. The twist is the ownership pitch. The big groups compete on price and scale. Suveto competes on keeping the seller and the younger vets inside the ownership story. Whether that lets it buy hospitals on better terms, we can't say from public sources. But it's clearly the intent.

**NORA:** So how long has the current owner held it?

**JAMES:** Since October 2020, so just over six years as we record. In private equity terms, that's a mature hold. No exit has been announced. But at six years, people naturally start thinking about what the next chapter looks like.

### The problem Suveto solves

**NORA:** Let's get to the customer problem. And I think we need to be clear about who the customer actually is, because it's not the pet owner.

**JAMES:** That's right, and it's an important point. Pet owners are the patients' families, and they pay the bills at the hospital. But the customer Suveto is really competing for is the independent practice owner, the vet who might sell, and the young vet who might one day want to own.

**NORA:** Start with the seller. What does she want?

**JAMES:** Suveto's page for what it calls legacy owners lays out its pitch. Three things stand out. First, a full-value purchase of the hospital. Second, an equity roll, which means the seller can keep a stake in the larger group rather than walking away with only cash. Third, and this one is emotional as much as financial, owners keep medical decision-making. The vets decide how animals are treated, not a central office.

**NORA:** Why does that third point matter so much?

**JAMES:** Because the fear among many vets, in our general understanding of the profession, is that corporate ownership means pressure on the medicine. Targets for how many procedures, which products to push, how long appointments can be. Whether that fear is fair or not, it's real. So a buyer that promises clinical autonomy is speaking to the seller's identity, not just her bank balance.

**NORA:** And the younger vet?

**JAMES:** For the younger vet, the pitch is ownership and career growth. Suveto offers an equity plan it calls VSOP, which it describes as having no cost to buy in. So a vet working in the network can share in the value of the business without having to raise the money to buy a practice outright.

**NORA:** That's quite different from the traditional path.

**JAMES:** It is. Traditionally, a young vet might work as an associate for years, save money, borrow heavily and eventually buy a practice from a retiring owner. That path is hard, and it gets harder as practice values rise. Suveto is offering a shared version of ownership instead.

**NORA:** And underneath both of those, there's the back-office problem.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Running a small animal hospital means doing a lot of things vets weren't trained for. Hiring and HR, payroll, IT, marketing, financial reporting, recruiting. Suveto's legacy-owners page lists all of those as services the network provides centrally. The idea is that the vet focuses on medicine and the network handles the rest.

**NORA:** So the problem Suveto solves is really three problems. Exit for older owners, ownership for younger vets, and administration for everyone.

**JAMES:** That's a good way to put it. And the reason the model hangs together is that all three feed each other. Sellers join because they keep autonomy and an equity stake. Younger vets join because they can own a piece. And the shared back office is what lets the network grow without every hospital reinventing payroll.

### How the model works

**NORA:** Let's go a bit deeper on the machinery. What are the pieces?

**JAMES:** I'd think about four. The hospitals themselves, which are the core. The central support office in Pennsylvania that runs the shared services. Calico, the lending arm. And Harbor, the career platform.

**NORA:** Tell me about Calico.

**JAMES:** Calico is described as Suveto's lending business, and the research points to payment plans for pet owners. We don't have much public detail on how it works, how big it is or what its terms are, so I'll stay cautious. But the logic is clear. Veterinary bills can be large and unexpected, and if a family can't pay a big bill up front, they may decline treatment. A financing option at the point of care can help the family and the hospital.

**NORA:** And the network already has a relationship with a well-known pet financing brand, doesn't it?

**JAMES:** CareCredit, which is a widely used health and veterinary financing card, was a partner at Suveto's 2026 conference. So it seems pet-owner financing is something the network thinks about from more than one angle. How Calico and CareCredit fit together, we don't know.

**NORA:** And Harbor?

**JAMES:** Harbor, or Harbor dot vet, is a career and networking platform for vets. It's linked to programs like externships for veterinary students. In February 2026, Suveto's podcast, which is called Let's Vet Together, had an episode with Dr. Rob Trimble from Harbor about externships. I want to be clear that Harbor is a networking and careers tool. It's not an AI product, and we didn't find any evidence it uses AI.

**NORA:** Why would a hospital group run a careers platform?

**JAMES:** Because, in our view, the hardest constraint in veterinary care is people. You can buy a building and equipment. You can't easily conjure up vets and credentialed technicians. So owning a pipeline to students and new graduates is a strategic asset. Suveto also hired a well-being manager back in 2022, and it publishes content on what vets want from employers. That all points the same way. Talent is the battleground.

**NORA:** Tell me about the conference you mentioned.

**JAMES:** It's called the Suveto ONE Conference. The 2026 edition ran in Dallas from the eleventh to the thirteenth of March. The workshops focused on two operational topics in particular: treatment acceptance and forward booking. The partners listed included Zoetis, Elanco, Merck Animal Health, CareCredit and Purina.

**NORA:** Explain those two terms for people who don't work in vet clinics.

**JAMES:** Treatment acceptance is how often a pet owner says yes when the vet recommends a treatment. If a vet recommends dental work and the family declines, that's lost care for the animal and lost revenue for the hospital. Forward booking is scheduling the next visit before the client leaves today. Book the next vaccine or check-up now, rather than hoping they call back in six months.

**NORA:** And both of those are things a network can coach.

**JAMES:** Exactly, and they're measurable. The fact that the network runs training on them tells me those measures exist somewhere inside the business. That's going to matter when we talk about data and AI.

**NORA:** Anything else on the brand side?

**JAMES:** In April 2025, Suveto won silver and bronze VETTY awards at the VMX conference, which is one of the big veterinary industry gatherings. They were for marketing campaigns, one around network dental care and one around grand openings. Nice recognition, but I'd note they're marketing awards, not operating results.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** Let's talk market. How big is veterinary care?

**JAMES:** The only sizing we found is a global one, from Grand View Research, a market research aggregator. It put global veterinary services at about one hundred and fifty-six billion dollars in 2025, growing at about eight percent a year to around two hundred and ninety-three billion dollars by 2033. It says the US is the largest country, but it didn't give a US-only figure we could use.

**NORA:** So big, and growing.

**JAMES:** Big and growing, at least by that estimate. And I'd treat aggregator forecasts as directional. What we didn't find, and I want to be honest about this, is a reliable US-only figure, a figure for veterinary software, or a solid number for what share of US practices are now corporate-owned. Those would all be useful, and we just don't have them from our sources.

**NORA:** Who are the competitors?

**JAMES:** Think of two kinds. First, the big hospital groups that compete to buy independent practices and to hire vets. Second, the software vendors that sit inside every hospital.

**NORA:** Start with the hospital groups.

**JAMES:** The giant is Banfield Pet Hospital. Its own site says it has been a Mars affiliate since 2007 and has more than one thousand hospitals. It offers a service called Pet Chat, which is twenty-four-seven advice, and virtual visits. Then there's VCA, which, from general background knowledge, is also owned by Mars and runs a large hospital network. IVC Evidensia says it has about two thousand five hundred locations in twenty countries. And there are other large platforms backed by private equity, like National Veterinary Associates and Thrive Pet Healthcare. I'd flag that some of those ownership details are from general knowledge rather than pages we read.

**NORA:** So Suveto is small next to those.

**JAMES:** Much smaller. Seventy-five plus hospitals against more than a thousand at Banfield and two and a half thousand at IVC Evidensia. Suveto isn't trying to out-scale them. It's trying to win a particular kind of seller.

**NORA:** And how does it differentiate?

**JAMES:** Ownership. The large groups mostly buy the practice and employ the vets. Suveto pitches shared ownership, an equity roll for sellers, an equity plan for vets and clinical autonomy. For a seller who cares about her legacy and her team, that's a meaningful difference.

**NORA:** And the software side?

**JAMES:** The example in our research is Covetrus, which sells practice software including a system called Pulse, plus communication and payment tools. Its site also has a page for something called Covetrus AI, though we didn't find detail on it. Covetrus isn't a rival hospital operator. It's a vendor. But in our view it's a different kind of challenger, because if the AI in veterinary care arrives mainly through vendors like this, then every hospital group, large or small, gets similar tools and the advantage shifts to who adopts them best.

**NORA:** Explain practice software for the non-vets.

**JAMES:** It's usually called a practice information management system, or PIMS for short. It's the core system of a vet hospital. Appointments, the medical record, invoicing, reminders, inventory. Everything that happens to a patient leaves a trace there.

**NORA:** Before we get to AI, what are the big shifts in the industry?

**JAMES:** I'd pick five. The first is the one Suveto is built on: shared and employee ownership as an alternative to the corporate roll-up. Suveto markets that directly.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Consolidation continues at scale. Banfield with more than a thousand hospitals, IVC Evidensia with around two and a half thousand locations. The big groups keep getting bigger.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Talent and well-being are now competitive weapons. Programs for students, new graduates and owners. Suveto has its well-being role, its externship content and Harbor. The research didn't find a market figure on the vet shortage, so I won't give one, but the way the whole industry behaves tells you hiring and keeping vets is hard.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Telemedicine and round-the-clock advice. Suveto's own October 2025 article on what vets want from employers in 2026 mentions telemedicine as a general industry trend. It doesn't present it as a Suveto product. Banfield already sells Pet Chat.

**NORA:** And fifth, I suspect, is AI.

**JAMES:** Yes. AI is entering the category through practice software and marketing tools. In 2023, Suveto published an article giving practices advice on using AI for marketing and customer relationship management. I want to be precise about that. It's advice to vets about marketing, not evidence of Suveto using AI in its own operations.

**NORA:** So what does all this add up to?

**JAMES:** In my view, a business where the constraint is professional time. Vets and technicians are scarce, they're expensive, and they're the reason the hospital exists. Anything that gives them more time with patients, or keeps them from burning out, is valuable. That's exactly where AI is showing up first in veterinary care, from what we see in the general market.

### AI at Suveto today

**NORA:** So let's take stock. Where is Suveto with AI, honestly?

**JAMES:** Honestly, nowhere visible. We looked at the main website, the careers pages, the ONE conference agenda, the podcast episode list and the press pages. No AI deployment, no AI product, no AI sessions at the conference. The only AI content is that 2023 marketing advice article.

**NORA:** Does that mean they're doing nothing?

**JAMES:** Not necessarily. A private company with a chief technology officer may well be piloting things it hasn't talked about. Individual hospitals in the network may be using AI tools through their practice software or on their own. We just can't see it. And I'd rather say that clearly than guess.

**NORA:** What about the data foundations? Do we know what systems the hospitals run on?

**JAMES:** We don't. Suveto doesn't publicly name a single practice software platform, an analytics platform or a data stack. And here's my inference: when a network grows by acquiring independent hospitals, each one arrives with whatever system it was using. Unless the network has done the hard work of migrating them, you end up with several different systems across the group. That's common in roll-ups generally, but I want to label it as our guess for Suveto specifically.

**NORA:** And security? Certifications?

**JAMES:** We didn't find any public security certification. Again, that doesn't mean there isn't one. It means it isn't published.

**NORA:** You mentioned the data hygiene issue earlier with the hospital counts.

**JAMES:** Yes, and I'll say this carefully, as an inference. If the company's own website gives four different hospital counts, it suggests that keeping one clean, central source of truth isn't yet a strength. That's not a scandal. It's a website. But AI runs on clean, consistent data, so it's a small signal worth noticing.

**NORA:** So your overall read?

**JAMES:** Suveto is at the very start of the AI journey, at least publicly. It has a strong network, a clear ownership pitch, real operating programs like treatment acceptance and forward booking, and a tech leader. What it doesn't yet have, visibly, is a unified data layer or any AI in a core workflow. In our view that's actually a reasonable place to start from, because the first AI wins in this industry don't require inventing anything.

**NORA:** Let me ask about the people side of an AI rollout here. Who has to be won over?

**JAMES:** I'd think about four groups. The vets, who own the medicine and, in many cases, a slice of the equity. The credentialed technicians, who do a huge share of the hands-on care and a lot of the record-keeping. The front-desk teams, who carry the phones, the check-ins and the payments. And the practice managers, who make each hospital run day to day.

**NORA:** And what does each group care about?

**JAMES:** Vets care about accuracy and autonomy. A tool that writes a sloppy record or nudges them toward a treatment will be rejected. Technicians care about workload and being respected for their skills. Front-desk staff care about not being overwhelmed, and about not being replaced, which is a reasonable worry when you talk about phone AI. And practice managers care about whether the tool actually works on their system without a month of disruption.

**NORA:** So the messaging has to be different for each.

**JAMES:** It does. For the front desk, I'd be explicit: the phone assistant takes the overflow and the after-hours calls, so you can look after the family standing in front of you. Whether that's true depends on how the network uses the savings, so leadership has to mean it.

### A day at a network hospital

**NORA:** Let's make this concrete. Walk me through a day at a hospital in a network like this. Invented, not real.

**JAMES:** Let's invent one. A three-vet general practice in a mid-sized town, call it Maple Street Animal Hospital. The founding vet, Dr. Ruiz, sold to a network a couple of years ago and still practices there. Two younger associate vets, a team of technicians and assistants, and two people at the front desk.

**NORA:** Seven thirty in the morning.

**JAMES:** The front desk opens the phones. There's already a backlog of voicemails from overnight. A dog that vomited twice. A cat that needs a prescription refill. Someone asking about prices for a dental cleaning. Someone who just wants to move an appointment. The receptionists start working through them while also checking in the first patients of the day.

**NORA:** And some of those voicemails won't get returned until lunchtime.

**JAMES:** And some won't get returned at all, because the phone keeps ringing. Every missed call is potentially a missed booking. The family with the vomiting dog might just go to the emergency clinic down the road.

**NORA:** Then the appointments start.

**JAMES:** Dr. Ruiz sees a ten-year-old Labrador for a check-up. She examines the dog, talks to the family, recommends bloodwork and a dental cleaning. Maybe they say yes to the bloodwork and "we'll think about it" to the dental. She moves to the next room. In between, she's meant to write up the medical record for the Labrador. But the next patient is waiting, so the notes pile up.

**NORA:** When does she write them?

**JAMES:** In a lot of practices, at the end of the day, or in the evening at home. That's one of the most common complaints in the profession, from what we understand generally: hours of record-keeping after the clinic closes. That's time away from family, and it's a big contributor to burnout.

**NORA:** And the "we'll think about it" on the dental?

**JAMES:** That's treatment acceptance. If nobody follows up, the dental probably never happens. The dog's teeth get worse, and the hospital loses the revenue. Meanwhile, when the family checks out, the receptionist is supposed to book the next visit, that's forward booking, but there's a queue and she forgets.

**NORA:** So in one ordinary morning, we've seen missed calls, notes piling up, a declined treatment and a missed rebooking.

**JAMES:** Exactly. None of those are dramatic. But multiply them across seventy-five hospitals and three hundred-plus days a year and they add up to a lot of lost time, lost revenue and tired staff.

**NORA:** And where does the network's back office fit into Dr. Ruiz's day?

**JAMES:** Mostly invisibly, which is the point. She didn't run payroll this week. She didn't negotiate the drug supply contract. She didn't write the job ad for the receptionist who quit. That's the value of the model. But the network's back office, as far as we can see, doesn't yet help her with the medical record, the phone backlog or the follow-ups. That's where AI comes in.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about the challenges. What would worry you if you were on the board?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. The first is fragmented systems. If acquired hospitals run different practice software, then building network-wide reporting, benchmarking or AI is much harder. That's an inference, but it's the classic roll-up problem.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, data and disclosure. The inconsistent hospital counts, no published operating metrics, no named data platform. Internally, I'd bet they know their numbers. But if a future buyer asks for clean, comparable data across every hospital, it needs to exist and be trustworthy.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, autonomy cuts both ways. The whole pitch is that vets keep medical decision-making. That's a great promise for recruiting sellers. But it makes it harder to standardise. If the network wants every hospital to adopt a new tool, it can't just order them to. It has to persuade. Independent-minded vets are, by nature, independent-minded.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, debt. PitchBook lists acquisition debt as the latest deal type. Borrowing to buy hospitals works well when volumes and prices grow. If interest rates stay high or visit volumes soften, debt service squeezes cash. We don't know the size of the debt, so this is a general risk, not a specific one.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, people. If vets and technicians are scarce and wages rise, the economics of every hospital get tighter. The ownership pitch helps retention, but it doesn't create more vets. And the shared-ownership promise only works if the equity is worth something, which depends on the whole network performing.

**NORA:** Is there a sixth? You haven't mentioned the buyer's view.

**JAMES:** There is, and it ties these together. Picture a future buyer's due diligence team. They'll want hospital-by-hospital revenue, visit counts, treatment acceptance, staff turnover and pricing, all on a consistent basis. If those numbers live in a dozen different practice systems and spreadsheets, the diligence is slow and the buyer applies a discount for uncertainty. If they live in one clean data layer, the story is easier to tell and easier to believe. So data work isn't just an operating project. It's an exit project.

**NORA:** That's an interesting point. The equity plan is a retention tool, but it's also a bet on the exit.

**JAMES:** Right. A vet who holds equity cares a lot about what the group is eventually sold for. That aligns incentives nicely. But it also means the people in the exam rooms are, in a small way, investors in the private equity outcome.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's move to opportunities. If you were advising Suveto, where would you push?

**JAMES:** I'll group them. The first and biggest, in my view, is AI help with visit documentation and discharge notes across the network. The vet talks to the family and examines the animal, and an AI tool listens, with consent, and drafts the medical record and the take-home instructions. The vet reviews and signs.

**NORA:** That's the AI scribe idea.

**JAMES:** Yes. In human medicine, AI scribes have become one of the most widely adopted AI tools, and in veterinary care there are now a number of vendors offering the same thing, based on general knowledge of the market. The payoff is in vet and technician hours, which in a hospital business is the biggest cost and the scarcest resource. I can't size the value for Suveto, because we don't have visit volumes or minutes per record. But the logic is strong.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Standardise the practice software and build one data layer across the network. Not glamorous, but foundational. Once every hospital's appointments, treatments, pricing and outcomes flow into one place, the network can benchmark. Which hospitals have the best treatment acceptance? Which have the strongest forward booking? How do prices compare? And the ONE conference already teaches those measures, so the concepts exist.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Phone and booking AI for the front desk. An AI assistant that answers overflow calls, books and moves appointments, handles refill requests, and routes the urgent ones to a human straight away. The payoff is revenue from calls that would otherwise be missed, and it plugs into the forward booking workflow the network already trains on.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Treatment acceptance analytics, by vet and by hospital. Today, as far as we can see, the treatment acceptance program is human-led, workshops and coaching. Analytics would support that by showing where the gaps are. And AI could help draft clear, friendly follow-up messages for the "we'll think about it" families, reviewed by the team.

**NORA:** And the non-AI opportunities?

**JAMES:** Group purchasing is one. Zoetis, Elanco, Merck Animal Health and Purina were at the network's conference. With seventy-five-plus hospitals buying drugs and supplies, there should be real buying power. Another is cross-selling financing through Calico at checkout, linked to that treatment acceptance problem. If a family declines the dental because of cost, a payment plan might change the answer. And the third is geographic density. Adding hospitals in states where the network already operates can cut regional overhead and make shared services more efficient. Suveto hasn't published an acquisition pipeline, so that's our inference.

**NORA:** How do those fit together?

**JAMES:** Nicely, actually. Better data tells you where treatment acceptance is weak. AI drafts the follow-up. Calico removes the cost barrier. Forward booking secures the next visit. It's one patient journey, supported at each step.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI use case to bet the next eighteen months on, what is it?

**JAMES:** AI-assisted visit documentation and discharge notes. It's network-wide, it uses data the hospitals already create, it doesn't require Suveto to build a product, and it addresses the profession's biggest pain point: hours of paperwork. It also supports the talent story. If you can tell a vet "you'll go home on time," that's a recruiting message.

**NORA:** And how would you run it, in the first hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: pick eight to ten pilot hospitals that volunteer. Volunteers matter, because autonomy is the culture. Choose hospitals on different practice software, so you learn about integration early. Agree the measures up front: minutes spent on records per visit, how many records are finished the same day, and how the vets feel about it. And sort out consent, so pet owners know when a visit is being recorded and how the data is handled.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Run the tool. I'd test two vendors side by side, because this market is moving fast. Have the vets review every draft, and ask the chief veterinary officer's team to audit a sample for accuracy. A scribe that invents a symptom is worse than no scribe.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Compare results. If notes are getting done faster and the vets want to keep it, pick a vendor, negotiate network pricing, and plan the rollout wave by wave. And here's the key: let the pilot vets present the results at the next ONE conference. Vets trust other vets far more than they trust a central office.

**NORA:** That's the peer-to-peer adoption idea.

**JAMES:** Exactly. In a network built on clinical autonomy, you don't mandate. You make the early adopters visible and let the results speak.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** One person, with clinical credibility and the authority to make decisions. I'd pair the chief technology officer with someone from the chief veterinary officer's team, but with one clear owner accountable for the result. Split ownership is how pilots drift.

**NORA:** What about the privacy side? Recording a conversation in an exam room feels sensitive.

**JAMES:** It is, even though it's an animal patient. The conversation involves a family, their home life, sometimes their finances. So I'd want a clear consent step, a published policy on how recordings are stored and deleted, and a contract with the vendor that says the network's data isn't used to train models for others without permission. Veterinary practice is also regulated by state boards, which generally set rules about medical records. We didn't research those rules in detail, so I'd simply say the network should check each state's requirements before rolling out.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** Phone and booking AI. The value is easy to see in missed calls and bookings, it's low clinical risk, and it frees front-desk staff for the people standing in front of them.

### A second walk-through: the follow-up that worked

**NORA:** Let's go back to Maple Street Animal Hospital. Replay that day, with the tools you've described. Still invented.

**JAMES:** Seven thirty, the phones open. Overnight, an AI phone assistant has already handled the refill request, logged it for the vet to approve, and moved the appointment the family wanted to change. The vomiting dog's message was flagged as urgent, so the first receptionist calls that family right away. The price question for the dental got a standard answer and an offer to book a consult.

**NORA:** So the backlog shrank before anyone sat down.

**JAMES:** Right. And the receptionists are spending more time with the people in the lobby. Now Dr. Ruiz sees the Labrador. With the family's consent, the scribe tool listens to the visit. By the time she walks out of the room, there's a draft record and a draft discharge note for the family, in plain language. She spends a minute reviewing it, fixes one detail, signs it.

**NORA:** And the dental?

**JAMES:** The family still says "we'll think about it." But this time, two days later, they get a follow-up message reminding them why the dental matters, drafted by AI and approved by the clinic team. It mentions that payment plans are available through the network's lending arm. A week later, they book.

**NORA:** And at the end of the day?

**JAMES:** Dr. Ruiz goes home at six, not eight, with her records done. And across the network, the central team can see that Maple Street's treatment acceptance is improving and its forward booking rate is among the best. They ask Dr. Ruiz to share what's working at the next conference.

**NORA:** That's a nice story. But let me push back. Isn't there a risk this feels like the corporate pressure the sellers were worried about? Follow-ups, conversion rates, dashboards?

**JAMES:** That's a really important pushback, and I think it's the central tension. There's a fine line between helping a vet follow up on care she recommended and pushing families to buy things. The way to stay on the right side, in my view, is that the vet decides the recommendation, the vet's team approves the message, and the measures are framed around care completed, not revenue extracted. If the dental was medically needed, getting it done is good medicine.

**NORA:** And if the vets feel monitored?

**JAMES:** Then adoption fails. That's why I'd start with the scribe, which gives time back to the vet, before anything that looks like performance tracking. Earn trust first.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Suveto?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, your ownership model is a product. Suveto doesn't just buy hospitals. It sells a deal: full value, equity, autonomy. That's a differentiated offer in a market where the big players have much more money. Any roll-up competing against larger buyers can learn from that.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, your first AI win doesn't have to be built. In veterinary care, the obvious first tools, scribes and phone assistants, exist from vendors. The skill is choosing well, piloting fairly and getting people to use them. A lot of companies get stuck thinking AI strategy means building models. Often it means good procurement and good change management.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, clean up the data before you need it. A roll-up that has different systems in every location will eventually hit a wall, whether that's AI, benchmarking or a buyer's due diligence. The best time to standardise is before it becomes urgent.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, in a professional business, adoption runs on trust and peers. Vets, lawyers, engineers, doctors: professionals adopt tools when they see colleagues they respect using them successfully, and when the tool clearly serves them, not just the head office. If you lead with time back for the professional, you earn the right to do the harder things later.

**NORA:** I'd add a fifth from our conversation. Say what you are clearly.

**JAMES:** I like that. Four different hospital counts on one website is small, but it's a symptom. Clear public facts build trust with sellers, recruits and buyers alike.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, scale competitors with bigger AI budgets. Banfield and IVC Evidensia can outspend a seventy-five-hospital network on software and data. If AI becomes a real differentiator in vet care, the giants have an advantage. That's our inference.

**NORA:** Two?

**JAMES:** Two, AI and twenty-four-seven advice could reduce low-acuity visits. If a family can get a good answer from a chat service about whether their dog's mild upset stomach needs a visit, some of those visits disappear. Banfield already offers Pet Chat. That's a speculative risk, and it could also free capacity for sicker animals, but it's worth thinking about.

**NORA:** Three?

**JAMES:** Three, vet labour scarcity and wage pressure. We didn't find a figure in our sources, but the industry's behaviour says talent is tight. Rising wages squeeze hospital margins, and the ownership pitch only partly offsets that.

**NORA:** Four?

**JAMES:** Four, the debt-funded roll-up risk. Heavy borrowing plus slower growth at existing hospitals is the classic squeeze. And five, exit timing. Six years into the hold, the owners will want a clear story for a buyer. Clean data, a unified platform and some proof of operational improvement, including AI, would strengthen that story.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any announcement that Suveto has standardised its hospitals on one practice software platform. Second, any public AI pilot, especially scribes or phone assistants. Third, a consistent, current hospital count and any published operating metrics. And fourth, any news on the ownership, whether that's a recapitalisation, a sale or a big add-on.
