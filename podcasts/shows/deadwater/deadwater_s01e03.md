# DEADWATER
## Episode 3: "The Department"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] In the spring of 2019, the chief of the South Shore Police Department was arrested in the parking garage of a restaurant in Garden City. He was taken into custody by federal agents who had spent three years building the case against him. The charges included conspiracy, obstruction of justice, and witness tampering in connection with a sustained cover-up of a brutal assault committed by officers under his command on a man in their custody. He pleaded guilty. He went to prison.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] I want to be careful about what I am and am not saying here. I am not saying that Chief Gary Holden was connected to the Deadwater murders. He was not. The investigation and prosecution of Roland Voss has no direct connection to the corruption case that ended Holden's career. They are two separate matters involving two separate sets of institutional failures.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What I am saying is this. The South Shore Police Department was led, for the most critical years of the Deadwater investigation, by a man who had built a culture of institutional self-protection so pervasive and so normalized that it had contaminated every significant decision the department made. A culture where loyalty to the institution, defined as loyalty to the people inside it, consistently took precedence over accountability to the public it was supposed to serve. Where the question of how something would look for the department was asked before the question of what the right thing to do was. Where certain categories of victims - women who were poor, women who worked in the sex trade, women who did not fit the profile of who the department's institutional attention was designed for - were deprioritized in ways that were never written down and never formally decided but that showed up, with painful consistency, in where the resources went and where they did not.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This episode is about that culture. Not because it explains everything that went wrong with the Deadwater investigation - it doesn't. But because understanding the institutional environment the investigation lived inside is necessary for understanding why it took as long as it took and why the things that went wrong went wrong.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater, Episode Three: The Department.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh joined the South Shore Police Department in 1998. She had done a year of graduate work in criminology after finishing her undergraduate degree and then decided she wanted to be in the field, not studying it. She walked a beat for four years in one of the department's more demanding precincts, transferred to narcotics in 2002, moved to homicide in 2006. Three years later the bodies were found at the barrier island.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She has spent her entire career inside one institution. She knows it the way you know a place you have lived for a quarter century - its rhythms, its internal politics, its spoken and unspoken rules, the distance between what it says about itself and what it actually does. She is a careful woman who has learned to be careful specifically because the environment she works in rewards caution in people who look like her and punishes the kind of directness that a different person in a different position might get away with.

[MARSH] I was one of very few women in the homicide unit when I joined. That is not a complaint - it is a factual description of the environment. And that environment had a culture, shaped over decades, that made certain assumptions about whose judgment was worth trusting and whose needed to be more thoroughly demonstrated before it was accepted. I learned early to document everything. To have the evidence for my positions assembled before I raised them. To be right in ways that were so clearly right that disagreement would be difficult. That is the overhead cost of operating in a particular kind of institutional environment. It is real and it is significant and it directly shaped how I ran the Deadwater investigation.

[NARRATOR] Gary Holden became chief in 2008, approximately a year before the discovery. He came up through what longtime officers of the department call the old guard - a cohort of men who had joined in the late seventies and early eighties, who shared a specific culture of institutional loyalty that was defined as loyalty to each other rather than to the public. He was politically skilled, good at managing upward, effective at presenting the department to county leadership and to the press in ways that emphasized stability and minimized scrutiny.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He was also, as would become clear in a federal courtroom a decade later, willing to protect officers who had committed serious misconduct in ways that compromised the integrity of the department's leadership for most of his tenure.

[REESE] The Holden corruption had a specific domain - protecting officers who had committed physical misconduct against people in custody. That is not the same thing as suppressing the Deadwater investigation. I want to be absolutely clear about that because it matters. What the Holden culture did to Deadwater was indirect. It created an institutional default mode where protecting the department from reputational damage consistently outweighed other priorities. And in a case where the victims were women who worked in the sex trade - women who did not generate political pressure, women whose families were not well-connected, women who a certain institutional mentality would not consider the department's most important priority - that default mode meant there was never sufficient pressure from above to keep the investigation at full resource intensity.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Nobody sat in a room and decided these women mattered less. That's not how it works. It works through budget decisions that seem reasonable on their face. Through the absence of internal pressure to do more. Through the way that cases generating no political heat get quietly deprioritized, incrementally, until what's left is two people doing their genuine best against something that should have thirty people working it. Our genuine best was real. It was just insufficient.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In 2013, four years into the reduced-resource phase of the investigation, a deputy commissioner with concerns about the pace of the case quietly commissions an independent review. He hires a retired federal investigator with no prior connection to the SSPD. The review takes eight months. It covers every significant investigative decision made in the Deadwater case from the discovery through the review period. It identifies specific failures and specific missed opportunities in analytical and accessible language.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is never officially released.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh does not know it exists. She is not consulted in its preparation. For three years the document sits in a department file, available to senior command and apparently read by almost nobody.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She learns about it in 2016 when a source inside the department - someone who has watched her work this case for a decade and has reached the point where remaining silent feels like a moral failure - sends her a two-page summary as an email attachment late on a Tuesday night.

[MARSH] I was at home when the notification came. My husband was asleep. I went to the kitchen and sat at the table and opened my laptop and I read those two pages. Twice. Very carefully. And then I sat there until about two in the morning without moving, thinking about what I had just read.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] The summary described four categories of failure. Missing persons reports not cross-referenced with the victim profile on an adequate timeline. A witness account from the second year that had been logged but not followed through to a full interview. A vehicle description that had been entered into the system and never properly actioned. And a database cross-reference that had been identified as a recommended investigative action and had been executed with parameters too narrow to surface the relevant record.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] That fourth one. A database query run with wrong parameters. That is the one that kept me at the kitchen table until two in the morning.

[NARRATOR] The database in question is the county parking enforcement system. Early in the investigation, the team running background checks on vehicles in the target area recommended a cross-reference: match vehicle descriptions from witness accounts against parking enforcement records for the barrier island corridor during the relevant period. The cross-reference was assigned to an analyst who was working under significant time pressure and who set the query parameters to include vehicles registered in Suffolk and Nassau counties - the local area.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It was a reasonable scope decision. It was wrong.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss's dark green Chevrolet Avalanche pickup truck was registered not to his Massapequa Park home address but to his architecture firm's Manhattan business address. It was not in the local county registration subset. When the query ran, it did not appear.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The citation that had been issued to that truck - at the barrier island access point lot, six months before the bodies were found - was in the database. It was simply invisible behind the query's geographic filter.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] One parameter set one degree too narrow. Nine years.

[MARSH] I have trained myself not to calculate what nine years means in human terms. I know the number. I know what it represents. I do not let myself say it out loud anymore because it is not useful and because it is genuinely painful in a way that I don't find productive. What I will say is that the families waited nine years longer than the evidence warranted. That is real, whatever else is also true.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey learns about the internal review from Carol Marsh in 2017, a year after Marsh receives the two-page summary. Marsh tells her in general terms - a review exists, it identified failures, it has not been released publicly, there may be legal avenues to pursue that release.

[DONNA] She told me carefully. She was threading a needle between giving me information I deserved to have and not putting herself in an impossible position professionally. I understood the constraint. I was not going to make her situation harder. But I was going to do something with what she'd told me.

[NARRATOR] Donna retains a civil rights attorney with specific experience in police accountability litigation. The attorney files Freedom of Information requests for the review document. The department denies each request. The attorney challenges. The challenge moves through the administrative appeals process for eighteen months, generating filings that attract attention - local journalists pick up the story of the victim families seeking the review, it runs in regional papers, county legislators receive letters.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The pressure accumulates. After eighteen months, a settlement is reached. A redacted version of the review is released to the families' legal representative. Thirty-one percent of the content is redacted. What remains is enough.

[DONNA] I read it and I was not surprised by the substance. I had been watching this investigation closely enough to have a fairly accurate sense of where the failures were. What hit me was reading it in the department's own words. Methodical, documented, specific. They had written it themselves. They had reviewed it internally. And then they had filed it away.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I want to be fair about one thing. The review also documented what the investigation did right. The forensic work, especially the DNA preservation. The geographic analysis that Danny built. The sustained effort by Carol and her team under conditions of inadequate resource. Those were genuine achievements and the review said so. The failure is not that the investigation was incompetent. The failure is that it was compromised by a culture that didn't give it what it needed, and by specific technical decisions that were wrong in ways nobody caught in time.

[NARRATOR] Donna takes the review to Albany. She testifies before two legislative committees about its findings. Her testimony is detailed and precise, built on years of educating herself in investigative procedure and in the specific mechanics of what went wrong.

[DONNA] People would ask me how I knew all this. How I understood database cross-referencing requirements and missing persons coordination protocols and the specific technical details of what should have happened. And the answer is: I read. I had been reading since Renee disappeared. I read court documents and academic papers and investigative methodology literature and anything else I could find that would help me understand this specific system and its specific failures. Because understanding something is the first step to changing it, and I was not interested in stopping at understanding.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The legislation that results from her testimony passes two years after her first committee appearance. It requires that missing persons reports filed in New York State be automatically cross-referenced against open homicide investigation databases within thirty days of filing, using search parameters that include commercial vehicle registrations and business addresses across the full metropolitan region - not just local county registrations.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] One of the explicit amendments to the bill, added in the final committee markup, is a requirement that database queries in cold case investigations be documented with their specific parameter settings and reviewed annually for appropriateness.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The bill is named the Renee Kelsey Missing Persons Coordination Act.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] Renee would have found the whole thing mortifying. She hated having attention on her. She would have made some deflecting joke and then probably cried when she thought I wasn't looking. That was her. She felt things deeply and she didn't like you to know it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She pauses. Looks at her hands.

[DONNA] I miss her. Every day, in very ordinary ways. I miss calling her on the phone. I miss her bad coffee and her too-loud laugh. I miss having someone who knew me before I became whoever this version of me is. The investigation is resolved. The person responsible is in prison. None of that touches those ordinary daily absences. They are just there. They are just what it is.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Chief Holden retires in the spring of 2019. The retirement is announced as routine transition. Three months later he is in a parking garage in Garden City, being handcuffed by federal agents. The department enters a period of significant internal disruption: two interim commissioners in eighteen months, a federal oversight agreement requiring substantial procedural reforms, an internal affairs process that reshapes the leadership structure.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The Deadwater investigation, during this period, reaches its lowest active resource level. Carol Marsh is the primary detective. She carries four other open homicide cases simultaneously. She does what she can, which is not enough, and she knows it, and she documents that knowledge in a series of internal memos that she files knowing they are unlikely to produce immediate results but that will matter later when the right person reads them.

[MARSH] Every significant resource request I made in those years, I wrote down. Every time I flagged a lead and was told resources would be assessed and then nothing happened, I documented it. Every time I identified an investigative action that should be taken and couldn't be taken because of capacity, I noted it. I was building the brief for a task force that I never stopped believing was coming. I did not know when or who would authorize it. But I was writing the document they would read when they did.

[REESE] That documentation is the reason we got the task force when we did. When the new commissioner came in and did his cold case review, he found Carol's memos in the file. He read them. He called her in for four hours. She had been building the road map for two years before anyone was willing to drive it.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The new commissioner arrives in early 2022. He comes from a federal background and has led institutional reforms in two other departments under external oversight. He is not interested in protecting the institution from scrutiny. He is interested in making it function. He conducts a personal review of every major cold case in the department's jurisdiction in his first month. He reads the files himself. Not summaries.

[MARSH] He called me in on a Tuesday morning in February. I had never had a conversation with anyone at commissioner level about Deadwater that lasted more than twenty minutes. That one lasted four hours.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] He had read everything. The case file. My memos. The internal review. The cell cluster analysis. Danny's geographic work. Priscilla's witness account. He had annotated them. He asked me specific questions about specific decisions made in specific years, and he did not accept vague answers. He wanted to understand exactly what had been done and not done and why.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] At the end of four hours he said: what do you need?

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I had been waiting twelve years to be asked that question by someone with the authority to act on the answer. I told him exactly what I needed. Twelve detectives of my choosing. A dedicated digital forensics team. A forensic genealogy consultant. Task force level cooperation from the FBI and state police. Access to the full unredacted internal review from 2013. And authority to run the investigation without interference from above.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] He said: you have it. All of it. When do you want to start?

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I said: I already started. I've been started for twelve years. But now we can actually finish.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The task force is formally constituted in February 2022. Twelve detectives, handpicked by Marsh. Dedicated digital forensics from the county crime lab. Dr. Nina Park as forensic genealogy and DNA lead. FBI and state police at full task force level. A dedicated operations space with its own case management system. A direct reporting line to the commissioner's office.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The first day, Marsh puts ten photographs on the table in front of the assembled team. The ten victims. She says: these are who we are here for. Not the case number. Not the institutional reputation. These people.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Then she says: we have twelve years of work and we have a suspect. Let me tell you what we know and what we need to prove.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She has been building toward this briefing for twelve years. She delivers it in forty minutes. It is, by every account from everyone in the room, one of the most precise and comprehensive investigative briefings they have ever sat through.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] At the end of it, she asks if anyone has questions. Nobody does. They go to work.

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: The List. The task force builds its full picture of Roland Voss - and discovers that hiding in plain sight works exactly as well as it sounds until it doesn't.

## Extended Act: What Was Lost

[NARRATOR] I want to spend some time on a question that I have been asked in almost every conversation I have had about the Deadwater investigation with people who were not directly involved in it. The question is a version of the same thing every time: how did this happen? How do you have ten victims, ten sets of remains found in the same location by the same searchers on the same days, and not solve the case for twelve years?

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The honest answer has several parts. The first part is about technology - the forensic genealogy methodology that eventually identified Voss did not exist in operationally usable form when the investigation began. The hair sample was preserved correctly, but the analytical tools to use it were a decade away. That is a real limitation that cannot be laid at anyone's feet. Science moves at its own pace.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The second part is about the database query. The parking citation that would have put Roland Voss's name in front of investigators in the second year of the case was in the database. It was invisible because the query ran with a parameter set slightly too narrow. That is an error. It is a consequential error. It is also the kind of error that is almost impossible to see in advance - you do not know to widen a parameter until you know what it's supposed to catch.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The third part is about resources. The investigation was resourced at approximately a third of what it needed to be, for approximately two-thirds of its duration. The reasons for that are partly institutional - the culture of the Holden department, the deprioritization of certain categories of victims - and partly practical - budgets are finite, cases compete for attention, and the absence of new physical evidence makes it hard to justify sustained large-scale resource commitment.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] None of these explanations is satisfying. None of them is meant to be. Ten women were murdered. The case took twelve years. Something went wrong in ways that should have gone differently.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What I want to spend some time with here is the thing that went wrong that is hardest to talk about cleanly. The thing that is less about specific technical errors and more about a broader institutional disposition toward specific kinds of victims.

[REESE] I am going to be direct about this because I think it deserves directness. The women who were killed in the Deadwater case shared certain characteristics. They worked in the sex trade. They were economically vulnerable. Several of them were women of color. Several of them had histories with the criminal justice system. And in the period when the investigation was running at reduced capacity, when the decisions were being made about where to put the limited resources available, those characteristics shaped the institutional response in ways that nobody ever wrote down and that would be denied in any formal review.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I am not saying anyone explicitly decided these women mattered less. I want to be precise about that, because the difference between explicit decisions and cultural dispositions matters. Explicit decisions are accountability problems. Cultural dispositions are systemic problems. And systemic problems are harder to fix because they do not have a specific villain - they have a context. The context was the Holden department's culture, but it was also a broader cultural disposition in law enforcement toward victims who do not fit the ideal template of victimhood. And that broader disposition did not begin with Holden and did not end with his arrest.

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey has thought about this more carefully and more systematically than anyone else I have interviewed for this series. It is the animating concern of the legislative and advocacy work she has spent more than a decade doing.

[DONNA] The families in our network come from a specific subset of the missing and murdered: women who worked in the sex trade, women who were economically marginalized, women who did not fit the profile of who the system imagines when it thinks about victims worth protecting. And what we have found, consistently, across different jurisdictions and different cases and different years, is that those characteristics correlate with reduced investigative response. Not in every case. Not in every department. But consistently enough that it is not coincidence.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I want to be careful not to be reductive about this. There are investigators who have worked these cases with extraordinary commitment and care regardless of who the victims are. Carol Marsh is the obvious example from this specific case, but she is not unique. There are good people in law enforcement who are doing the right thing every day. What I'm describing is a structural tendency, not a universal individual failure.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] But structural tendencies have real consequences. The missing persons reports that weren't cross-referenced. The tip line calls that weren't followed up. The database queries that were run with parameters that excluded certain types of registrations. Each of those is an individual decision that has an individual explanation. Together they form a pattern. And the pattern correlates with who the victims were.

[NARRATOR] The Renee Kelsey Act, the legislation Donna shepherded through Albany, is one attempt to address the structural dimension of this. By requiring standardized cross-referencing protocols that apply regardless of the identity of the missing person, it removes one point of discretionary decision-making that was being exercised in ways that were not equitable.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] But Donna is clear that one law, however well-crafted, does not address a cultural disposition. The law addresses a procedural failure. The cultural disposition requires a different kind of change.

[DONNA] We are working on it. The coalition I'm part of nationally is pushing for mandatory bias training in missing persons intake processes, for disaggregated reporting on investigation resource allocation by victim demographic, for independent oversight of cold case prioritization decisions. These are longer fights than the Renee Act. They do not have a clear finish line. But they are the fights that matter for the long term.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh has been active in every public conversation about the Deadwater case since the arrest of Roland Voss. She is careful about what she says and how she says it. She is not interested in self-promotion. She is also not interested in letting the institutional failures of the investigation be obscured by the ultimate success of it.

[MARSH] There is a tendency, when a case like this is resolved, to write a narrative where the resolution justifies everything that preceded it. Where the fact that we got him means the investigation was fundamentally sound. I resist that narrative because it is not honest. The investigation succeeded despite significant failures that cost time and prolonged the suffering of families who deserved answers sooner. Those failures should be documented, understood, and used to improve what comes after. The resolution is real and it matters. The failures are also real and they also matter.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] The things I'm most proud of in the Deadwater investigation are things that happened in spite of the institutional constraints rather than because of them. The forensic evidence preservation in those early years - that was Nina Park operating with extraordinary professional discipline under conditions that did not provide her with nearly the resources she needed. The geographic analysis that eventually pointed at the right area - that was Danny Reese doing independent analytical work on his own time because the case management system wasn't configured to support that kind of thinking. The sustained connection with the families - that was a personal commitment I made on the first day and honored because I believed it was right, not because the institution supported it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] The investigation succeeded because some individuals operated above the institutional baseline. The institutional baseline should have been higher. That is the lesson I want people to take away.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The internal review from 2013 is now publicly available in its redacted form. It can be found in the case archive published by the Suffolk County district attorney's office in connection with the Voss prosecution. If you want to understand what the investigation looked like from the inside, during the years when it was struggling, it is worth reading.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What strikes me most about it, having read it several times, is not the failures it documents. It is the language. The careful, measured, institutional language of a document written by a retired federal investigator who understood exactly what he was looking at and chose words that would survive legal scrutiny and administrative review and the indefinite future of a case that had not yet been resolved. Words that would be true regardless of outcome.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The words that describe the parking citation cross-reference failure are: "the database query was executed with geographic parameters that may not have been sufficiently broad to surface all potentially relevant records." May not have been. Sufficiently broad. Potentially relevant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Nine years of additional waiting for nine families, described in fourteen words that contain three qualifications.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] That is how institutions process their own failures. Carefully. Cautiously. With enough hedging to survive whatever comes next.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The families do not have the luxury of hedging. Their grief is not qualified.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: Twelve Years of Silence. The long years of the cold investigation and the people who refused, through all of them, to stop.

## Extended Act: The Review and Its Consequences

[NARRATOR] The 2013 internal review - the document that Carol Marsh learns about through an email at eleven-thirty on a Tuesday night, three years after it was completed - is seventy-four pages in its full, unredacted form. Donna Kelsey's attorney eventually obtains the redacted version through the administrative challenge process, and the redacted version is fifty-two pages. Thirty-one percent of the content is hidden behind black bars.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] I have read the redacted version multiple times in the course of reporting this series. What it contains is precise and damning and written in the measured language of a retired federal investigator who understood that he was producing a document that would eventually have a legal life beyond the administrative context for which it was originally intended.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The document opens with a methodology section describing how the review was conducted. Forty-seven interviews. Complete review of the case management archive. Review of the budget allocation records. Review of the tip log. Review of the forensic evidence chain of custody. Six months of document review and nine months of analysis and writing.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What strikes me most about the document, reading it now with the benefit of knowing how the case eventually resolved, is how much it already knew. How close it came, in its analysis of what went wrong, to identifying specifically what would eventually go right.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The section on the geographic investigation reads: "The investigation's early geographic analysis was methodologically sound and produced conclusions that, while they did not at the time point to a specific individual, would have significantly narrowed the investigative scope if they had been pursued more aggressively. The decision to reduce geographic field resources in favor of digital forensics, while operationally defensible given available leads, may have foreclosed investigative avenues that were not fully exhausted."

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] May have foreclosed. In the language of institutional review, that is a fairly damning statement.

[MARSH] I read that section three times when I finally saw the redacted document. And what I thought was: yes. That is correct. That is exactly what happened. And the extraordinary thing is that someone wrote it down and put it in a file and it sat there for six years while we continued to not pursue those avenues with adequate resources.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The section on the database cross-reference is even more specific. It identifies the parking citation cross-reference as an action that was recommended, documented as recommended, and not executed with the parameters required to surface all relevant records. The document does not name the specific record that was not surfaced - the citation for Voss's vehicle - because the review was completed before Voss was identified as a suspect and the reviewers did not have access to the outcome that would have made that specific citation visible as significant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] But the failure is there, documented with surgical precision. A recommended action. A documented recommendation. An execution that fell short. A consequence that would not be understood for a decade.

[REESE] When I read the database section I thought: someone knew. Someone in 2013 looked at this investigation and understood that a specific thing had been done wrong in a specific way and wrote it down. And nothing changed for nine more years. The document was in the file. The failure was documented. And nothing changed.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That is the thing I find most difficult about the institutional failure story. Not that errors were made - errors are inevitable in a complex investigation running under resource pressure. What is difficult is that the errors were identified and documented and then not acted on. That is a choice. That is an institutional choice to know something and not use the knowledge.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The legislative consequences of the review's release extend well beyond the Renee Kelsey Act. Donna's advocacy organization uses it as a primary reference document in its work across multiple states. It becomes, in effect, a case study for how institutional failures in homicide investigation develop and why they persist.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Three other states have produced similar reviews of specific cold case investigations in the years since the Voss prosecution. Two of those reviews cite the Deadwater internal review and the public advocacy that led to its partial release as a model for what independent cold case review can accomplish.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] None of them have resulted in the identification of a suspect. The work is ongoing.

[DONNA] I want to be clear about what I hope for from these reviews and what I don't hope for. I don't hope that every cold case has a resolution like this one. That would be wonderful but it is not realistic. What I hope for is that the systemic failures that allow these cases to stay cold longer than they need to are identified and corrected. That the next investigation of this kind has better protocols and better resources and better cross-referencing systems from the beginning, so that it doesn't take twelve years to arrive at an answer that should have come in three or four.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] Every year that a case is cold is a year that families wait. That is not an abstraction. I know what that year feels like. I lived twelve of them. I am not willing to be quiet about the systemic failures that make those years necessary.

[NARRATOR] She is not quiet. She has not been quiet in thirteen years and she does not appear to be becoming quieter.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: Twelve Years of Silence. The long maintenance phase of the investigation and the people who refused to let it settle into permanence.
