### What an ESOP is

**NORA:** Before we get to the company, I think we need a quick primer. What exactly is an ESOP?

**JAMES:** Sure, and this is general background rather than anything specific to Blue Ridge. An employee stock ownership plan is a type of retirement plan in the United States. Instead of mainly holding a mix of investments like a typical 401k, it holds shares in the company the employees work for. The shares sit in a trust, and each employee has an account that gradually fills up with shares over their career.

**NORA:** And employees don't buy the shares themselves?

**JAMES:** Usually not, in the typical setup. The company contributes shares, or contributes cash that the plan uses to buy shares, and those get allocated to employees' accounts, often based on pay. Employees then vest over time, which means they gradually earn the right to keep them. When they retire or leave, they get paid out the value of their vested shares, under the plan's rules.

**NORA:** Why would an owner sell to employees rather than to a buyer?

**JAMES:** In general, a few reasons. It's a way for a founder to get liquidity and retire while keeping the company independent and rooted in its community. It can come with tax advantages, which is a big topic I'll leave to the specialists. And many owners simply like the idea of rewarding the people who built the business. The buying of shares is often financed with a loan, which is what people mean by a leveraged ESOP.

**NORA:** And this is all heavily regulated, I assume.

**JAMES:** Very. ESOPs fall under a federal law called the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, usually called ERISA, which sets rules for retirement plans and the duties of the people who run them. There are annual government filings, the main one being Form 5500. There are rules on how shares are valued, usually by an independent appraiser each year. And there are rules about fairness, so that the plan doesn't favour highly paid people over everyone else.

**NORA:** You mentioned employees getting paid out when they leave. Where does that money come from?

**JAMES:** This is one of the less obvious parts of ESOPs, and again it's general background. In a privately held company, there's no stock market where a departing employee can sell their shares. So the company, or the plan, generally has to buy them back. Over time, as more employees retire, that buy-back commitment, often called the repurchase obligation, can become a big number that the company needs to plan for.

**NORA:** So the administrator's numbers feed the company's cash planning too.

**JAMES:** Exactly. A good administrator doesn't just produce this year's statements. It helps the company understand what it may owe departing employees in the years ahead. That depends on who's vesting, who's nearing retirement and what the shares are worth. It's another reason accurate, well-structured data matters so much in this business.

**NORA:** It sounds like the administrator becomes a long-term partner rather than a vendor.

**JAMES:** In general, yes. An ESOP can last for decades, through many changes in the workforce, the share price and the rules. The administrator holds the history of every account. That's part of why clients rarely switch, and why this kind of business is attractive to investors.

**NORA:** So who does all the work?

**JAMES:** There's a cast of characters. A trustee, who acts for the plan. An independent valuation firm. Lawyers. The company's own finance team. And a third-party administrator, or TPA, which is the role Blue Ridge plays. The TPA keeps the participant records, does the allocation and vesting calculations, prepares the compliance testing and filings, and produces the participant statements.

**NORA:** The engine room, basically.

**JAMES:** That's a good way of putting it. If the TPA gets the numbers wrong, every other party is working off bad information. And because the participants are employees saving for retirement, mistakes aren't just embarrassing. They can create legal exposure for the company and the plan's fiduciaries.

### What a TPA actually does

**NORA:** Let's go deeper on the day-to-day work. What does Blue Ridge actually do for a client company each year?

**JAMES:** I'll describe the general shape of ESOP administration, since the company doesn't publish its exact process. It starts with data. The client sends employee census data: who worked there, their pay, hours, dates of hire and termination, and so on. Then there's the valuation, done by an independent appraiser, which sets the share price. And there are details of contributions, loan payments if it's leveraged, and any distributions to people who left.

**NORA:** That's a lot of inputs from a lot of places.

**JAMES:** And they come in all sorts of formats. Spreadsheets, payroll exports, PDF reports from the trustee and the appraiser, letters, emails. A big part of the job is collecting, checking and reconciling that data. If a former employee's termination date is wrong, their vesting is wrong. If pay is missing a bonus, the allocation is wrong.

**NORA:** And then the calculations.

**JAMES:** Right. The TPA allocates shares and cash to accounts according to the plan document, calculates vesting, tracks the release of shares as any loan is paid down, and works out distributions. Then there's compliance testing, which checks that the plan meets the fairness rules. Then the government filing, Form 5500. And finally participant statements, so each employee sees their account.

**NORA:** Where does it go wrong, typically?

**JAMES:** In general, at the data stage. Bad inputs are the root of most problems. And at the edges: unusual events like a rehire, a death, a divorce order that splits an account, or a company transaction. Each of those needs careful handling. That's why experience matters so much in this field, and why the specialists can charge for their expertise.

**NORA:** And the platform Blue Ridge mentions?

**JAMES:** The Ambrose release talks about a dedicated ESOP administration platform, and the company describes itself as technology-enhanced. We don't have details on how that platform works, whether it's built in-house or licensed. But the fact that acquired clients are moved onto it is important. It means Blue Ridge has a single home for client data, at least as a goal.

### A plan year, a hypothetical

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Invent a client for me.

**JAMES:** Sure. A made-up company called Shenandoah Valves. It makes industrial valves, has about three hundred and fifty employees, and became employee-owned eight years ago when its founder retired. It has an ESOP that holds all of its shares, plus a separate 401k plan. Its finance director, Mike, is responsible for working with the administrator.

**NORA:** And the plan year ends. What happens?

**JAMES:** In the first few weeks, Mike's team exports the employee census from payroll. It's a big spreadsheet with three hundred and fifty current employees and about forty who left during the year. Separately, the trustee sends a statement, the independent appraiser sends a valuation report as a long PDF, and the bank sends loan statements. All of this goes to the administrator.

**NORA:** And then the administrator's team gets to work.

**JAMES:** An analyst checks the census. She spots that twelve people have hours missing, that two people who left are listed with no termination date, and that one person appears twice with slightly different spellings. She emails Mike with a list of questions. He sends answers over the next week. She enters the valuation figures from the appraiser's report into the system, checks the loan payments, and runs the allocations.

**NORA:** And then compliance.

**JAMES:** The system runs the fairness tests. One test is close to the limit because the company paid big bonuses to a few senior people. The analyst flags it for a senior reviewer, who checks the plan document and confirms it passes. Then the Form 5500 is prepared and reviewed. And finally, three hundred and ninety participant statements are generated and checked before they go out.

**NORA:** How long does all that take?

**JAMES:** It varies a lot in general, and I don't have Blue Ridge's figures. But what I'd notice is how much of the analyst's time went on reading documents, retyping numbers and chasing questions, rather than on judgment. And that, in my view, is where AI fits naturally.

**NORA:** And then the employees get their statements.

**JAMES:** And here's the second part of the story. When the statements go out, the phone starts ringing. Why did my balance go down? What does vested mean? When can I get my money? Mike's HR team takes some of those calls, and others go to the administrator's participant service team. Across nine hundred and ten thousand participants, that's a lot of questions, and many of them are the same ones over and over.

**NORA:** And some of those employees may not have much financial background.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Which is why the communications side matters. Explaining ownership clearly is part of making it valuable to the people who hold it.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** Let's talk market. How big is ESOP administration?

**JAMES:** We found two estimates, both from market research vendors selling reports, and they conflict. One puts the ESOP administration market at about one point four billion dollars in 2025, growing to about three and a half billion by 2035, which is a bit over nine percent a year. Another puts it at about two point one billion dollars in 2024, growing at almost nine percent a year. I'd treat both as low-confidence. The direction, steady growth, is probably more reliable than the level.

**NORA:** What drives demand?

**JAMES:** The main one cited in the sources is strong demand for new ESOP formations. That's the reason Ambrose gave for refocusing. In general, a lot of business owners are approaching retirement, and selling to employees is one option for them. Every new ESOP needs an administrator, for as long as the plan exists, which can be decades.

**NORA:** So it's a recurring revenue business.

**JAMES:** In general, yes. Once a company picks an administrator, switching is painful, because the records and history are complex. So, our inference is that client relationships tend to be long. That makes administration firms attractive targets for roll-ups: steady revenue, loyal clients, and a fragmented market of small firms.

**NORA:** Why is the market so fragmented? Why are there so many small firms?

**JAMES:** In general, because the work has historically been local and relationship-driven. A lot of administration firms started as a handful of specialists serving companies in their region, often connected to a local accounting or advisory practice. They do excellent work, but they may not have the scale to invest in technology, or a succession plan when the founders want to retire.

**NORA:** Which makes them natural targets.

**JAMES:** Right. For a small firm whose founders are thinking about retirement, selling to a larger specialist that will look after their clients is an attractive option. For the buyer, each acquisition brings a book of long-term clients and experienced staff. And if the buyer can move those clients onto a better platform and run them more efficiently, the economics improve. That's the classic roll-up logic, and it's our reading of what Blue Ridge is doing.

**NORA:** And the Crowe deal is a slightly different flavour.

**JAMES:** Yes. That was a big accounting firm deciding that plan administration wasn't core and selling it to a specialist. Same logic as Ambrose, really. Firms that do lots of things are handing this work to firms that do only this.

**NORA:** How does a company choose its administrator?

**JAMES:** Usually around the time the ESOP is formed, the owner, the finance director or their advisor picks the administrator. Advisors have a lot of influence, which is why the Ambrose relationship matters. And our view is that buyers value compliance depth and good communication, not just software. They want someone who knows the rules cold and won't get them in trouble.

**NORA:** Who are the competitors?

**JAMES:** There are a few types. Specialist ESOP administrators and advisors, like Menke Group and ESOP Partners. Large retirement recordkeepers, like Ascensus, which bought an ESOP specialist called SES Advisors in 2019, and Principal Financial Group, which a comparison site lists against Blue Ridge. Then adjacent firms, like the trustee and valuation firms GreatBanc Trust, Prairie Capital Advisors and Verit Advisors, which are more partners than rivals.

**NORA:** And the tech companies?

**JAMES:** That's the interesting one. Carta, the venture-backed equity management platform, is named as a key player in one of the market reports. And Pulley and EquityX are named as equity software challengers in another. We couldn't verify how far they go into ESOP administration specifically. But the threat, in our view, is that equity software companies who already track who owns what could bundle ESOP administration into their platforms.

**NORA:** Is that a realistic threat?

**JAMES:** It's a possibility worth watching rather than a present danger, in my view. ESOP administration is full of regulatory nuance, and a cap-table tool doesn't automatically handle it. But software companies are good at simplifying complex workflows, and if AI lowers the cost of handling that nuance, the barrier could drop.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about the challenges.

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, integration load. Every acquired firm comes with its own clients, its own data, its own systems and its own ways of working, across at least five states. Moving all of that onto one platform takes time, and while it's happening, teams may be running parallel systems.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, inconsistent public metrics. We've seen very different numbers for headcount, clients and plans. Some of that is growth, some is definitions. But from the outside, it makes the business harder to understand. For a firm on a continuation-fund clock, a clear, consistent set of metrics will matter when it tells its story to future buyers. That's our inference.

**NORA:** Third.

**JAMES:** Third, starting from zero on AI. The CTO arrived in August 2026. Building a technology function, choosing tools, hiring people and putting governance in place all take time. Meanwhile competitors, including big recordkeepers with large technology budgets, are moving too.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**JAMES:** Leadership change. The chief executive was Bill Yoerger in 2022 and 2023 and Mark Agustin by late 2025, and now there's a new technology leader. Leadership changes can bring fresh energy, but also some disruption. In a relationship business, continuity matters to clients and advisors.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fiduciary risk. This is a business where getting numbers wrong has legal consequences. Any automation, and especially AI, has to be introduced with strong controls. An AI error in a participant statement or a government filing could create real exposure under ERISA. That makes the company rightly cautious, but it can also make change slow.

**NORA:** What about the people in the acquired firms? That must be its own challenge.

**JAMES:** It's a big one, and it's often underestimated. When a small firm is bought, its staff may worry about their jobs, their clients and their way of working. They often have deep personal relationships with clients. If they leave, clients may follow. So integration isn't only about systems. It's about bringing people along, giving them better tools and showing that the new home is good for them and their clients.

**NORA:** And technology can be part of that pitch.

**JAMES:** It can. If the message to acquired staff is, here's a platform and tools that take away the worst of the retyping and chasing, so you can spend more time on the interesting work and with clients, that's attractive. If the message is, here's a new system you have to learn in the busiest season of the year, that's not. Timing and training matter a lot.

**NORA:** Is talent a challenge too?

**JAMES:** In general, yes. Experienced ESOP administrators are specialists, and the work is intricate. If the firm is growing through acquisitions, it needs enough skilled people to handle the volume. Automation is partly about freeing those experts from routine work so they can focus on the hard cases.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's move to opportunities. Where would you push?

**JAMES:** First and biggest, in my view, automated document intake and data extraction. All those inputs we talked about, census files, trustee reports, valuation reports, participant documents, could be read and structured by AI, with checks for inconsistencies. That cuts the hours analysts spend retyping and chasing, which is the main cost in the business.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Acquisition integration. Every time Blue Ridge buys a firm, it has to convert that firm's client data onto its platform. AI can help map fields, spot gaps and check that converted balances match the old system. If the company builds a standard, AI-assisted conversion playbook, each acquisition becomes cheaper and faster to integrate. With a deal pace of more than one a year, that adds up.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** A participant service assistant. With more than nine hundred thousand participants, by the company's count, a lot of questions repeat. A carefully designed assistant that explains a participant's own statement in plain language, answers common questions and passes anything complex to a person could cut call volumes and improve the experience. It ties naturally into the communications business.

**NORA:** That one sounds risky.

**JAMES:** It needs care. It must never give tax or legal advice, it must only use the participant's own data, and it must hand off to humans on anything sensitive, like a divorce order or a hardship question. But answering "what does vested mean" and "why did my balance change" well is valuable, and those are the questions people ask most.

**NORA:** And the communications team. Could AI help there too?

**JAMES:** I think so, and it's a nice fit. The communications team produces education and content for employee-owners. Generative AI could help draft explainers tailored to each client's plan, translate materials into other languages for diverse workforces, or turn a dense plan summary into a short, friendly guide. A human expert would review everything, but the production time could drop a lot.

**NORA:** Personalised, even?

**JAMES:** Potentially. Imagine a statement that comes with a short plain-language note: here's what changed in your account this year and why. That's the kind of thing that's hard to do by hand for nine hundred thousand people, and much more feasible with automation, as long as every number comes straight from the verified records rather than being generated.

**NORA:** That last point seems important.

**JAMES:** It's the key design rule. Let AI write the words around the numbers, never the numbers themselves. The numbers come from the administration system, which has been checked. The AI explains them.

**NORA:** What else?

**JAMES:** Fourth, the formation-to-administration channel. Building a strong flow from advisors who set up ESOPs, like Ambrose, into Blue Ridge's administration. That's a commercial opportunity rather than an AI one. Fifth, pricing. Pricing per participant and bundling communications, which is already a dedicated service line, could grow revenue per client. And sixth, continuing the roll-up, buying smaller administrators in new states, using that standard integration playbook.

**NORA:** How would you sequence them?

**JAMES:** Integration and data first, because everything else depends on clean data on one platform. Document intake second, because it's the biggest hours saver. Participant assistant third, because it's customer-facing and needs the most governance. And the commercial moves alongside.

### The best AI bet and the first 100 days

**NORA:** One AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** Automated document intake and data extraction for plan administration, combined with conversion of acquired clients' data. They're really the same capability: reading messy documents and files, turning them into clean, structured data, and flagging anything that doesn't add up. Use it for the annual cycle, and use it for every acquisition.

**NORA:** Why that over the participant assistant?

**JAMES:** Three reasons. It's internal, so the risk is lower: humans review everything before it reaches a client. It saves the most hours, because data handling is where analysts spend so much time. And it builds the clean data foundation that every other AI idea, including the assistant, will need.

**NORA:** Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: measure the current state. How many hours per plan go on data collection and checking? How many questions go back to clients? How long does a typical acquisition conversion take? Pick a set of plans, maybe fifty, across different sizes and origins, including some from recent acquisitions. And write down the controls: what must a human always review.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Pilot extraction on those plans. Use AI to read census files and the main documents, like valuation and trustee reports, and produce structured data plus a list of issues: missing hours, duplicate people, termination dates that don't line up. Analysts review every output, compare it with their own work, and record where the AI was right, wrong or uncertain. That's shadow mode, and in a fiduciary business it's essential.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Measure and decide. Hours saved per plan, error rates, how many issues were caught earlier. If it works, roll it into the annual cycle for more plans and into the next acquisition conversion. At day one hundred, the CTO should be able to say: here's what it saves, here's the control framework, and here's the rollout plan. I'd also want a written AI policy by then, covering data handling and human review.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** The new CTO is the obvious owner on the technology side. But I'd pair them with a senior operations leader, someone who has run plan administration for years, as co-owner. In this business, the people who know the rules have to trust the tool, or it won't be used.

### Converting an acquisition, a hypothetical

**NORA:** Let's do a second walk-through, on an acquisition. Invent one.

**JAMES:** Sure. Imagine, hypothetically, Blue Ridge buys a small administration firm in Ohio. We'll call it Buckeye Plan Services. It has twenty staff and about three hundred plans. It runs on an older software system and a lot of spreadsheets. The goal is to move all three hundred plans onto Blue Ridge's platform before the next plan year.

**NORA:** And without AI?

**JAMES:** In a typical conversion, a team exports data from the old system, maps each field to the new one, loads it, and then checks balances plan by plan. Spreadsheets have to be read and interpreted. Plan documents, which set the rules for each plan, have to be reviewed so that the new system calculates correctly. For a while, both systems run side by side to make sure the numbers match. That parallel running can last months.

**NORA:** Where does it hurt?

**JAMES:** Everywhere small differences hide. One plan uses an unusual vesting schedule. Another had a special allocation formula. A third has participant records with gaps. Each one takes an experienced person time to find and fix. And while the team does that, clients may notice delays or errors, which is the worst outcome in the first year after an acquisition.

**NORA:** And with AI help?

**JAMES:** The AI reads each plan document and extracts the key rules, vesting schedule, allocation method, eligibility, and highlights anything unusual. It maps fields from the old system to the new one and flags those it isn't sure about. It reads the spreadsheets and turns them into structured data. And after loading, it compares balances between the old and new systems and lists every mismatch with a likely cause.

**NORA:** So the experts get a list of problems, not a pile of documents.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Instead of searching for the needles, they get handed them. Then they make the judgment calls. And every conversion teaches the system more, so the next one goes faster. If Blue Ridge keeps buying firms at the pace it has, that learning loop is valuable. Fewer months of parallel running means lower cost and less risk with clients.

**NORA:** And what does Buckeye's finance director client notice?

**JAMES:** Ideally, very little. His plan moves to a new home, his first statement on the new platform is correct, and maybe it comes with clearer communications. In integration, the goal is that clients feel the benefits and none of the pain.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Blue Ridge?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, in a roll-up, integration is the strategy. Buying firms is the easy part. The value comes when they're on one platform, with one set of processes and clean data. Companies that treat integration as an afterthought end up with a collection of businesses rather than one.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, hiring a technology leader is a beginning, not an achievement. A first CTO with an AI mandate is a great signal. But what matters is the first year: what gets measured, what gets shipped, and what controls are in place. I'd encourage any company making that kind of hire to agree, early, on two or three concrete outcomes.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, in regulated work, start AI behind the scenes. Document extraction with human review is a much safer first step than a customer-facing chatbot. It builds skill, data and trust. Customer-facing AI can come later, once the foundations and controls are proven.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, get your numbers straight. We saw very different figures for this company's size depending on the source and the year. For any business that might be sold, raise money or compete on reputation, having clear, consistent, well-defined metrics is part of telling a credible story.

**NORA:** I'd add one: explaining things well is a product.

**JAMES:** Absolutely. Blue Ridge's communications business is a reminder that understanding is part of value. An employee who understands their ESOP values it more. That's true of lots of products: if customers don't understand what they have, they won't appreciate it.

**NORA:** And for the private equity world?

**JAMES:** Continuation funds are an interesting signal. When a firm keeps a company rather than selling it, it's saying there's more value to create. For the company, that's both a vote of confidence and a challenge. The extra time has to be used well, and in a firm like this, technology and integration are probably where much of that value lies.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks. Give me the list.

**JAMES:** One, AI-driven price compression. If AI automates much of the routine administration, clients may expect lower fees, and per-plan pricing comes under pressure. Two, platform bundling, if equity software companies like Carta add ESOP administration. Three, formation-cycle exposure: if fewer ESOPs are formed, the referral flow from advisors slows. Four, data and fiduciary risk: any error, especially one introduced by automation, has legal consequences. And five, integration execution across many acquisitions at once.

**NORA:** Let's dig into price compression. If AI makes the work cheaper, isn't that just good for Blue Ridge's margins?

**JAMES:** In the short term, probably. If Blue Ridge automates faster than clients expect lower prices, it keeps the savings. But over time, in general, when a type of work gets cheaper across a whole industry, competitors use that to win clients on price, and fees drift down. The firms that do well are the ones that use the savings to offer more, better communications, faster service, planning help, rather than simply charging less for the same thing.

**NORA:** So the defence is adding value, not just cutting cost.

**JAMES:** That's my view. And Blue Ridge has a head start there, because it already sells communications and has relationships with formation advisors. If the annual administration becomes partly automated, the expert time it frees up can go into advice-like services that clients value more, within the limits of what an administrator should do.

**NORA:** Which also makes clients even less likely to switch.

**JAMES:** Exactly. A client who just gets statements might shop around. A client whose administrator helps them plan for buy-backs, explains ownership to their staff and handles acquisitions smoothly is much less likely to.

**NORA:** Is there a risk the AI story stalls?

**JAMES:** Yes. A new CTO joining a services firm can find that the most urgent work is fixing basic systems, and AI gets delayed. That's not necessarily bad, because the basics matter, but it means the visible AI story might take longer than the headline suggested.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** The continuation fund clock. The investors who came in in late 2024 will want to see growth, successful integration and, ideally, a more efficient, technology-enabled business by the time of any eventual sale. Measurable results from the technology work would help that story a lot.

**NORA:** So what should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any announcement of technology or AI capabilities from the new CTO, ideally with results. Second, whether the acquisition pace continues, and whether a primary source confirms the reported May 2026 deal in New York. Third, whether Blue Ridge publishes consistent figures on plans, participants and staff. And fourth, any move by equity software companies into ESOP administration.

**NORA:** And for employee-owners listening?

**JAMES:** Read your statement, and if you don't understand it, ask. That's not advice about your plan, just encouragement. It's your ownership.
