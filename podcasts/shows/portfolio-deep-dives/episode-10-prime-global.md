### What medical communications is

**NORA:** I suspect a lot of listeners have never heard of medical communications as an industry. Give us the primer.

**JAMES:** Sure, as general background. Pharmaceutical companies have to communicate about their medicines in tightly controlled ways. There's a broad split, in general terms, between medical affairs, which is about sharing scientific information in a balanced, non-promotional way, and commercial, which is about promotion within strict rules. Medical communications agencies work on both sides, but the science is at the core.

**NORA:** What does the work look like?

**JAMES:** A lot of it is publications. When a trial finishes, the results need to be written up for a peer-reviewed journal, and presented as abstracts and posters at medical congresses. Agencies help plan which publications to produce, when and where, and medical writers help the researchers draft them, following ethical guidelines on authorship and transparency.

**NORA:** And beyond publications?

**JAMES:** Slide kits for medical teams to present data. Training for pharmaceutical company staff who talk to doctors. Educational programmes. Advisory boards, where companies hear from experts. Materials for patients. And increasingly, digital content, websites, apps, interactive tools.

**NORA:** Why do pharma companies hire agencies for this? Couldn't they write it themselves?

**JAMES:** Some do some of it. But in general, there are a few reasons to outsource. Workload is lumpy: a big trial readout or a launch creates a surge of work, and it's cheaper to bring in an agency than to staff for the peak. Expertise is specialised: writing a publication to journal standards, or building a health economic model, takes skills a company may not need full-time. And agencies see many companies and many therapy areas, so they bring experience of what works.

**NORA:** And there are ethical rules around all this, I assume.

**JAMES:** Yes, and they matter. Speaking generally, publication guidelines in the industry set out that authors must be the people who genuinely contributed to the research and the paper, and that professional writing support should be disclosed. The aim is transparency: readers should know who did what and who paid for it. Good agencies take those rules very seriously, because their reputation, and their clients', depends on it.

**NORA:** So the writer is a support act, not the author.

**JAMES:** In publications, typically, yes. The writer helps the authors communicate clearly and accurately. That's a useful frame for thinking about AI too. If an AI helps the writer, that's another layer of support, and the same questions about disclosure and responsibility apply.

**NORA:** You mentioned MLR earlier. What is it?

**JAMES:** Medical, legal and regulatory review. In general, before any material goes out, it's reviewed by the pharma company's medical, legal and regulatory teams to make sure it's accurate, balanced, properly referenced and compliant with the rules. That review is thorough and often slow. Every claim must be supported by a reference. That's a crucial fact for the AI story, because it means speed in drafting doesn't automatically mean speed to market.

**NORA:** And market access?

**JAMES:** That's about getting a medicine paid for. A regulator might approve a medicine as safe and effective, but then a health system or an insurer decides whether to cover it and at what price. They want evidence of value: how much the disease costs, how the medicine changes outcomes, how it compares with alternatives. That's where health economics and outcomes research, shortened to HEOR, comes in, along with real-world evidence, meaning data from actual clinical practice rather than trials.

**NORA:** So Prime covers the journey from the lab results all the way to the insurer.

**JAMES:** That's its pitch. Its own five service lines are strategic consultancy and lifecycle management; medical strategy, publications and scientific communications; HEOR, real-world evidence and market access; commercial launch and promotional education; and patient engagement and insights.

### A publication, a hypothetical

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through one piece of work, invented.

**JAMES:** Sure. A made-up mid-sized biotech, let's call it Northbrook Therapeutics, has just finished a phase three trial of a new medicine for a chronic lung condition. It hires an agency like Prime to support the primary publication in a medical journal, plus an abstract and poster for a major respiratory congress in six months.

**NORA:** Who's involved?

**JAMES:** On the agency side, a medical writer with a doctorate in respiratory science, an editor, a project manager and an account lead. On the client side, the medical affairs team, the clinical team who ran the trial, and of course the academic investigators who will be the authors. Under good publication practice, the authors are the scientists who did the work; the writer supports them.

**NORA:** And the work?

**JAMES:** The writer starts with the clinical study report, which is a very long document, plus the statistical tables. She reads it all, and builds an outline with the authors: the key findings, the story, the structure. Then she drafts the manuscript: introduction, methods, results, discussion. Every number must match the source tables. Every statement in the discussion must be supported by references.

**NORA:** How long does that take?

**JAMES:** In general, weeks of skilled time, and then rounds of review. Authors comment. The client's medical team comments. MLR reviews. The writer revises. Several cycles. Then submission to the journal, peer review, more revisions. And in parallel, the abstract and poster for the congress, which need the same accuracy in fewer words.

**NORA:** Where does the time go?

**JAMES:** In my view, a lot of it goes on tasks that are skilled but not the highest-value part of the job. Reading and summarising long documents. Checking that every number matches. Formatting references. Turning the full paper into a shorter abstract and then into a poster. Reconciling comments from five reviewers who disagree. The truly expert work, framing the story honestly and getting the science right, is a smaller share of the hours than you might think.

**NORA:** So that's where AI could help.

**JAMES:** That's the hypothesis. Not to replace the writer's judgment, but to take some of the reading, summarising, checking and reformatting off her plate. And we'll come back to how that might look.

**NORA:** And what happens to the doctor at the end of the chain?

**JAMES:** A respiratory specialist reads the paper in the journal, sees the poster at the congress, and gets a clear, accurate picture of what the trial showed, benefits and risks. That's the point of the whole exercise. Done well, it helps doctors make better decisions for their patients.

**JAMES:** That's the other competitor, in a sense. If generative AI makes drafting much faster, some pharma companies may decide to do more of the work in-house with smaller teams and AI tools. That's an inference, but it's a real risk for any agency whose value is mainly hours of writing.

In October 2025, the creation of a global executive vice president role for medical communications, AI and digital healthcare, with James Karcher in post, and a remit that covers AI-enabled communications and advanced analytics. Prime Solutions Lab, which says it uses AI to distil complex data and cites predictive modelling. The chief scientific officer, Valerie Moss, co-leading a session on AI in medical communications at an industry academy run by ISMPP, the International Society for Medical Publication Professionals, though the year isn't stated. An undated social media post about the chief technology officer leading an internal session on small-scale AI applications. And the homepage claims award-winning creative, digital and AI solutions.

**NORA:** And what didn't you find?

**JAMES:** No named AI product or tool. No description of AI inside the core writing workflow. No AI partnerships. No published AI governance policy or security certifications. So the picture is a company with AI leadership in place, an internal hub, and clear interest, but no public evidence yet of AI embedded in how its core deliverables are produced.

**NORA:** How would you characterise that?

**JAMES:** In my view, Prime is ahead of many services firms on leadership and intent. Putting AI in a senior executive's title is a real commitment. It's at an earlier stage on process: making AI a normal part of how a publication or slide kit gets made, with measured results.

**NORA:** Why might it be cautious?

**JAMES:** Good reasons. First, accuracy. In medical communications, a hallucinated number or a made-up reference is not a typo, it's a serious problem. Second, confidentiality. Clinical trial data before publication is highly sensitive, and clients will want to know exactly where it goes. Third, client rules. Many pharma companies have their own policies on whether and how agencies can use AI on their work. And fourth, industry guidance on AI in publications is still evolving, including around disclosure of AI use.

**NORA:** Let's dig into the confidentiality point. What would a client want to know?

**JAMES:** Speaking generally, a pharma client sharing unpublished trial data with an agency would want to know a few things before allowing AI anywhere near it. Which AI tools are used, and are they enterprise versions with contractual protections? Where is the data processed and stored? Is any of it used to train models that others benefit from? Who at the agency can see it? And how is it deleted when the project ends? Those are the same questions they'd ask about any system, but AI makes them more pressing.

**NORA:** And disclosure? Do journals need to know if AI helped?

**JAMES:** That's an evolving area. In general, many journals and industry bodies have been developing guidance on AI use in writing, and a common theme is that humans remain responsible for the content and that AI use should be disclosed where relevant. I won't quote specific rules, because they vary and change. But an agency should have a clear position that it can explain to clients and authors.

**NORA:** It's interesting that Prime's chief scientific officer has been teaching on exactly this topic.

**JAMES:** It is. Co-leading a session on AI in medical communications at a professional society suggests Prime wants to be part of setting good practice, not just following it. That's a credible position for a science-led agency, and it could be a selling point with cautious clients.

**NORA:** So caution makes sense.

**JAMES:** It does. But caution can become delay. The risk is that clients start expecting AI-driven efficiency, and an agency that hasn't built the capability has to either cut prices without the savings, or lose work.

**NORA:** What about the CTO's internal session on small-scale AI applications?

**JAMES:** I find that encouraging, actually. Small-scale applications, used internally, is exactly where to start. Summarising documents, checking references, drafting internal notes. Low risk, quick learning. The question is whether that turns into a systematic programme with measured outcomes.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about challenges.

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, the people-heavy model. Prime's strength is its PhD-level staff, but they're also its main cost. If revenue is tied to hours and AI reduces hours, the business model itself has to adapt.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, regulatory review slows down AI's benefit. Even if AI halves drafting time, MLR review still takes as long as it takes. So the end-to-end speed-up may be smaller than the drafting speed-up, at least until review processes adapt too. Meanwhile, faster-moving competitors could gain.

**NORA:** Third.

**JAMES:** Third, scale relative to the big groups. Larger agency networks can invest more in AI. Prime has to choose carefully, focusing on use cases that matter most to its clients.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**JAMES:** Visibility. Little public data on scale, no named AI product, and thin news flow over the past two years. For a company in a mature private equity hold, a clearer public story would help, in my view. Not every private company needs to publish numbers, but a visible AI offer would make the positioning more credible.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Integration and cross-selling. The acquisitions brought in health economics, digital and US payer expertise. The value comes when those teams work together on the same clients. That's harder than it sounds in agencies, where teams often have their own cultures and client relationships.

**NORA:** What about the writers themselves? How do they feel about AI?

**JAMES:** We don't have any public information on Prime's staff views, so this is general. In many expert professions, people have mixed feelings. Some are excited to offload the tedious parts of the job. Others worry about their craft being devalued, or about their jobs. And some are simply sceptical, because they've seen AI make confident mistakes in their field.

**NORA:** And that matters for adoption.

**JAMES:** Hugely. If the writers don't trust the tools, they'll either avoid them or spend so long double-checking that there's no saving. The best approach, in my view, is to involve senior writers in designing how AI is used, let them set the quality bar, and be honest about how the role will evolve. People adopt tools they helped shape.

**NORA:** Is there a career question too, for junior writers?

**JAMES:** There is, and it's one many professions face. Junior writers often learn by doing the routine work: summarising documents, building first drafts. If AI does that, how do they learn? Agencies will need to design training so that juniors still develop judgment, perhaps by reviewing and correcting AI drafts under supervision. It's a solvable problem, but it needs deliberate attention.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** The exit question. After five and a half years, a future buyer will ask what AI does to this business. If the answer is a threat with no response, that weighs on value. If the answer is a measured productivity gain plus new AI-enabled services, that supports it.

**JAMES:** First, generative AI first drafts for publications, abstracts and slide kits, with MLR tracking built in. The lever is writer hours per deliverable. We don't have Prime's numbers, so we can't put a figure on it, but in a business where expert time is the main cost, even modest time savings matter.

A client using Prime for European health economics may also need US payer communications. That's not an AI opportunity directly, but AI-assisted evidence work can make the combined offer faster.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Digital tools from earthware inside the omnichannel programmes that James Karcher and David Hogben lead. For example, interactive tools for doctors that explain data, built once and used across many channels.

**JAMES:** Expanding US medical writing from the Aventine base. The US is the biggest market, and Prime already has people there. And acquisitions: buying a firm with AI-enabled evidence analytics would repeat the 2022 to 2023 playbook, but with an AI focus.

**NORA:** What about the patient side? One of the five service lines is patient engagement.

**JAMES:** I think that's an underappreciated area. In general, there's growing demand for plain-language summaries of research, so that patients and the public can understand what a trial found. **NORA:** And insights from patients?

**NORA:** Any AI opportunity on the market access side specifically?

**JAMES:** I think so. Health economics involves reviewing large amounts of published literature, building models and writing value dossiers. AI can help screen and summarise literature much faster, with experts checking. That's an area where speed and thoroughness both matter, and where clients may pay for being first.

### The best AI bet and the first 100 days

**NORA:** One AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** Generative AI-assisted first drafts of publications and slide kits, with review tracked through MLR. It's the core billable work. The expensive resource is PhD-level writer time. And it's where clients will first expect AI to make a difference.

**NORA:** Why not lead with Prime Solutions Lab's predictive modelling?

**JAMES:** Because that's newer and harder to measure. Drafting is the volume business. If Prime can show that its writers produce high-quality first drafts faster, with fewer review cycles and no loss of accuracy, that's a story every client understands. And it frees time for the higher-value strategic work.

**NORA:** Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: set the rules and the baseline. Agree with a few willing clients that AI can be used on their work, under clear terms. Confirm the tools and data handling, so trial data stays secure and isn't used to train outside models. Measure the baseline: hours per deliverable type, number of review cycles, number of errors found in review.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Pilot. Pick a few deliverable types, say abstracts, posters and slide kits, which are shorter and repeatable. Writers use AI to produce a first draft from the source documents, with every number and claim linked to its source. Then the writer edits and checks. Track hours, accuracy and how reviewers respond. Everything goes through normal review; nothing is published that a human expert hasn't verified.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Measure and decide. Did hours per deliverable fall? Did review cycles change? Were there any accuracy problems, and what caused them? If the results are good, extend to full manuscripts and more clients, and decide how to price, ideally moving some work to fixed fees. And publish an AI policy that clients can read, covering data, disclosure and human review.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** James Karcher's role seems designed for it. But I'd pair him with the chief scientific officer, Valerie Moss, because the writers need to trust that scientific standards come first. And with the CTO for the tools and security side. Clear single accountability, with science and technology both at the table.

**NORA:** How do you measure quality, not just speed?

**JAMES:** Really important, because speed without quality is worthless here. I'd track the number and type of corrections reviewers make on AI-assisted drafts versus traditional ones. Factual errors, especially numbers and references, should be at or below the traditional baseline, and that's non-negotiable. Then softer measures: did authors and clients rate the draft as clear and well-structured? And did the number of review rounds go down?

**NORA:** And if quality slips?

**JAMES:** Stop, find out why, and fix it before scaling. One serious error in front of a client can undo months of trust. That's why I'd start with a few willing clients and shorter deliverables, where problems are easy to catch.

**NORA:** What's the key design rule?

**JAMES:** Traceability. Every number and every claim in an AI-assisted draft should be linked to its source, so the writer and reviewers can check it in seconds. An AI that writes beautifully but can't show its sources is useless in this field, or worse.

### A slide kit with AI, a hypothetical

**NORA:** Let's do a second walk-through. Same made-up biotech?

**JAMES:** Same Northbrook Therapeutics. The paper has been accepted, and now the medical affairs team needs a slide kit, a set of forty slides its medical science liaisons can use to present the trial results to specialists in balanced, non-promotional discussions.

**NORA:** And the old way?

**JAMES:** A writer goes back through the paper, the clinical study report and the statistical tables. She decides which results go on which slide, designs the charts with a designer, writes speaker notes, and references every statement. Then it goes through review, and comments come back: this chart is misleading, this claim needs a reference, this slide should show the safety data more prominently. Revisions, another round. It's skilled, careful work, and it takes time.

**NORA:** And with AI assistance?

**JAMES:** The writer loads the accepted paper and the approved tables into a secure tool. She asks for a proposed outline of forty slides following the paper's structure. The AI suggests one, she adjusts it. Then for each slide, the AI drafts the headline and bullet text, proposes which table or figure to use, and drafts speaker notes. Crucially, every statement is linked to the exact place in the source document.

**NORA:** And then she checks it.

**JAMES:** She checks every slide. Some are good; some need rewording because the AI emphasised the wrong thing; one has a number from the wrong subgroup, which the source link makes easy to spot and fix. She spends her time on judgment, balance and clarity, rather than copying numbers. Then a designer builds the final charts, and it goes to review.

**NORA:** Does review get faster?

**JAMES:** Possibly, if the reviewers can see the source links too. Instead of hunting for the reference behind each claim, they click and check. That's speculative, and it depends on the client's review systems. But it's where the bigger speed-up might eventually come from, not just the drafting.

**NORA:** And what does Northbrook notice?

**JAMES:** Ideally, the slide kit arrives sooner, with fewer errors in the first review round, and the same or better quality. And if Prime has moved this kind of work to a fixed fee, Northbrook gets price certainty while Prime keeps the efficiency gain. Both sides win, and the doctors at the end of the chain get accurate information sooner.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Prime?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, put AI in someone's job title. Prime did that in October 2025. When AI is everyone's side project, it's no one's responsibility. A named leader with a clear remit is a strong start.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, in regulated work, traceability is everything. Whether it's medicine, law or finance, AI is only useful if every output can be checked against its source quickly. Design for verification, not just generation.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, rethink pricing before your customers do it for you. If you sell hours and AI reduces hours, you need a plan. Fixed-fee products, outcome-based pricing, new services. Firms that move first keep the benefit; firms that wait end up giving it away in discounts.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, acquisitions are a beginning. Prime bought health economics, digital and US payer capability. The payoff comes from combining them into offers that no single piece could deliver alone. AI can be the glue, for example in evidence work that spans HEOR and communications.

**NORA:** I'd add one: experts are the best AI users, not the victims.

**JAMES:** Great point. A PhD-level medical writer using AI well is far more valuable than either the writer alone or the AI alone. The writer knows what's right; the AI does the heavy lifting. Businesses that frame AI as a tool for their experts, rather than a replacement, tend to get better adoption and better results.

**NORA:** Is there a lesson about building an in-house hub like Prime Solutions Lab?

**JAMES:** I think so. Mixing creatives, technologists, strategists and scientists in one team is a smart way to make sure AI work is grounded in real client problems, rather than technology for its own sake. The risk with hubs, in general, is that they become a showcase disconnected from day-to-day delivery. The test is whether the hub's work ends up inside ordinary projects, used by ordinary teams.

**NORA:** So the hub has to be a bridge, not an island.

**JAMES:** Exactly. A good sign would be a named tool or method that started in the lab and is now used routinely across the business. A less good sign would be a lab that produces impressive demonstrations but no change in how the core work gets done. From the outside, we can't yet tell which way it's going, which is why it's on our watch list.

**NORA:** And for the private equity world?

**JAMES:** In a services business late in a hold, a buyer will want to see that AI has been absorbed as a productivity gain, not left as an unanswered threat. Measured results, even modest ones, tell a much better story than a strategy slide.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks. Give me the list.

**JAMES:** One, AI disruption to the revenue model. If drafting hours fall and pricing stays per hour, revenue per deliverable falls, and some clients may bring work in-house. Two, regulatory review slowing Prime's own AI rollout while rivals move faster. Three, larger agency groups with bigger AI budgets. Four, an AI error in a medical document, which could damage trust with a client very quickly. And five, the exit timing, with the AI story not yet proven publicly.

**NORA:** Tell me more about the insourcing risk.

**JAMES:** It's our inference, but here's the logic. Today a pharma company might outsource a slide kit because its own small team doesn't have time. If AI tools make that team three times more productive, it might keep more work in-house. Agencies would then be left with the most complex, strategic and surge work. That's not necessarily bad, because that work is often higher value, but the volume of routine work could shrink.

**NORA:** So the agency needs to move up the value chain.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Strategy, evidence generation, health economics, digital experiences, and the scientific judgment that clients can't easily replicate. That's where Prime's acquisitions point, and where AI can make its experts even more valuable rather than less.

**NORA:** Is there a reputational risk for the industry too?

**JAMES:** Yes. If AI-generated medical content becomes associated with errors, or with undisclosed use, regulators and journals may tighten rules, and clients may become more cautious. Agencies that are transparent and careful will be better placed if that happens.

**NORA:** So what should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, whether Prime names an AI tool or service, especially coming out of Prime Solutions Lab. Second, whether it announces fixed-fee or outcome-based services that suggest a shift away from hours. Third, any new acquisition, especially one with AI or analytics capability. And fourth, whether it publishes an AI policy for clients, covering data handling and human review.

**NORA:** And if none of those happen in the next year?

**JAMES:** Then Prime may still be doing good work quietly, which is very common for agencies, because so much of their work is confidential. But for a company that has put AI in a senior title and launched a hub, I'd expect some public evidence of results within a year or so. A case study with a client's permission, a named service, or even a talk at an industry event sharing what the company has learned would all count.

**NORA:** And on the industry side?

**JAMES:** How publication guidelines and pharma companies' own policies on AI evolve. That will set the pace for everyone, Prime included.
