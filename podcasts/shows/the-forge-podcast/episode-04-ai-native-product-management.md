### SEGMENT 1: The Fundamental Shift -- From Feature Specification to System Design (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** So let's start at the highest level. You said the loop itself is changing. Paint me a picture of what the old loop looks like versus what the new loop looks like, in terms of how a PM actually spends their time and what they're accountable for.

**KEVIN:** Sure. In the traditional model, I think of the PM as sitting in the center of a translation chain. On one side you have the market -- customers, competitors, business stakeholders -- generating signal about what needs to exist. On the other side you have the engineering team, which has the capacity to build things but needs clear direction about what to build. The PM's job is translation. You take messy, ambiguous market signal and turn it into structured, unambiguous specifications. User stories, acceptance criteria, wireframes, prioritized backlogs. The better you are at that translation, the better PM you are. And the key assumption underlying all of it is that every behavior your product exhibits was explicitly specified by a human, reviewed by a human, and implemented by a human. The product does exactly and only what someone decided it should do.

**KEVIN:** In the AI-native model, that assumption breaks. Your product now contains components that behave probabilistically. An AI agent that classifies documents, or generates recommendations, or takes actions on behalf of the user -- that agent will behave differently on different inputs. It might be right 95 percent of the time and wrong 5 percent of the time, and which 5 percent it gets wrong is not fully predictable in advance. So the PM can't just specify "the system should classify this document as an invoice." They have to think about what happens when the classification is wrong. They have to think about how confident the system needs to be before it acts autonomously versus asking for human confirmation. They have to design the feedback mechanism that lets the system improve over time. The PM's job shifts from specifying features to designing systems. And those are genuinely different disciplines.

**ALEX:** Give me a concrete example. Something from your actual work at GWI that illustrates what that shift feels like in practice.

**KEVIN:** Here's one that's very tangible. We have a feature where users create audience segments -- essentially, they define a group of people based on demographic and behavioral criteria, and then they analyze that group. In the traditional model, a PM would specify the audience builder interface: the filters, the operators, the way results are displayed. Very deterministic. You click "age 25-34" and "interested in gaming" and the system returns that exact segment. Now we're building an AI-native version where users can describe their audience in natural language. They type "young gamers in the UK who are likely to buy premium subscriptions" and the AI interprets that, selects the right filters and criteria, builds the segment, and presents it.

**KEVIN:** The PM's job on that feature is radically different. Instead of specifying which filters appear in which order, the PM has to define what "correct interpretation" means across thousands of possible natural language inputs. They have to decide what the system does when the intent is ambiguous -- does it ask a clarifying question, does it make a best guess and show its reasoning, does it generate multiple interpretations and let the user choose? They have to set confidence thresholds: at what confidence level does the system just build the segment, and at what level does it flag uncertainty? They have to design the feedback loop: when a user edits the AI-generated segment, how does that correction flow back to improve future interpretations? None of that looks like writing user stories. It looks like designing a system with policies, boundaries, and learning mechanisms.

**ALEX:** So the PM becomes more like a policy designer than a feature specifier.

**KEVIN:** That's exactly right, and I think "policy designer" is actually the best two-word description of where the PM role is heading. You're defining the rules and boundaries within which an intelligent system operates, rather than defining every specific behavior the system exhibits. It's the difference between writing a script for an actor versus setting the objectives and constraints for an autonomous agent. Both require deep product thinking, but the second one requires you to think in terms of distributions, edge cases, escalation paths, and learning curves rather than linear user flows.

### SEGMENT 2: The Five Activities of AI-Native PM Workflow (15 minutes)

**ALEX:** All right, so you've convinced me the shift is real. Now let's get into the framework. You've described five core activities that make up the AI-native PM workflow. Walk me through each one, because I think this is where listeners can start to immediately map this onto their own work.

**KEVIN:** The five activities are: outcome definition, autonomy boundary design, feedback loop architecture, confidence calibration, and exception flow design. I want to be clear that these aren't replacing the traditional PM activities. You still need to do discovery, you still need to understand your market, you still need to prioritize. But these five are the new layer that sits on top of that foundation, and they're what distinguish an AI-native PM from a traditional PM.

**KEVIN:** The first one, outcome definition, sounds simple but it's where most teams get it wrong. In traditional PM work, you define outcomes at the feature level: "users should be able to export reports in PDF format." In AI-native work, you have to define outcomes at the system behavior level, and you have to define them in a way that's measurable and evaluable. Instead of "the AI should classify documents correctly," you need something like "the document classification system should achieve 97 percent accuracy on invoice detection, with a false positive rate below 1 percent, processing documents within 3 seconds, and the accuracy should improve by at least 0.5 percentage points per quarter as the feedback loop ingests corrections." That's a fundamentally different kind of outcome definition. It's quantitative, it has tolerance bounds, and it includes a trajectory expectation. PMs who've spent their career writing qualitative user stories find this transition genuinely difficult.

**ALEX:** That level of precision is interesting because it almost reads like an SLA or a machine learning objective, not a product requirement. How do PMs who don't have a technical background get comfortable writing requirements like that?

**KEVIN:** That's one of the core skills gaps we identified early in our training program, and I'll talk more about that later. But the short answer is: they learn it the same way they learned to write user stories 15 years ago -- through practice, templates, and iteration. We give PMs a template for AI outcome definitions that includes accuracy targets, latency bounds, confidence thresholds, improvement trajectories, and failure mode tolerances. The first time they fill it out, it's rough. By the third time, they start to develop intuition for it. The key insight we hammer home is that if you can't define what "good" looks like in measurable terms, you cannot build an AI feature, because you'll have no way to evaluate whether the system is working. Evaluation is the foundation of everything in AI product development.

**KEVIN:** The second activity is autonomy boundary design, and this is where it gets really interesting. For every AI-powered capability in your product, you need to decide: what can the AI do on its own, what does it need human approval for, and what should it never do at all? I think of this as three zones. The green zone is full autonomy -- the AI acts without asking. The yellow zone is human-in-the-loop -- the AI recommends but a human confirms. The red zone is human-only -- the AI doesn't touch it. Designing these boundaries is a product decision, not an engineering decision. It requires deep understanding of your users, your domain, the consequences of errors, and the trust dynamics between your users and your product.

**ALEX:** Can you give me an example of how those zones play out in a real product?

**KEVIN:** Sure. Take an AI-powered expense management system. In the green zone, you might put automatic categorization of common expenses -- the AI sees a charge from Starbucks and categorizes it as "meals and entertainment" without asking anyone. That's low stakes, high confidence, easy to correct if wrong. In the yellow zone, you might put policy compliance checks -- the AI flags an expense that might violate company policy and presents it to an approver with its reasoning, but it doesn't auto-reject. The consequence of a false rejection is too high. In the red zone, you put things like final payment authorization -- the AI never initiates a payment without explicit human approval, no matter how confident it is. Getting these boundaries right is the PM's job, and getting them wrong has real consequences. Too much autonomy and users lose trust when errors happen. Too little autonomy and the product doesn't deliver enough value to justify the AI investment. The sweet spot is different for every product, every user segment, and often every specific workflow within a product.

**KEVIN:** The third activity is feedback loop architecture. This is about designing how the system learns and improves from actual usage. Every time a user interacts with an AI recommendation -- accepting it, modifying it, or rejecting it -- that's a signal. The PM needs to design how those signals are captured, processed, and fed back into the system. This isn't just a technical question about model retraining. It's a product design question. Do you tell users their corrections are improving the system? Do you show them how the system has changed based on their feedback? How quickly should corrections take effect -- immediately for that user, or after aggregation across many users? At GWI, we spent significant time designing these loops for our audience builder. When a researcher modifies an AI-generated segment, we capture not just what they changed but why -- was the interpretation wrong, was it right but incomplete, or was it technically correct but not what they intended? Each of those categories feeds back differently.

**ALEX:** So the feedback loop itself becomes a product surface, not just a backend pipeline.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And here's what I tell PMs: the feedback loop is your compounding advantage. A product with a well-designed feedback loop gets better every day. A product without one is static. Over 12 months, the product with the feedback loop will be dramatically better than the one without it, even if they started from the same baseline. Architecting that loop is PM work, not engineering work.

**KEVIN:** The fourth activity is confidence calibration. This is about understanding and tuning the relationship between the system's internal confidence scores and actual correctness. Most AI systems produce some form of confidence score -- a probability, a logit, a ranking. The PM needs to understand what those scores mean in practice and set thresholds that drive product behavior. If the system is 99 percent confident, it acts autonomously. If it's 80 percent confident, it shows a recommendation with its reasoning. If it's 50 percent confident, it asks the user to make the decision. The tricky part is that confidence scores are not always well-calibrated out of the box. A system might say it's 95 percent confident but actually be correct only 80 percent of the time. The PM needs to understand this gap and work with engineering to calibrate the thresholds based on empirical evaluation, not just model outputs.

**ALEX:** That feels like it requires a new kind of thinking for PMs. Probabilistic thinking rather than deterministic thinking.

**KEVIN:** It does, and I'd go further -- I'd say probabilistic thinking is the single most important new cognitive skill for AI-native PMs. Traditional software is deterministic: given the same input, you always get the same output. AI systems are probabilistic: given the same input, you might get different outputs, and any given output has some probability of being wrong. PMs who internalize this build better products because they design for the full distribution of outcomes, not just the happy path. They think about what happens in the 5 percent failure case, not just the 95 percent success case. They build products that are robust and trustworthy rather than products that work great in demos but fall apart in production.

**KEVIN:** The fifth activity is exception flow design, and honestly, this might be the most underappreciated of the five. Exception flows are what happens when the AI can't handle something -- when the input is outside its training distribution, when the confidence is too low, when the user explicitly disagrees with the AI's output. In traditional software, exceptions are error states. In AI-native products, exceptions are a normal and expected part of operation. Your system will be wrong some percentage of the time by design. The PM needs to design graceful, productive exception flows that maintain user trust, capture useful signal, and avoid blocking the user's workflow. A good exception flow doesn't just say "something went wrong." It shows the user what the AI attempted, explains why it's uncertain, offers alternatives, and makes it easy to manually complete the action. The exception flow is often where users form their strongest opinions about whether they trust the product.

**ALEX:** So if I'm a PM listening to this, I should be thinking about outcome definition, autonomy boundaries, feedback loops, confidence calibration, and exception flows as the five new core competencies layered on top of everything I already do.

**KEVIN:** Right. And I want to emphasize the "layered on top" part. You still need market understanding, you still need customer empathy, you still need business acumen. Those don't go away. But if you're building AI-native products and you're not doing these five things well, you're going to ship products that are fragile, that users don't trust, and that don't improve over time.

### SEGMENT 3: How PRDs Change and the Death of Deterministic Specs (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about the artifacts. The PRD has been the canonical PM deliverable for a long time. How does it change in this new world? Is it dead, or does it just look different?

**KEVIN:** It's not dead, but it transforms pretty dramatically. The traditional PRD is fundamentally a specification document. It says: here is exactly what the system should do, step by step, in every scenario. The user clicks this button, this screen appears, these fields are populated with these values. It's a blueprint for deterministic behavior. In an AI-native world, you can't write that kind of spec for the AI-powered components of your product because the whole point is that the system's behavior emerges from a model rather than being hardcoded. So the PRD evolves into what I call a product policy document.

**KEVIN:** A product policy document has several sections that don't exist in a traditional PRD. First, it has an intent section that describes what the feature is trying to accomplish in terms of user outcomes, not system behaviors. Not "when the user clicks analyze, show a chart" but "the analysis feature should surface the three most actionable insights from the user's data, prioritized by statistical significance and business relevance." Second, it has a constraints section that defines the boundaries of acceptable behavior. This is your red zone -- the things the AI must never do. In a financial product, that might include "the system must never present projected returns as guaranteed" or "the system must never make a trade without explicit user confirmation." Third, it has an evaluation criteria section that defines how you'll measure whether the feature is working. This includes quantitative thresholds for accuracy, latency, and confidence, but also qualitative criteria for things like explanation quality and user trust.

**ALEX:** What does this look like in practice? Can you walk me through a before-and-after for a specific feature?

**KEVIN:** Let me use a real example. We had a feature at GWI where users could get AI-generated summaries of audience data. The traditional PRD approach would say something like: "The system should generate a summary paragraph for each audience segment. The summary should include the segment size, the top three demographic attributes, and the top three behavioral attributes. The summary should be no more than 150 words." That's a perfectly fine deterministic spec. But it completely misses the point of using AI, because it constrains the output so tightly that you might as well use a template.

**KEVIN:** The product policy document version says something different. The intent section says: "Audience summaries should give researchers an immediate, actionable understanding of what makes a segment distinctive, enabling them to make a go or no-go decision about deeper analysis within 30 seconds of reading." The constraints section says: "Summaries must be grounded in the actual data -- no hallucinated statistics. Summaries must not make causal claims. Summaries must flag when a segment is too small for statistical reliability." The evaluation section says: "Researchers should rate summaries as 'useful for decision-making' at least 85 percent of the time. Summaries should highlight at least one insight that the researcher had not already identified from the raw data at least 60 percent of the time. Factual accuracy should be 99 percent or higher as validated by automated checks against source data."

**KEVIN:** See the difference? The second version gives the AI room to be creative and to surface genuinely useful insights, while establishing clear boundaries and measurable success criteria. It's harder to write than a traditional PRD because it requires the PM to think deeply about what "good" actually means, rather than just describing the mechanics of the interface.

**ALEX:** That evaluation section is really interesting. It reads almost like a test specification for the AI itself. Is the PM now responsible for what the ML world calls "evals"?

**KEVIN:** Yes, and this is one of the biggest skill shifts. In traditional product management, QA is a downstream activity -- you build the thing, then QA tests it against the spec. In AI-native product management, evaluation design is an upstream activity -- you define your evals before you build anything, because the evals are what tell you whether your AI system is actually working. This is the concept of eval-driven development, which is the AI-native analog to test-driven development. The PM doesn't write the eval code, but they absolutely own the eval criteria. What are the test cases? What are the edge cases? What does a good output look like versus a bad output? What are the failure modes that matter most? If a PM can't articulate those things, the engineering team is building blind.

**KEVIN:** I'll give you a concrete example of why this matters. We had one squad that built an AI feature without clear eval criteria from the PM. The engineers optimized for the metrics they could measure -- response time and basic format compliance. The feature was fast and well-formatted but the insights it generated were superficial and obvious. Users hated it. If the PM had defined upfront that "the system should surface at least one non-obvious insight per summary," the engineering team would have optimized for depth and novelty rather than speed and format. The eval criteria shape the system's behavior just as much as the model architecture does.

**ALEX:** So the PM is now effectively co-designing the objective function, even if they're not writing the loss function in code.

**KEVIN:** That's a great way to put it. The PM defines what the system should optimize for in human terms. The engineer translates that into technical optimization objectives. When those are aligned, you get products that users love. When they're misaligned -- when the PM says "insightful" and the engineer optimizes for "syntactically correct" -- you get technically functional products that nobody wants to use.

### SEGMENT 4: New PM Skills -- Probabilistic Thinking, Eval Design, and Policy Writing (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** We've been circling around the skills question, so let's tackle it head on. If I'm a VP of Product and I need my PM team to be effective in this new world, what are the specific skills I should be investing in? You've mentioned probabilistic thinking and eval design. What else?

**KEVIN:** I think there are three core new skill areas: probabilistic thinking, evaluation design, and policy writing. Let me take each one.

**KEVIN:** Probabilistic thinking is about shifting your mental model from "the system does X" to "the system does X approximately Y percent of the time, and here's what happens the other Z percent of the time." This isn't natural for most PMs because the entire training and culture of product management has been deterministic. You write a spec, engineering builds to the spec, QA verifies the spec. Done. But when you're working with AI systems, you need to be comfortable with statements like "this feature will be correct 93 percent of the time" and then immediately ask the right follow-up questions: Is 93 percent good enough for this use case? What does the 7 percent error case look like? Is the 7 percent randomly distributed or concentrated in specific scenarios? What's the cost of a false positive versus a false negative? Can we get to 97 percent with more training data or do we need an architectural change?

**KEVIN:** The PMs I've seen make this transition successfully are the ones who develop an intuition for trade-offs in probabilistic systems. They understand that pushing accuracy from 90 to 95 percent might be straightforward, but pushing from 95 to 99 percent might require 10x the effort and 5x the cost. They understand that a system that's 85 percent accurate but tells you when it's uncertain is more useful than a system that's 90 percent accurate but gives you no signal about its confidence. These aren't obvious intuitions. They have to be built through practice.

**ALEX:** How do you build that intuition in PMs who have never worked with probabilistic systems before?

**KEVIN:** We use a few approaches. One is what I call "prediction exercises." We give PMs a set of AI outputs and ask them to predict which ones are correct before they see the ground truth. This builds calibration -- the ability to estimate probabilities accurately. We also do "failure mode workshops" where we take a real AI feature and systematically explore what can go wrong. Not in the abstract, but with real examples. Here's an input where the system gave a wildly wrong answer. Here's one where it was subtly wrong in a way that's hard to catch. Here's one where it was technically correct but misleading. Walking through 50 of those examples in an afternoon changes how PMs think about AI capabilities and limitations in a way that no amount of reading or lecturing can replicate. The concrete, visceral experience of seeing the AI be confidently wrong is what rewires the mental model.

**KEVIN:** The second skill area is evaluation design. I touched on this earlier, but let me go deeper. Eval design is the ability to create test sets, define scoring criteria, and assess AI system performance in a rigorous and systematic way. In practice, this means the PM needs to be able to curate a representative set of test inputs, define what a "correct" output looks like for each input, identify edge cases and adversarial inputs, define quantitative metrics like precision, recall, and accuracy in terms that map to user outcomes, and run evaluation cycles regularly as the system evolves. This is a new discipline for most PMs, but it's absolutely essential. I tell my PMs: "If you can't evaluate it, you can't ship it." And "if you can't evaluate it, you definitely can't improve it."

**ALEX:** Does the PM actually run the evals, or do they work with engineering to define what gets evaluated?

**KEVIN:** Both, depending on the maturity of the team. In the early stages, I actually want PMs to run evaluations manually. I want them to sit with a spreadsheet of 200 AI outputs and grade each one against their criteria. It's tedious, but it builds deep understanding of the system's strengths and weaknesses. Once that understanding exists, the PM works with engineering to automate the evaluation pipeline. The PM continues to own the eval criteria and curates the test set, but the execution becomes automated. The PM then shifts to reviewing eval results, identifying trends, and updating criteria as the product and user expectations evolve. Think of it like how a PM might manually test their product in the early days and then work with QA to build automated test suites as it matures. The same evolution happens with AI evaluation, just with probabilistic rather than deterministic assertions.

**KEVIN:** The third skill area is policy writing, and by that I mean the ability to articulate clear, unambiguous rules that govern AI system behavior. This is related to the product policy document I described earlier, but it goes beyond the PRD into things like system prompts, guardrail configurations, and operating rules. A PM working on an AI feature needs to be able to write things like: "When the user's query is ambiguous, the system should ask a clarifying question rather than guessing. An ambiguous query is defined as one where the system identifies two or more plausible interpretations with less than 20 percentage points separating their confidence scores." That kind of policy writing requires precision, domain knowledge, and an understanding of how the AI system actually processes inputs. It's closer to writing regulations than writing user stories, and it's a muscle most PMs haven't developed.

**ALEX:** That's a really different skill from what PMs are typically hired for. When you interview PM candidates now, are you screening for this differently?

**KEVIN:** We are. We've added a case study to our interview process where candidates are given an AI feature concept and asked to define the autonomy boundaries, the evaluation criteria, and the exception flows. We're not looking for perfect answers -- this is new for almost everyone. We're looking for how they think about uncertainty, how they reason about trade-offs between automation and user control, and whether they naturally ask questions like "what happens when the system is wrong?" versus only thinking about the happy path. The candidates who gravitate toward that kind of thinking tend to thrive in AI-native product work. The ones who keep trying to specify exact behaviors for every scenario tend to struggle, because they're trying to apply a deterministic mental model to a probabilistic system.

### SEGMENT 5: How the PM-Engineering Collaboration Model Changes (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's shift to the organizational side. The PM-engineering relationship has always been central to how products get built. How does that dynamic change when AI is in the mix?

**KEVIN:** It changes in several important ways. The first and most obvious one is that the gap between idea and working prototype collapses. When your engineering team has AI-assisted coding tools and the product itself is AI-powered, you can go from concept to functioning prototype in hours rather than weeks. At GWI, I've seen engineers take a PM's product policy document and have a working proof of concept by the end of the day. That sounds great, and in many ways it is. But it also fundamentally changes the rhythm of PM-engineering collaboration.

**KEVIN:** In the traditional model, there's a long lag between when the PM hands off a spec and when there's something to evaluate. That lag creates natural checkpoints: spec review, design review, sprint planning, mid-sprint check-in. In the AI-native model, the cycle time is so short that those checkpoints need to be reimagined. You can't do weekly spec reviews when your team is producing three prototypes a day. The collaboration becomes more continuous and more real-time. PMs need to be available for rapid evaluation and feedback rather than batching their input into scheduled meetings.

**ALEX:** Does that mean PMs need to be more technical? I know that's a perennial debate in product management, but it feels like the answer might actually be different this time.

**KEVIN:** I think the answer is more nuanced than "PMs need to be more technical." What PMs need is more technical fluency in AI-specific domains. They don't need to write Python or understand transformer architectures at a mathematical level. But they do need to understand concepts like what a prompt does and why prompt design matters, what RAG is and when it's the right approach versus fine-tuning, what embeddings are and why they enable semantic search, what temperature and other generation parameters do and how they affect output quality, and what evaluation metrics like precision, recall, and F1 score mean in practical terms. At GWI, we call this "conversational technical fluency" -- the ability to have a productive, substantive conversation with an AI engineer about system design trade-offs without needing to understand the implementation details. It's the same level of fluency we've always expected PMs to have with web technologies. You don't need to write SQL, but you should understand what a database join is and why it matters for page load time.

**KEVIN:** The second big change in the PM-engineering relationship is that the PM becomes more involved in what I'd call "system tuning" rather than "feature specification." In traditional product development, the PM's job is mostly front-loaded -- you do the heavy lifting on requirements and then engineering executes. In AI-native development, the PM's involvement is more evenly distributed throughout the lifecycle because the system needs continuous calibration. You ship the feature, you observe how users interact with it, you review the evaluation metrics, you adjust the confidence thresholds, you refine the prompts, you update the guardrails. The PM is a continuous participant in the system's behavior, not a one-time input at the beginning.

**ALEX:** How do you structure that operationally? Because it sounds like it could become overwhelming if every PM is continuously tuning every AI feature they own.

**KEVIN:** Great question, and this is where organizational design matters. At GWI, we've evolved our squad model so that each squad with significant AI-powered features has what we call an "AI triad" -- a PM, an engineer, and a data or ML specialist who share responsibility for the ongoing performance of the AI system. The PM owns the "what" and the "why" -- what should the system do, why are the current thresholds where they are, why did we choose these evaluation criteria. The engineer owns the "how" -- the technical implementation, the model architecture, the infrastructure. The ML specialist owns the "how well" -- the model performance, the evaluation pipeline, the data quality. None of them can do their job without the other two, and they meet daily rather than weekly because the iteration cycle is that fast.

**KEVIN:** The third change is that engineers become more involved in product decisions, and PMs become more involved in technical decisions. The boundary between product and engineering gets blurrier because the decisions are entangled. When you're deciding whether to use a frontier model with high accuracy but high latency and cost, versus a smaller model with lower accuracy but faster and cheaper, that's simultaneously a product decision and a technical decision. The PM can't make that call without understanding the technical trade-offs, and the engineer can't make it without understanding the product implications. So you end up with more joint decision-making, which requires more trust and more shared context between PMs and engineers.

**ALEX:** Does that create friction? In my experience, blurry boundaries between roles can lead to either productive collaboration or territorial conflict.

**KEVIN:** It can go either way, and I've seen both. The teams that handle it well are the ones that are clear about decision rights even when the boundary between roles is blurry. We use a simple framework: the PM has final say on "what" and "for whom," the engineer has final say on "how" and "with what," and they jointly own "how well" and "at what cost." When there's disagreement, we escalate to the question of user impact -- the option that delivers more value to users with acceptable risk wins, regardless of which side of the PM-engineering divide it came from. The teams that struggle are the ones where either the PM tries to dictate technical decisions or the engineer tries to dictate product decisions. AI-native product development requires genuine cross-functional partnership, not just coordination.

### SEGMENT 6: Training Programs -- Building AI-Native PM Capability at Scale (7 minutes)

**ALEX:** You mentioned the training programs you've built at GWI. Tell me about that. If a listener is a VP of Product or a CPO and they need to upskill their PM team, what should the curriculum look like?

**KEVIN:** We built a four-module program that we've now run across all 20 squads, and I think the structure is transferable to most product organizations. The first module is AI Literacy. Before PMs can design AI-native products, they need a mental model for how AI actually works. We cover large language models, embeddings, RAG versus fine-tuning, agents and function calling, and the fundamental distinction between deterministic and probabilistic systems. The goal is not to turn PMs into ML engineers. The goal is to give them enough understanding that they can make informed product decisions. A PM who understands that LLMs can hallucinate will design products with verification layers. A PM who understands token economics will make better decisions about where to use AI and where traditional logic is more appropriate. A PM who understands that model performance degrades on out-of-distribution inputs will design better feedback loops for edge cases.

**KEVIN:** The second module covers AI Product Design Patterns -- the recurring patterns that show up across successful AI products. Progressive autonomy, where the system starts with low autonomy and earns more as it demonstrates accuracy. Confidence-gated actions, where behavior changes based on the system's confidence level. Explanation interfaces, where the AI shows its reasoning alongside its recommendation. Human-in-the-loop workflows, where the AI does the heavy lifting but a human validates critical decisions. Graceful degradation, where the product remains fully usable even when the AI fails. We teach each pattern with real examples, anti-patterns, and hands-on exercises where PMs apply the patterns to their own products. By the end of this module, PMs have a vocabulary and a toolkit for common AI product challenges.

**ALEX:** What's the most impactful part of the program? The thing that creates the biggest shift in how PMs think?

**KEVIN:** Without question, it's the fourth module, which is Hands-On Building. We give PMs access to AI development tools and ask them to build a working AI prototype for their own product. They write system prompts, configure tools, set up guardrails, test with real data, and present the results. The first time a PM who's never touched AI development builds a working prototype in an afternoon, it completely reframes their understanding of what's possible. They stop thinking of AI as a black box that engineering delivers and start thinking of it as a material they can shape directly. That shift in mindset is worth more than all the conceptual training combined. We also have a third module on AI Ethics and Safety, covering bias, transparency, regulatory requirements, and error consequences. That module is especially critical for our teams because we deal with audience data that touches demographics and behavioral patterns, so the ethical considerations are real and immediate, not abstract.

**KEVIN:** But I want to be honest about what training can and can't do. Training builds awareness and foundational skills. What actually changes behavior is practice, feedback, and organizational incentives. If you train your PMs on AI-native product design but then evaluate them on feature velocity and story point throughput, you'll get exactly the same behavior you had before. The training has to be paired with changes to how you evaluate PM performance, how you structure team rituals, and how you make decisions about what to build. Otherwise it's just a nice offsite that doesn't change anything.

### SEGMENT 7: Real-World Lessons and What Comes Next (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's close with some real-world lessons. You've been doing this across 20 squads in 4 offices. What's surprised you? What have you gotten wrong? What advice would you give to someone who's just starting this journey?

**KEVIN:** Three lessons stand out. The first is that the adoption curve is bimodal. About 30 percent of our PMs took to AI-native product design almost immediately. They were curious, they were experimenting, they were pushing the boundaries of what our AI capabilities could do. Another 30 percent were resistant -- not because they didn't understand the value, but because the shift felt threatening to their identity and expertise. They'd spent years building the skill of writing detailed specifications, and now we were telling them that skill was necessary but not sufficient. The middle 40 percent were waiting to see which direction the organization would go. The lesson there is that you need to invest disproportionately in the early adopters, make their successes visible, and create pull rather than push. Mandating AI adoption doesn't work. Showing people that their peers are building better products with new methods does work.

**KEVIN:** The second lesson is that the biggest obstacle isn't skills, it's mindset. Specifically, it's the transition from a control mindset to a guidance mindset. Traditional PMs want to control every aspect of the user experience. They want to know exactly what will happen when a user clicks a button. AI-native PMs need to be comfortable with the fact that they're guiding a system that has some degree of autonomy and unpredictability. They're setting the boundaries, not scripting the behavior. That mindset shift is harder than learning any specific skill, and it takes longer. We've found it typically takes 3 to 6 months of active practice before a PM genuinely internalizes probabilistic thinking rather than just intellectually understanding it.

**ALEX:** And the third lesson?

**KEVIN:** The third lesson is that the PM role becomes more valuable, not less, in an AI-native world. There's a narrative out there that AI will replace PMs, or at least dramatically reduce the need for them. I think the opposite is true. When your product behaves probabilistically, when it makes autonomous decisions, when it needs continuous calibration and evaluation -- the need for someone who deeply understands the user, the domain, and the business context actually increases. The nature of the work changes, but the importance of having someone who can bridge the gap between what users need and what the technology can deliver only grows. The PMs who adapt will be more valuable than ever. The ones who don't will find themselves increasingly sidelined, not because the role disappears, but because it evolves past them.

**ALEX:** If you could give one piece of actionable advice to a PM listening right now who wants to get ahead of this curve, what would it be?

**KEVIN:** Start building. Today. Don't wait for your company to have an AI strategy. Don't wait for a training program. Take a real problem from your product, open up Claude or whatever AI tool you have access to, and try to build a prototype solution. You'll learn more in one afternoon of hands-on building than in a month of reading think pieces about AI product management. Try to build an AI feature that automates something your users currently do manually. You'll immediately encounter all five of the activities I described: you'll need to define what "correct" looks like, you'll need to set autonomy boundaries, you'll need to think about feedback, you'll hit confidence questions, and you'll need to design what happens when it's wrong. That one afternoon will give you a visceral understanding of why AI-native product management is a genuinely different discipline. And then start every task in your regular work by asking, "Can AI do the first pass on this?" Build the habit of what I call "AI first, human in the lead." Use AI as your starting point for research, for drafting, for analysis, for prototyping. But always apply your judgment, your domain knowledge, and your product intuition as the final layer. That combination -- AI capability plus human judgment -- is what makes an AI-native PM powerful.
