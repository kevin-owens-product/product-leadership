### SEGMENT 1: THE CAPABILITY TAXONOMY (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Before you can decide whether to build, buy, or partner, you have to know what you're deciding about. You've developed a taxonomy of AI capabilities that I think is really useful. Walk us through how you categorize the different kinds of AI capabilities a software company might need.

**KEVIN:** The taxonomy has four buckets, and the bucket a capability falls into should heavily influence your build-buy-partner decision. The first bucket is what I call strategic differentiators. These are the AI capabilities that directly create unique customer outcomes -- the things that make your product irreplaceable. If you're a vertical SaaS company serving the logistics industry, your strategic differentiator might be a route optimization engine that learns from your customers' actual delivery patterns over time. If you're in healthcare software, it might be a clinical decision support model trained on years of patient outcomes data from your installed base. These are the capabilities where your proprietary data, your domain knowledge, and your deep understanding of customer workflows combine to create something that no generic vendor can replicate. Strategic differentiators almost always skew toward build.

**ALEX:** That makes intuitive sense. What's the second bucket?

**KEVIN:** The second bucket is operational enablers. These are AI capabilities that make your business run better but don't directly face the customer as a product feature. Think about internal tooling -- an AI system that automates your QA process, or a model that predicts customer churn so your success team can intervene early, or an intelligent routing system that assigns support tickets to the right specialist. These capabilities create real value, but they're not the reason a customer chooses your product over a competitor's. They make you more efficient, they improve your margins, they make your team more productive. But a customer isn't going to switch from a competitor to you because you have better internal churn prediction. Operational enablers often skew toward buy, because there are usually mature vendor solutions and the cost of building from scratch rarely justifies the investment.

**ALEX:** I'd imagine there's a gray zone, though. Like, if your support ticket routing becomes so good that it materially changes the customer experience, doesn't it start to cross over into being a differentiator?

**KEVIN:** That's a really astute observation, and it highlights why this taxonomy isn't static. Capabilities can migrate between buckets over time. Your ticket routing system that starts as an operational enabler might evolve into something that genuinely differentiates your customer experience. When that happens, you need to reassess whether your acquisition strategy still fits. Maybe you started with a bought solution, but now the capability has become so central to your value proposition that you need to bring it in-house. That migration pattern is actually one of the most important things to watch for, and we'll talk more about it when we get to portfolio management. But let me finish the taxonomy. The third bucket is compliance-critical controls. These are AI capabilities where regulatory requirements, auditability, or liability exposure are the dominant considerations. In financial services, this might be an AI model used for credit decisioning that needs to comply with fair lending laws. In healthcare, it's anything touching patient data that falls under HIPAA. In insurance, it's underwriting models that regulators want to be able to audit and explain. For compliance-critical controls, the build-buy-partner decision isn't primarily about speed or cost -- it's about control, transparency, and your ability to demonstrate to regulators exactly how the system makes decisions.

**ALEX:** And I'd imagine that creates a strong pull toward building in-house, because you need to be able to open the hood and explain everything to a regulator.

**KEVIN:** It does pull toward build, but not always. Sometimes the smartest move is to partner with a vendor that specializes in regulated AI and has already done the work of getting their models validated by regulators in your industry. The key is that you need deep governance visibility regardless of whether you build or buy. If you buy a compliance-critical capability, your contract and your architecture need to give you the same level of auditability and control that you'd have if you built it yourself. That's a non-negotiable. The fourth and final bucket is commodity infrastructure. These are the AI building blocks that every company needs but that create zero competitive differentiation. Large language model APIs, vector databases, embedding services, speech-to-text transcription, OCR, basic sentiment analysis -- these are capabilities where the technology is well-understood, multiple vendors offer high-quality solutions, and the differences between providers are marginal. Building your own vector database from scratch in 2025 is like building your own relational database in 2005. You could do it, but why would you? Commodity infrastructure should almost always be bought, and the primary decision variable is vendor reliability and pricing, not strategic alignment.

**ALEX:** So just to crystallize this: strategic differentiators skew build, operational enablers often skew buy, compliance-critical controls need deep governance regardless of the model, and commodity infrastructure should almost always be purchased. That gives you a starting framework, but I'm guessing the real complexity is in the nuance. How do you actually score a capability to determine which bucket it belongs in?

**KEVIN:** That's exactly where the decision matrix comes in. The taxonomy tells you what kind of capability you're dealing with. The decision matrix tells you what to do about it.

### SEGMENT 2: THE FIVE-DIMENSION DECISION MATRIX (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** So let's get into the decision matrix. You've identified five dimensions that should drive the build-buy-partner decision. What are they, and how do you actually use them in practice?

**KEVIN:** The five dimensions are strategic relevance, time-to-value, total cost of ownership, risk and compliance exposure, and option value. And the critical point is that you score each capability across all five dimensions, not just one or two. I've seen teams make terrible decisions because they optimized for a single dimension -- usually time-to-value, because the pressure to ship something is always intense -- and ignored the others. Let me walk through each one. Strategic relevance is about how closely the capability ties to your core value proposition and competitive differentiation. Score this on a spectrum. At one end, you have capabilities that directly produce the outcomes your customers are paying for. At the other end, you have capabilities that are purely supportive infrastructure. A useful test is to ask: if a competitor had a significantly better version of this capability, would we lose customers? If the answer is yes, strategic relevance is high, and that pulls you hard toward build.

**ALEX:** What about time-to-value? That feels like the dimension that creates the most tension in practice, because there's always someone in the room saying, "We need this yesterday."

**KEVIN:** Time-to-value is genuinely important, but it's also the dimension most susceptible to short-term thinking. The question is: how quickly do you need this capability deployed and delivering measurable results? If you're a PE-owned company and your value creation plan calls for launching an AI-powered feature by Q3 to hit your revenue targets, you may not have 18 months to build a custom solution from scratch. In that scenario, buying a vendor solution and integrating it might be the only path that gets you to market in time. But here's the trap: time-to-value pressure often leads teams to buy capabilities that they should be building, and then they spend the next two years paying the tax on that decision. The vendor's roadmap doesn't align with yours, the integration is brittle, the cost scales in ways you didn't anticipate. So when I score time-to-value, I always ask a second question: what's the time-to-value penalty if we build, and is that penalty truly fatal, or is it just uncomfortable? Sometimes waiting six extra months to ship a built solution is the right call even if it's painful in the short term, because the long-term value of owning the capability is so much higher.

**ALEX:** Total cost of ownership is the third dimension. I imagine this is where a lot of build-versus-buy analyses fall apart, because people compare the wrong costs.

**KEVIN:** This is the dimension where I see the most analytical errors. When teams evaluate a buy option, they look at the vendor's pricing page and say, "It's 50,000 dollars a year, that's way cheaper than hiring two engineers to build it." And they're right about the direct cost comparison. But total cost of ownership includes so much more. On the buy side, you need to account for integration costs, which are almost always higher than you expect. You need to factor in the ongoing maintenance of the integration as both your product and the vendor's product evolve. You need to consider data egress costs, API rate limit constraints, the cost of your team learning and managing the vendor's tooling. You need to model what happens to your costs as you scale -- does the vendor charge per API call, per user, per gigabyte? How does that scale curve compare to the scale curve of a built solution? And you need to think about switching costs. If you decide in two years that this vendor isn't working, what does it cost to migrate? On the build side, the common error is underestimating the total effort. Teams scope the initial build but forget about ongoing maintenance, model retraining, monitoring infrastructure, on-call burden, and the opportunity cost of those engineers not working on something else. A genuine TCO analysis models all of these factors over a three-to-five-year horizon, not just the year-one comparison.

**ALEX:** The fourth dimension is risk and compliance exposure. This connects back to the compliance-critical controls bucket in the taxonomy, but I imagine it's relevant even for capabilities that aren't primarily about compliance.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. Risk and compliance exposure isn't just about regulated industries. Every AI capability carries some level of risk. If you're using an AI model to generate customer-facing content, there's brand risk if the model produces something inappropriate. If you're using AI to make recommendations that affect pricing, there's legal risk if the model exhibits discriminatory patterns. If you're processing customer data through a third-party AI service, there's data privacy risk. The question is: how much risk does this capability introduce, and does your build-buy-partner decision adequately account for that risk? When risk exposure is high, you need either direct control over the system, which favors build, or contractual and architectural guarantees from your vendor that give you equivalent control. I've seen companies learn this lesson the hard way. One portfolio company I worked with bought a third-party AI model for customer scoring and deployed it in production without fully understanding how the model weighted certain demographic features. When a customer complained about discriminatory treatment and the company couldn't explain the model's reasoning, they had a real problem. They ultimately had to rip out the vendor solution and build their own model with full explainability, which cost them more time and money than building it right the first time would have.

**ALEX:** The fifth dimension is option value, which is a concept I don't hear discussed enough in these decisions. Explain what you mean by that.

**KEVIN:** Option value is the strategic flexibility that a particular build-buy-partner decision creates or forecloses. Think of it like financial options. When you build a capability in-house, you create optionality. You can extend it, modify it, combine it with other internal systems, take it in directions you didn't originally anticipate. You own the asset, and ownership confers flexibility. When you buy from a vendor, you gain speed but you lose some optionality. You're constrained by the vendor's API surface, their roadmap, their architectural decisions. When you partner, you might gain shared optionality if the partnership is structured well, or you might lose it if the partnership creates dependency. A concrete example: imagine you buy a third-party recommendation engine for your e-commerce platform. It works well today. But two years from now, you want to integrate your recommendation engine with your pricing system, your inventory management, and your customer loyalty program to create a holistic personalization experience. Can the vendor's system support that level of integration? Probably not, because the vendor built a general-purpose recommendation engine, not one that's deeply integrated with your specific business systems. If you'd built the recommendation engine yourself, you'd have the option to extend it in any direction. That lost optionality has a real cost, and it's the cost that teams most often fail to account for in their analysis.

**ALEX:** So when you put all five dimensions together, you're essentially creating a scorecard for each AI capability, and the scores guide your decision. Is it as simple as adding up the numbers, or is there more art to it?

**KEVIN:** There's definitely art to it. The scores create a direction, not a destination. A capability that scores high on strategic relevance and option value but moderate on time-to-value pressure is a strong build candidate, even if a vendor could deliver it faster. A capability that scores low on strategic relevance, low on risk exposure, and high on time-to-value pressure is a clear buy. But there are cases where the dimensions conflict, and that's where leadership judgment comes in. The matrix doesn't replace decision-making -- it structures it so that you're at least asking the right questions instead of letting the loudest voice in the room win.

### SEGMENT 3: WHEN TO BUILD (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Now that we have the framework, let's get specific about each option. Let's start with build. When is building the right answer, and what are the indicators that tell you this capability must be built in-house?

**KEVIN:** There are four primary indicators for build, and when multiple indicators are present simultaneously, the case becomes very strong. The first indicator is that the capability drives unique customer outcomes that are directly tied to your value proposition. If you're a supply chain optimization company, your predictive demand modeling is the product. No vendor is going to build a demand model that understands the specific patterns of your customers' supply chains as well as you can, because those patterns are encoded in your proprietary data and your domain expertise. When you hear a customer say, "I chose your product because your predictions are better than anyone else's," that's the clearest possible signal that the underlying capability must be built and owned. You cannot outsource your reason for existing.

**ALEX:** What's the second indicator?

**KEVIN:** The second indicator is what I call proprietary workflow intelligence. This is when your product's value comes not just from a single model or algorithm, but from the deep understanding of how your customers actually work. Let me give you a concrete example. I worked with a legal technology company that built an AI system for contract review. On the surface, you might think, "Just use GPT-4 with a good prompt -- that's a buy decision." But the reality was far more nuanced. This company had spent years watching how corporate lawyers actually review contracts. They understood the specific clauses that get negotiated in different industries. They knew that a limitation of liability clause in a software licensing agreement is reviewed differently than the same clause type in a construction contract. They had mapped out the entire workflow: which sections lawyers read first, what they flag versus what they skip, how the review process differs between a first draft and a third revision. All of that workflow intelligence was baked into their proprietary system. A generic LLM couldn't replicate it because the value wasn't in the language processing -- it was in the orchestration layer that knew how to apply language processing within the specific context of each workflow. That orchestration layer had to be built in-house because it encoded years of domain observation that no vendor had.

**ALEX:** The third indicator?

**KEVIN:** Compounding memory advantages. This is one of the most powerful arguments for building, and it's the one that's most relevant in the AI era. Some capabilities become exponentially more valuable as they accumulate data and experience. Every prediction your model makes, every customer interaction it processes, every feedback signal it receives makes it slightly better. Over time, this creates a flywheel that becomes incredibly difficult for competitors to replicate, because they'd need to accumulate the same volume and quality of data to train a comparable model. If you buy this kind of capability from a vendor, you don't own the flywheel. The vendor's model might improve over time based on aggregate data across all their customers, but you lose the advantage of your specific data making your specific instance of the model better than anyone else's. I've seen this play out in fraud detection for fintech companies. A company that builds its own fraud model and trains it on years of its own transaction data develops a model that's exquisitely tuned to its customer base, its transaction patterns, its specific risk profile. A bought fraud detection service might be good generically, but it'll never be as good for your specific context as a model trained on your data.

**ALEX:** And the fourth indicator?

**KEVIN:** Integration depth as a differentiator. This is when the value of the AI capability comes not from the AI itself in isolation, but from how deeply it's woven into the fabric of your product. Think about a product analytics platform that uses AI to surface insights. The value isn't just in the AI's ability to find patterns -- it's in how those patterns are connected to specific user actions, how insights trigger automated workflows, how the AI's recommendations integrate with the customer's existing dashboards, alerts, and reporting pipelines. That level of integration requires the AI to be an organic part of your architecture, not a bolted-on external service. When you buy a vendor's AI and plug it in via API, there's always a seam. The data has to be serialized, sent over the wire, processed externally, and the results sent back. That seam introduces latency, limits the richness of context you can provide to the model, and creates a dependency boundary that constrains how tightly you can integrate. When you build, there's no seam. The AI has direct access to your entire data model, your event stream, your application state. It can operate on the same data structures that the rest of your application uses. That architectural intimacy enables experiences that are simply impossible with an external service.

**ALEX:** I want to push back on one thing, because I can already hear the counterargument from the "buy everything" camp. They'd say that modern AI APIs are incredibly capable, that the gap between a custom model and a foundation model with good prompting has narrowed dramatically, and that building from scratch is hubris. How do you respond to that?

**KEVIN:** It's a fair challenge, and they're right that the gap has narrowed at the model layer. Foundation models are astonishingly capable. But the argument conflates two different things. When I say "build," I don't necessarily mean training a model from scratch. Almost nobody should be pre-training large language models. What I mean by "build" is owning the intelligence layer that sits on top of foundation models. It means owning the fine-tuning data, the prompt engineering, the retrieval-augmented generation pipeline, the evaluation and feedback loops, the orchestration logic, the decision policies. You might use a foundation model from OpenAI or Anthropic as your base, and that's a buy decision for commodity infrastructure. But the system that wraps around that model -- the system that decides what context to provide, how to break complex tasks into subtasks, how to validate outputs, how to learn from user corrections -- that system is your intellectual property, and that's what you build. The confusion between "building a model" and "building an AI capability" leads to a lot of bad decisions. People hear "build" and think you're saying they should train their own transformer from scratch. That's not the argument at all.

**ALEX:** That's a really important clarification. So building is about owning the orchestration, the data pipelines, the fine-tuning, and the domain-specific intelligence -- not necessarily the base model.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And that distinction maps cleanly to the taxonomy. The base model is commodity infrastructure -- buy it. The orchestration and domain intelligence are strategic differentiators -- build them.

### SEGMENT 4: WHEN TO BUY AND WHEN TO PARTNER (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's flip to the other side. When is buying the right answer? What signals tell you to reach for a vendor rather than building?

**KEVIN:** There are four clear indicators for buy. First, you're dealing with a commodity layer where the technology is well-established and multiple vendors offer comparable solutions. Vector databases, embedding APIs, speech-to-text services, basic document OCR -- these are capabilities where there is no competitive advantage in building your own. The vendors have teams of specialists dedicated to optimizing these specific capabilities. Your engineers would be reinventing an already well-optimized wheel. Second, speed is the dominant constraint and a vendor can get you to production in weeks rather than months. This is common in PE-owned companies where the value creation plan has specific milestones tied to specific dates. If you need an AI-powered chatbot for customer support deployed by the end of Q2 because it's in the operating plan, and a vendor can have you live in six weeks while a build would take six months, buying is the pragmatic answer. The strategic purity of building is worthless if you miss the window that matters.

**ALEX:** That makes sense. What's the third indicator?

**KEVIN:** The third indicator is that there's a well-established vendor market with proven solutions and meaningful competitive dynamics. When multiple vendors are competing in a category, that competition drives quality up and prices down. You benefit from the vendor's ongoing R&D investment across their entire customer base. In OCR, for example, vendors like Google and AWS have invested billions of dollars in training document processing models across millions of documents in every language and every format. Their solutions handle edge cases -- faded receipts, handwritten notes, unusual layouts -- that your internal team would spend years discovering and solving. The breadth of their training data is a genuine technical advantage that you can't replicate even if you tried. The fourth indicator is low strategic value. If the capability doesn't differentiate your product and isn't going to become a differentiator in the foreseeable future, buying is almost always the right answer. Your engineering team's time is a finite resource, and every hour they spend building a non-differentiating capability is an hour they're not spending on something that moves the needle competitively.

**ALEX:** Now let's talk about the third option: partner. I feel like this is the one that gets the least attention in these discussions, but it can be incredibly powerful when it's the right fit. When does partnership make sense?

**KEVIN:** Partnership is the right model in several specific situations, and I want to distinguish genuine partnership from just being a customer with a fancy contract. A real partnership involves shared risk, shared investment, and shared influence over the roadmap. It's fundamentally different from a vendor-customer relationship. The first situation where partnership excels is shared R&D in domains that require deep specialization that neither party can efficiently develop alone. Let me give you an example. Suppose you're a property management software company and you want to build AI capabilities for predictive maintenance -- predicting when HVAC systems, elevators, and plumbing are going to fail before they actually break. You have the software platform and the customer relationships, but you don't have expertise in IoT sensor data or industrial equipment modeling. An industrial IoT company has the sensor expertise and the equipment models, but they don't have a property management software platform or the customer base. Neither of you can build the complete solution efficiently alone. But together, you can combine your platform and customer relationships with their sensor expertise to build something neither could create independently. That's a partnership candidate.

**ALEX:** What about complementary capabilities as a driver for partnership?

**KEVIN:** That's the second major situation. Sometimes two companies each have a piece of the puzzle, and combining them creates something more valuable than either piece alone. I saw this work beautifully with a healthcare analytics company that partnered with a medical imaging AI firm. The analytics company had deep relationships with hospital systems and a platform for clinical decision support. The imaging company had state-of-the-art models for radiology analysis but no distribution channel. They structured a partnership where the imaging AI was embedded within the analytics platform, the analytics company got a capability that would have taken them years to build, and the imaging company got access to hundreds of hospitals overnight. The key structural element was that both parties had skin in the game -- revenue sharing, joint development commitments, and shared governance over the integrated product roadmap. When both sides have something to lose, partnerships tend to be durable.

**ALEX:** Are there situations where partnerships make sense primarily for market access or regulatory reasons?

**KEVIN:** Absolutely. Market access partnerships are common when you're trying to enter a geography or a vertical where you lack credibility or regulatory standing. In financial services, for example, certain AI capabilities in areas like credit scoring or anti-money-laundering require regulatory approval that takes years to obtain. Partnering with a company that already has that regulatory standing can cut your time-to-market from years to months. Similarly, in government and defense technology, you might need a partner with specific security clearances or contract vehicles that would take your company years to obtain independently. The partnership isn't about the technology -- it's about access. And regulatory-driven partnerships can be incredibly sticky, which is a double-edged sword. They can provide durable competitive advantage, but they can also create dependencies that are hard to unwind.

**ALEX:** You mentioned distinguishing genuine partnership from just being a customer with a fancy contract. What makes a partnership genuinely strategic versus just a vendor relationship with better marketing?

**KEVIN:** Three structural elements. First, co-design rights. In a real partnership, both parties have meaningful input into the product roadmap. You're not just taking what the partner ships -- you're jointly deciding what gets built and when. Second, transparent SLAs with teeth. If the partner's contribution degrades, there are clear remedies, and the SLAs cover performance dimensions that actually matter to your customers, not just uptime. Third, and this is the one most people miss, clear portability terms. A well-structured partnership acknowledges from day one that the relationship might end, and it specifies what happens to data, models, and integrated capabilities if it does. If a "partner" won't discuss portability terms, they're not a partner -- they're a vendor trying to lock you in. The partnerships that work best are the ones where both parties could walk away but actively choose not to because the combined value is too compelling.

### SEGMENT 5: HYBRID MODELS AND PORTFOLIO MANAGEMENT (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** In practice, most companies don't make a pure build, pure buy, or pure partner decision for each capability. They end up with hybrid approaches. How does that work, and how do you manage the complexity that hybrids introduce?

**KEVIN:** Hybrid models are not just common -- they're the norm, and they're often the optimal strategy. The most prevalent hybrid is what I call "buy the foundation, build the differentiation." You purchase a commodity infrastructure layer -- a foundation model API, a vector database, a monitoring service -- and you build your proprietary intelligence layer on top of it. This is what most successful AI-native companies actually do. They're not training their own models from scratch, but they're not just calling an API with a generic prompt either. They've built sophisticated orchestration, fine-tuning pipelines, evaluation frameworks, and domain-specific data assets that sit on top of purchased infrastructure. This hybrid captures the best of both worlds: you get the scale and capability of foundation models without the absurd cost of building them, and you retain ownership of the intelligence that actually differentiates your product.

**ALEX:** What are some other common hybrid patterns?

**KEVIN:** A second pattern is "partner for velocity, build for long-term." You start with a partnership to get a capability to market quickly, and you simultaneously invest in building the capability internally, with the explicit plan to transition from the partner to your own solution over a defined timeline. This works well when time-to-market pressure is intense but the capability is strategically important enough that you can't afford to be permanently dependent on a third party. I worked with a company that partnered with a specialized NLP vendor to launch an AI-powered contract analysis feature. The partnership got them to market in three months. Meanwhile, their internal team spent the next year building a purpose-built contract analysis system trained on their customers' actual contracts. At the 15-month mark, they migrated from the partner's system to their own. They didn't burn the partnership -- the partner moved into a supporting role providing specific capabilities that weren't strategic -- but the core intelligence was now in-house.

**ALEX:** That's a clever approach, but it sounds expensive. You're essentially paying for the capability twice -- once through the partner and once through the internal build.

**KEVIN:** It is more expensive in the short term, and you need to be honest with your board and your investors about that. But the framing isn't "paying twice." The framing is "paying for speed and learning simultaneously." The partnership gives you market presence and revenue while your internal team is building. And critically, the partnership gives your team a working reference implementation. They can study what works well about the partner's solution, identify its limitations, and design their own solution to address those gaps. They learn faster because they have a live system to observe. So while it looks expensive on a pure cost comparison, the total value created -- market presence plus learning acceleration plus eventual ownership -- often justifies the investment. This is a pattern that PE firms are increasingly comfortable with because it manages the tension between short-term milestones and long-term asset building.

**ALEX:** Let's zoom out to the portfolio level. A typical mid-market software company might have fifteen or twenty AI capabilities in various stages of development or procurement. How do you manage that portfolio over time?

**KEVIN:** Portfolio management for AI capabilities requires a regular review cadence -- I recommend quarterly at minimum -- where you reassess each capability against the taxonomy and the decision matrix. And the key word is "reassess." The landscape is shifting underneath you constantly. A capability that was correctly classified as a buy twelve months ago might need to shift to build because the vendor's quality has declined, or because the capability has become more strategically important than you anticipated, or because your data assets have grown to the point where a proprietary model would significantly outperform the vendor's generic offering. Conversely, a capability you've been building might need to shift to buy because a new vendor has entered the market with a superior solution, or because your internal team is struggling and the opportunity cost of their continued investment is too high.

**ALEX:** What does the governance structure look like for this? Who owns the portfolio review, and how do you avoid it becoming a bureaucratic exercise?

**KEVIN:** The review should be owned jointly by the CTO and the CPO, with input from finance and the business unit leaders who are closest to customer impact. It's not a committee decision by consensus -- that produces mediocrity. You need a decision-maker, usually the CTO, with input from stakeholders. The structure I recommend is a capability portfolio map that visualizes every AI capability on two axes: strategic importance on the vertical axis and current acquisition model -- build, buy, or partner -- on the horizontal axis. Each capability is a dot on this map. You color-code by health status: green means the current acquisition model is appropriate and working well, yellow means it's working but showing signs of misalignment, red means the current model is clearly wrong and needs to change. The quarterly review focuses on the yellows and reds. Why is this capability misaligned? What's changed? What's the migration plan? And critically, what's the timeline and cost of migrating from, say, buy to build? The discipline is in doing this review consistently, not just when something breaks. By the time a buy-to-build migration becomes urgent, you've already lost months of development time that you could have been using to build incrementally.

**ALEX:** You mentioned migrations between models. What does a typical migration look like? Say a company realizes it needs to shift a capability from buy to build. How does that actually work?

**KEVIN:** The worst way to do it is a hard cutover -- you switch from the vendor to your internal solution on a specific date. That's high-risk and usually ends badly. The better approach is a parallel run. You build your internal capability alongside the vendor solution. Both systems process the same inputs, and you compare outputs. Your internal system is in "shadow mode" -- it's producing results, but those results aren't being served to customers yet. The vendor system remains the system of record. Over time, as your internal system's quality converges with or exceeds the vendor's, you start shifting traffic. Maybe 10 percent of requests go to your system first, with the vendor as fallback. Then 25 percent. Then 50. Then 90. Then you fully cut over and deprecate the vendor integration. This gradual migration reduces risk, gives you continuous quality comparison data, and lets you identify and fix issues before they affect customers. It typically takes three to six months from the start of parallel run to full cutover, depending on the complexity of the capability.

### SEGMENT 6: VENDOR EVALUATION AND COMMON MISTAKES (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's talk about vendor evaluation, because even when buy is the right decision, picking the wrong vendor can be disastrous. What framework do you use for evaluating AI vendors specifically, as opposed to traditional software vendors?

**KEVIN:** AI vendors require a different evaluation framework than traditional software vendors because the failure modes are different. With a traditional SaaS vendor, the primary risks are downtime, feature gaps, and pricing. With an AI vendor, you have all of those risks plus a set of AI-specific risks: model quality degradation over time, training data contamination, bias and fairness issues, inconsistent outputs, and the vendor's ability to keep pace with the absurdly fast rate of change in the AI field. So my evaluation framework has several AI-specific dimensions beyond the standard vendor assessment. First is model transparency. Does the vendor tell you what models they're using under the hood? Can you get access to evaluation metrics, not just marketing benchmarks? Some AI vendors treat their model as a complete black box and refuse to share any details about architecture, training data, or evaluation methodology. That's a red flag, especially for compliance-critical capabilities. You need to understand enough about how the model works to assess whether it's appropriate for your use case and whether it's likely to remain appropriate as your use case evolves.

**ALEX:** What other dimensions do you evaluate?

**KEVIN:** Second is data handling. Where does your data go when you send it to the vendor? Is it used to train or improve the vendor's models? Can you get a contractual commitment that your data won't be used for training? In some cases, you might actually want your data used for training if it means the model improves for your specific use case. But you need to make that decision consciously, not discover it in the fine print. Third is fallback and portability. What happens if the vendor goes down, raises prices dramatically, gets acquired, or pivots their product in a direction that doesn't serve you? Do you have a contractual right to your data and your fine-tuning assets? Can you migrate to a different provider without starting from scratch? The AI vendor landscape is volatile -- companies are being acquired, pivoting, running out of funding, and changing their pricing models at a pace that makes long-term dependency genuinely risky.

**ALEX:** That volatility point is important. I've heard of companies that built their entire product on top of a specific AI vendor's API, and then that vendor changed their pricing by 300 percent overnight.

**KEVIN:** It happens more than people realize. And the architectural defense against it is abstraction. You should never have a hard dependency on a single AI vendor's API throughout your codebase. Build an abstraction layer -- a common interface that your application code calls -- and implement that interface for each vendor you might use. If vendor A raises prices or degrades quality, you should be able to swap in vendor B by changing a configuration, not by rewriting your application. This is standard software engineering practice for any external dependency, but I'm continually surprised by how many teams skip it for AI vendors, probably because they're moving fast and treating the integration as a prototype that became production. Fourth dimension in the evaluation is roadmap alignment. Where is the vendor headed, and does it align with where you need to go? This is hard to assess because vendors are incentivized to tell you what you want to hear. But you can infer a lot from their recent product releases, their hiring patterns, their blog posts and conference talks, and the profile of their other customers. If a vendor is increasingly focused on enterprise sales and you're a mid-market company, their roadmap will eventually drift away from your needs. If they're investing heavily in a particular modality or domain that's irrelevant to you, that's a signal.

**ALEX:** Let's shift to mistakes. What are the most common errors you see companies making in their build-buy-partner decisions for AI?

**KEVIN:** The single most common mistake is what I call the "build everything" syndrome, and it's almost always driven by engineering culture rather than strategic logic. The CTO or VP of Engineering has a strong team, they're excited about AI, and they want to build everything from scratch. They convince the executive team that buying is risky, that vendors can't be trusted, that the internal team can build something better. And sometimes they're right. But often they end up spending 18 months building a mediocre version of something a vendor has already spent years perfecting. Meanwhile, the features that would actually differentiate the product sit in the backlog because the engineering team is consumed with rebuilding commodity infrastructure. I saw a company spend nine months building their own text embedding service. Nine months of senior engineering time to produce something that was marginally worse than what OpenAI offers via API for a fraction of a penny per call. That's not strategic building -- that's engineering vanity.

**ALEX:** What's the opposite mistake?

**KEVIN:** The opposite is buying your core differentiator. This usually happens when a non-technical executive or board member pushes for speed and doesn't understand what's strategically important. They hear that a vendor can deliver AI-powered feature X in eight weeks, while the internal team says it'll take six months. The executive chooses the vendor because the timeline is better. But feature X is the thing that makes the product special. The thing customers love. The thing competitors can't easily replicate. And now it's running on a vendor's infrastructure, built on the vendor's models, with the vendor's data pipeline. Two years later, every one of your competitors is using the same vendor and offering functionally identical capabilities. You've commoditized your own differentiator. This is the mistake I fight hardest against, because it's the one with the most severe long-term consequences. You can always build something later that you should have bought earlier -- you just wasted some time and money. But if you buy your core differentiator, you may permanently lose the opportunity to build a proprietary advantage.

**ALEX:** What about partner-specific mistakes?

**KEVIN:** The biggest partner mistake is what I call "partnership without portability." A company enters a partnership agreement that creates deep integration but includes no exit provisions. They build their product on the assumption that the partnership will last forever. And then something changes -- the partner gets acquired by a competitor, the partner pivots their product direction, the partner raises prices because they know you're locked in. And the company discovers that migrating away from the partner requires essentially rebuilding a major portion of their product. I've seen this freeze companies for years. They know the partnership isn't working, but the switching cost is so high that they can't afford to leave. The fix is to negotiate portability terms at the start of the partnership, when you have leverage, not after you've become dependent. Every partnership agreement should include clear provisions for data ownership, model ownership, integration specification portability, and reasonable transition timelines if either party exits.

**ALEX:** Are there patterns in how these mistakes correlate with company maturity or ownership structure?

**KEVIN:** Definitely. Startups tend toward the "build everything" mistake because they're engineering-led and they equate owning code with owning advantage. Growth-stage companies tend toward the "buy your differentiator" mistake because they're scaling fast and optimizing for speed. And PE-owned companies are susceptible to all three, depending on the operating partner's philosophy. The PE context adds a unique twist because the hold period creates artificial time pressure. An operating partner with a three-year exit horizon might push to buy capabilities that should be built, because the build timeline extends beyond the hold period. That's rational from the current fund's perspective, but it can leave the next owner -- whether that's another PE firm or a strategic acquirer -- with a product that's hollowed out and dependent on vendors for its core capabilities. The best PE operators think about this and structure their build-buy-partner decisions to create genuine long-term value, not just short-term optics.

### SEGMENT 7: THE PE PLAYBOOK AND CLOSING FRAMEWORK (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let's bring this together with some specific guidance for PE operating partners and portfolio company leaders. If you're walking into a PE-owned software company on day one and you need to establish a build-buy-partner strategy for AI, what's the playbook?

**KEVIN:** The first thing you do, ideally in the first 30 days, is build a comprehensive capability inventory. Map every AI capability the company currently has, everything on the roadmap, and everything they should have but don't. Classify each one using the taxonomy: strategic differentiator, operational enabler, compliance-critical control, or commodity infrastructure. Score each one across the five decision dimensions. This gives you a portfolio view that tells you where the company is correctly allocated and where it's misaligned. In my experience, you'll find that most companies have at least two or three major misalignments. Maybe they're building a capability that's clearly commodity. Maybe they're buying something that should be proprietary. Maybe they have no partnership strategy at all.

**ALEX:** And then what? Once you've done the inventory, how do you prioritize?

**KEVIN:** You sequence based on impact and urgency. The highest priority moves are the misalignments that are actively destroying value -- building commodity infrastructure that's consuming engineering capacity you desperately need, or buying a core differentiator that's eroding competitive advantage. Those get addressed first. The second priority is establishing new capabilities that the value creation plan requires but that don't currently exist. For each one, you run it through the framework and make an explicit, documented build-buy-partner decision with a clear rationale. The third priority is optimization -- taking existing capabilities that are correctly allocated but could be improved. Maybe you have a vendor solution that's working fine but you could negotiate better terms, or a built capability that could be enhanced with better data pipelines. The entire exercise should produce a living document that maps each capability to its acquisition model, its strategic rationale, its current health status, and its migration plan if applicable. That document becomes the foundation for your quarterly portfolio reviews.

**ALEX:** Any final rules of thumb for listeners who want to take this framework and start applying it tomorrow?

**KEVIN:** Three rules of thumb. First, buy speed and build moats. When time is the primary constraint and the capability isn't strategically differentiating, buy without guilt. But when you're dealing with something that could become a durable competitive advantage, invest the time to build it right, even if it takes longer. Second, partner where leverage compounds. The best partnerships are the ones where the combined value of the two parties is genuinely greater than the sum of the parts, and where the partnership creates competitive barriers that neither party could build alone. Don't partner for convenience -- partner for compounding advantage. Third, and this is the meta-lesson: treat your build-buy-partner portfolio as a living strategy, not a one-time decision. The AI landscape is changing faster than any technology landscape I've seen in 25 years of doing this work. A decision that was correct six months ago might be wrong today. Build the organizational muscle to reassess continuously and migrate when the facts change.

**ALEX:** Kevin, this has been an incredibly practical framework. I think the key takeaway for our listeners is that the question isn't "build or buy or partner" as a philosophical stance. It's "which capabilities belong in which bucket, and why, and how do we manage this as a portfolio over time." That reframe alone is worth the price of admission.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. The companies that get this right don't have a build culture or a buy culture. They have a strategic allocation culture. They know what they're good at, they know what matters for differentiation, and they're ruthlessly honest about where their engineering time creates the most value. That's the discipline, and it's one that separates the companies that successfully navigate the AI transformation from the ones that waste years and millions of dollars on the wrong side of the build-buy-partner equation.
