### Who Berkshire is

**NORA:** Let's start with the basics. Who is Berkshire Associates?

**JAMES:** Berkshire is an HR compliance and workforce analytics firm. It's been around for roughly forty-three years, founded in the early nineteen-eighties, and it's based in Columbia, Maryland, which is between Baltimore and Washington. That location isn't an accident. It's close to the federal agencies whose rules drove a lot of its business.

**NORA:** And what did they actually do, day to day?

**JAMES:** Historically, the heart of the business was affirmative action plans, usually shortened to AAPs. If you were a company with federal contracts above a certain size, you were required to produce a written affirmative action plan every year. That plan analyzed your workforce by job group, compared it to the available labor pool, set placement goals where you were under-represented, and documented what you were doing about it.

**NORA:** And most companies didn't want to build that in-house.

**JAMES:** Most didn't. It's specialized, it's data heavy, and the stakes are real, because the Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs, the OFCCP, could audit you. So companies hired firms like Berkshire to take their HR data, clean it, run the analyses and produce the plan. Berkshire says it serves more than two thousand clients a year across more than one hundred and fifty industries, and that its consultants average more than ten years of experience.

**NORA:** Two thousand clients a year is a lot of plans.

**JAMES:** It is. And it tells you something about the business model. This wasn't a firm doing a handful of giant projects. It was high volume, recurring, annual work. Very predictable revenue, because the requirement came back every year like a tax return.

**NORA:** And they built software around it too, right?

**JAMES:** They did. Berkshire has a web-based product family called BALANCEworks. There's BALANCEaap for building affirmative action plans and BALANCEpay for pay equity analysis. Some clients used the software themselves, and others had Berkshire's consultants do the work for them. And there's a client portal where clients upload their data.

**NORA:** So, a services firm with a software layer.

**JAMES:** That's how I'd describe it. Expert services, delivered through fairly light software. The expertise is the product. The software is how the data moves.

**NORA:** Now, Berkshire isn't independent anymore. Walk us through the ownership.

**JAMES:** In October 2022, Berkshire was acquired by Resolution Economics, which people in the industry call ResEcon. ResEcon is a Los Angeles-based economics and statistics consultancy, founded in 1998. They do expert testimony, damages analysis in labor and employment cases, financial advisory work, and more recently AI governance and bias testing services.

**NORA:** So a firm full of labor economists and testifying experts buys a firm full of compliance consultants.

**JAMES:** Which makes a lot of sense. The two sides speak the same language. Berkshire does the compliance analytics that employers need every year. ResEcon does the heavy statistical work when something turns into a lawsuit or an investigation. And a year later, in November 2023, ResEcon also bought the affirmative action, DEI and pay equity practices of Biddle Consulting Group, based in Folsom, California, and folded them in alongside Berkshire.

**NORA:** And behind ResEcon there's a private equity firm.

**JAMES:** Yes. Levine Leichtman Capital Partners, a Los Angeles private equity firm, acquired Resolution Economics in January 2020, in partnership with management. And in late 2024 ResEcon moved into a multi-asset continuation fund that Levine Leichtman closed, led by Hamilton Lane. So it's a classic private equity buy-and-build. A platform company, ResEcon, adding specialist businesses around it.

**NORA:** Who leads the business?

**JAMES:** Berkshire's website lists Beth Ronnenburg as President of the Berkshire division. And on the ResEcon side, Ali Saad is the managing partner. One name I'd flag for listeners is Victoria Lipnic, who leads ResEcon's Human Capital Strategy Group. She's a former Commissioner and acting Chair of the EEOC, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.

**NORA:** That's real credibility in this world.

**JAMES:** Huge credibility. When you're advising an employer on whether their pay practices or hiring tools would stand up to scrutiny, having someone who used to run the agency that enforces those laws is a meaningful differentiator. Keep that in mind, because it comes back when we talk about the future.

### The industry Berkshire grew up in

**NORA:** Let's zoom out to the industry itself. Because I think to understand what happened to Berkshire, you need to understand the rules it was built on.

**JAMES:** Agreed. So the story starts in 1965. President Lyndon Johnson signed Executive Order 11246. It said that companies doing business with the federal government couldn't discriminate in employment, and, importantly, that they had to take affirmative action to ensure equal opportunity. Over time that became a detailed set of regulations enforced by the OFCCP, which sits inside the Department of Labor.

**NORA:** And those regulations are where the annual plan came from.

**JAMES:** Right. If you were a federal contractor with fifty or more employees and a contract of fifty thousand dollars or more, you generally had to maintain a written affirmative action plan for women and minorities, updated every year. The regulations spelled out exactly what went in it. Organizational profiles, job group analyses, availability analyses, comparisons of incumbency to availability, placement goals.

**NORA:** Which sounds abstract. Make it concrete. What does that work actually involve?

**JAMES:** Okay. Picture a mid-sized manufacturer with three plants. Every year they need to pull together a snapshot of every employee: job title, job group, location, race and sex, pay. Then every applicant, every hire, every promotion and every termination over the plan year. Often that data lives in two or three different HR systems, a recruiting system and a pile of spreadsheets.

**NORA:** And it's never clean.

**JAMES:** It's never clean. Job titles don't map neatly to job groups. Applicants are missing demographic data. Someone changed locations halfway through the year. A plant uses a different code for the same role. So a huge amount of the work, before any analysis happens, is collecting the data, chasing the client for what's missing, verifying it, and getting it into a standard format.

**NORA:** That's interesting, because I think people imagine the value of a compliance firm is the analysis at the end.

**JAMES:** The analysis and the judgment are where the expertise shows. But if you look at where the hours go, I'd bet a large share goes into intake. Gathering files, emailing back and forth about missing fields, checking that the numbers tie out. That's true across most of this industry, not just Berkshire.

**NORA:** Hold that thought, because I think that's central to the opportunity later.

**JAMES:** It absolutely is.

**NORA:** Now, the race and sex plans weren't the only requirement, right?

**JAMES:** No. There were two other big pieces. Section 503 of the Rehabilitation Act, which covers individuals with disabilities, and VEVRAA, the Vietnam Era Veterans' Readjustment Assistance Act, which covers protected veterans. Both required their own affirmative action programs for federal contractors. Under Section 503, for example, there was a seven percent utilization goal for individuals with disabilities in each job group.

**NORA:** So three separate plans, layered on top of each other.

**JAMES:** Three plans, plus a set of annual reports. The EEO-1 report, which most employers with a hundred or more employees file with the EEOC, and the VETS-4212 report on veteran hiring for contractors. Plus audit support when the OFCCP came knocking.

**NORA:** Who were the customers?

**JAMES:** Federal contractors and subcontractors, which is a much bigger group than people realize. It's not just defense companies. It's banks, universities, hospitals, logistics firms, technology companies, manufacturers. If you sell to the federal government, directly or through a prime contractor, you were likely covered.

**NORA:** And the buying behavior was what?

**JAMES:** Mandatory and recurring. That's the key word. You didn't buy an affirmative action plan because it was strategic. You bought it because you had to, every year, and the cost of getting it wrong was an audit, a conciliation agreement or, in theory, losing your federal contracts. So switching costs were moderate, price mattered, but the demand itself was locked in.

**NORA:** That's a beautiful business, as long as the rule exists.

**JAMES:** As long as the rule exists. Which is exactly where our story turns.

[MUSIC STING]

### The shock

**NORA:** So what happened?

**JAMES:** On January twenty-first, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14173. Among other things, it revoked Executive Order 11246, the 1965 order we just talked about. Contractors were given a short wind-down period, about ninety days, to stop complying with the affirmative action obligations under the old order.

**NORA:** So, sixty years of a requirement, ended by one executive order.

**JAMES:** The legal foundation ended with that order. But the regulations themselves, the actual rules in the Code of Federal Regulations, still needed to be formally rescinded. And that took longer. The Department of Labor issued final rules in August 2026, and the rescission of the race and sex affirmative action regulations took effect on October twenty-sixth, 2026.

**NORA:** So just a few weeks ago.

**JAMES:** Just a few weeks ago. That's the date I'd circle. That is the moment the core annual product, the race and sex affirmative action plan, stopped being a legal requirement for federal contractors.

**NORA:** What about the disability and veterans plans?

**JAMES:** Those come from statutes passed by Congress, the Rehabilitation Act and VEVRAA, so an executive order can't simply make them disappear. But they've been slimmed down. From what we've read in law firm alerts, the Section 503 requirements were cut back significantly this year, including the seven percent utilization goal and the associated analysis, and the contract threshold for VEVRAA has gone up. So the plans still exist, but they're smaller, simpler, and, frankly, cheaper to produce.

**NORA:** Which is a different kind of problem for a firm like Berkshire.

**JAMES:** Right. It's not just that one product line disappeared. It's that the remaining products got simpler, and simpler products get commoditized. If a Section 503 plan is now a much lighter document, clients will expect to pay a lot less for it, and more of them may try to do it themselves with software.

**NORA:** And then there was another executive order this year, wasn't there?

**JAMES:** There was. In April 2026, Executive Order 14398. As law firms like Arnold and Porter described it, it requires federal contracts and subcontracts to include a clause barring what the order calls racially discriminatory DEI activities, with that obligation flowing down to subcontractors.

**NORA:** So the direction of travel isn't just "less compliance." It's a different kind of compliance.

**JAMES:** That's the nuance people miss. The obligation flipped. For decades, contractors had to show they were taking affirmative steps. Now, contractors have to make sure their diversity programs don't cross a line into what the government considers discrimination. And that creates its own demand for review and advice.

**NORA:** Let's be honest about the size of the hit, though. If two thousand clients a year were buying plans largely because of these rules, that's a serious problem.

**JAMES:** It's an existential-level change to the core revenue line. I don't want to overstate it, because Berkshire does more than race and sex plans. But that was the anchor product, and the anchor product is gone. Any firm in that position has to answer two questions quickly. How do we run the business profitably at a smaller size? And what do we sell next?

**NORA:** And the industry as a whole is going through this.

**JAMES:** Every affirmative action specialist in the country is going through it. Berkshire's competitors, the firms we'll talk about later, are all asking the same questions. What's different is how each one answers them.

**NORA:** One thing I want to ask. Could this swing back? A future administration could reinstate something like Executive Order 11246.

**JAMES:** It could. Rules that are made by executive order can be unmade by executive order. But I don't think a smart company bets its strategy on that. Even if it came back in a few years, the firms that survive the gap will be the ones that found new revenue in the meantime. So I'd treat a reversal as upside, not the plan.

**NORA:** That's a good principle. Don't plan around the policy you wish you had.

**JAMES:** Plan around the one you've got.

[MUSIC STING]

### What's still in force, and what's growing

**NORA:** Okay. So that's the bad news. Let's talk about what hasn't gone away, because I think this is where the story gets more hopeful.

**JAMES:** It does. A lot of employment law has nothing to do with federal contracting. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act still prohibits discrimination in pay and employment for most employers. The Equal Pay Act is still there. Employees can still sue. And class actions over pay and hiring are still a real risk for large employers.

**NORA:** So the legal exposure didn't go away. Just the paperwork requirement.

**JAMES:** That's a good way to put it. The duty to produce an annual affirmative action plan for women and minorities is gone for contractors. The risk of being sued for discrimination is not. And for a lot of employers, the plan was actually a useful early-warning system. It forced them to look at their numbers every year.

**NORA:** And reporting still exists too.

**JAMES:** Yes. EEO-1 reporting to the EEOC continues for employers with a hundred or more employees. VETS-4212 continues for covered contractors. Those are steady, but they're also pretty commoditized. Lots of software can produce them.

**NORA:** Now let's talk about the growth areas. Where's the new demand coming from?

**JAMES:** I'd point to three big ones. Pay equity and pay transparency, AI in hiring, and the European Union.

**NORA:** Start with pay.

**JAMES:** In the US, there's been a wave of state pay transparency laws over the past few years. Colorado was an early mover. California, Washington, New York and Illinois followed, along with others. Depending on the state, employers have to post salary ranges in job ads, provide pay data, or report pay by demographic group. California, for example, has required larger employers to file pay data reports with the state.

**NORA:** And that's a patchwork.

**JAMES:** It's a messy patchwork, which is great news for experts. Every state is a little different. Multi-state employers need someone to keep track of what applies where and to make sure their pay structures hold up when the ranges are public.

**NORA:** And the EU piece?

**JAMES:** The EU Pay Transparency Directive is a big deal. Member states had to transpose it into national law by June seventh, 2026. It requires pay transparency in hiring, gives workers the right to request pay information, and requires employers to report their gender pay gap. The largest employers, those with a hundred and fifty or more workers, have their first reports due in June 2027, and if there's an unexplained gap of five percent or more in a category, they may need to do a joint pay assessment with worker representatives.

**NORA:** That matters for American companies too.

**JAMES:** Absolutely, because any US multinational with operations in Europe is covered for those operations. And many of Berkshire's clients are exactly that kind of company. So you have a set of clients who already trust Berkshire with their HR data, who suddenly need help with a brand new pay reporting regime.

**NORA:** Is there any sizing on the pay equity market?

**JAMES:** There is, with a caveat. One market research firm, Mordor Intelligence, puts the pay equity analytics software market at about one point oh seven billion dollars in 2026, growing to about two point three nine billion by 2031. That's roughly seventeen percent a year. North America is about forty-four percent of it. I'd treat that as directional, because it's one vendor's estimate. But the direction is clearly up.

**NORA:** So the old market is shrinking while an adjacent market grows at high teens.

**JAMES:** That's the shape of it. And then the third area, AI in hiring.

**NORA:** This one feels very current.

**JAMES:** It's very current. Employers are using AI tools to screen resumes, rank candidates and run assessments. Regulators have noticed. New York City's Local Law 144 has required bias audits for automated employment decision tools since 2023. Colorado passed a broader AI law that covers high-risk decisions, including employment. And the EU's AI Act treats AI used in hiring as high-risk.

**NORA:** And underneath all of that is the same old question. Is this tool treating some groups unfairly?

**JAMES:** It's exactly the same statistical question that adverse impact analysis has always asked. Are selection rates different across groups? Is the difference significant? Can the employer justify it? Firms like Berkshire and ResEcon have been doing that analysis for decades for traditional hiring. The tool being tested has changed. The math hasn't.

**NORA:** That's a really nice bridge from the old business to the new one.

**JAMES:** It's the best bridge they have. And ResEcon already markets AI governance, bias assessment and model validation services. So this isn't a stretch. It's an extension.

**NORA:** Let me summarize where we are. Mandatory race and sex plans, gone. Disability and veterans plans, smaller and simpler. Standard reports, steady but commoditized. Pay equity, pay transparency and AI hiring audits, growing.

**JAMES:** That's the map. And the question is how fast Berkshire can move its weight from the shrinking boxes to the growing ones.

[MUSIC STING]

### The competitive landscape

**NORA:** Let's talk about competition, because Berkshire isn't the only firm making this move.

**JAMES:** Not at all. I'd group the competitors into three camps.

**NORA:** Go.

**JAMES:** Camp one is the other compliance services firms. The closest like-for-like rival is probably DCI Consulting. Then there are firms like Affirmity, OutSolve and Circa. They all did the same kind of affirmative action, audit and reporting work, and they're all facing the same shock.

**NORA:** So in that camp, everyone's fighting over a smaller pie.

**JAMES:** Right, and when the remaining products get simpler, the competition tends to become about price. If a Section 503 plan is now a lighter piece of work, the firm that can produce it most efficiently wins. That's a cost game.

**NORA:** Camp two?

**JAMES:** Camp two is pay equity software. Companies like Syndio, Trusaic, Payscale, Salary.com and beqom. These are software-first businesses. They sell platforms that let HR and compensation teams run pay equity analyses, model adjustments and handle pay transparency requirements. Trusaic, for example, has been expanding into Europe around the pay transparency directive.

**NORA:** So they own the growth market Berkshire wants to move into.

**JAMES:** They're already there, and they have product teams, venture or private equity funding and a head start on software. That's the honest challenge. If Berkshire moves into pay equity, it's moving into a market where software companies have already set customer expectations about dashboards, self-service and speed.

**NORA:** And camp three?

**JAMES:** Camp three is big-firm advisory. The large HR consultancies like Mercer and Aon, and employment law firms. They win the largest enterprises, especially when a company wants its pay audit done under attorney-client privilege, so that the findings are protected if there's later litigation.

**NORA:** So Berkshire is squeezed in the middle. Cheaper compliance shops on one side, slick software on another, and premium advisory on the third.

**JAMES:** That's the squeeze. And the question for any company in the middle is: what's your edge?

**NORA:** So what is Berkshire's edge?

**JAMES:** In my view, it's defensibility. Berkshire's analysis is backed by ResEcon's economists and testifying experts, people who have defended statistical methods in court. And, as we mentioned, someone like Victoria Lipnic brings deep regulatory credibility. Software can tell you there's a gap. Experts can tell you whether that gap would hold up in front of a regulator, a jury or a works council, and what to do about it.

**NORA:** So the pitch would be: software speed, expert defensibility.

**JAMES:** That's the pitch I'd want. Software tools tend to be strong on the dashboard and weaker on the "what would a court say" question. Big firms are strong on the court question but expensive and slow. A firm that could combine fast, modern delivery with real expert depth has a genuinely differentiated position.

**NORA:** The catch being that Berkshire has to actually become fast and modern.

**JAMES:** That's the catch. The edge only matters if the delivery model can compete on cost and experience. Which brings us to the operational challenge.

[MUSIC STING]

### The operational challenge

**NORA:** Let's dig into that. What does it actually take for a firm like Berkshire to compete in this new world?

**JAMES:** I think it starts with an uncomfortable fact. In most expert services firms in this space, the real work happens in spreadsheets. There may be a portal on top, a workflow tool, some software, but underneath, consultants are taking client files, reshaping them in Excel, checking them by hand and emailing clients about gaps.

**NORA:** And that worked when volume was locked in.

**JAMES:** It worked fine when you had two thousand clients who had to come back every year and prices that supported a large team. It doesn't work nearly as well when your highest-volume product disappears, your remaining products get cheaper, and you're trying to compete with software companies on new products.

**NORA:** So the business model has to change, not just the product list.

**JAMES:** Yes. Let me break down the workflow, because I think it makes the challenge concrete. There are roughly four stages in a typical engagement. One, intake: getting the data from the client. Two, verification: checking that the data is complete and correct. Three, preparation: getting it into the standard shape the analysis needs. And four, the analysis and advice itself.

**NORA:** And the expensive part is?

**JAMES:** The first three. My guess, from how this industry works, is that intake, chasing and cleanup consume a large share of consultant time. It's the most repetitive part of the job, and frankly it's not the part clients value. Nobody hires an expert to send them five emails asking for missing hire dates.

**NORA:** So that's where automation would go first.

**JAMES:** That's where I'd start. Let's take each one. Missing-data outreach. Today, a consultant notices a file is missing fields, writes an email, waits, follows up, waits again. Software could detect the gaps automatically as soon as the file lands, generate a clear request to the right client contact, track what's outstanding and nudge them. The consultant only gets involved when something is genuinely unusual.

**NORA:** And verification?

**JAMES:** Verification is about rules. Does every employee have a job group? Do the counts tie to last year plus hires minus terminations? Are there pay values that look like typos, like an annual salary entered as an hourly rate? A lot of those checks can be written down as rules, and AI can help with the fuzzier ones, like spotting that two job titles are probably the same role.

**NORA:** And then preparation.

**JAMES:** Preparation is mapping each client's messy files into a standard format. Every client's HR system exports data a little differently. Today, someone does that mapping by hand, often every year. If the system remembers last year's mapping for each client and proposes it automatically, and AI helps map new columns, that's a big time saver.

**NORA:** So far this sounds like classic automation. Where does AI really come in?

**JAMES:** I'd say AI is the glue that makes the messy parts tractable. Classic automation is great when data is clean and predictable. Client HR data is neither. Large language models are good at reading a strange spreadsheet, guessing what a column means, drafting a clear request in plain English, or summarizing what changed since last year. The important thing is that the AI proposes and a human expert approves.

**NORA:** I want to push on that, because this is sensitive data. We're talking about employee pay, race, sex, disability status, veteran status.

**JAMES:** That's the right push. This is some of the most sensitive data an employer holds. So any firm automating this needs to be very disciplined. Data stays in approved environments. Access is tightly controlled. There's a clear audit trail of who changed what. And no client data gets sent to tools that haven't been vetted.

**NORA:** And then there's the question of trust in the output.

**JAMES:** Right. The whole value proposition is defensibility. If automation introduces errors that end up in an analysis, you've damaged the one thing clients pay for. So I'd want every new piece of automation to run in what people call shadow mode first.

**NORA:** Explain shadow mode.

**JAMES:** You run the new process alongside the old one for a period. The consultants keep working the way they always have, and the new system runs on the same files in parallel. Then you compare. Did the system catch the same errors? Did it catch more? Did it miss anything? Only when the results match or beat the existing process do you switch over.

**NORA:** That's slower, but safer.

**JAMES:** Slightly slower, much safer. And it builds trust with the team, which matters, because the people who do this work today know where all the bodies are buried. If they help shape the automation, it'll be far better than anything built in isolation.

**NORA:** Let's talk about the people side, because that's the elephant in the room. If you automate a big chunk of the work, what happens to the people doing it?

**JAMES:** It's the hardest part, and I think it's important to be honest about it. Any services firm that automates repetitive work will need fewer hours for that work. The question is how leadership handles it. The best version, in my view, frames it as capacity and margin. You redeploy experienced consultants from data chasing to higher-value work: the new pay equity products, AI audits, client advisory. And where roles do change, you plan it openly with leadership before it becomes a rumor.

**NORA:** Because if staff hear about it the wrong way, the project fails.

**JAMES:** Exactly. You need the people who run these workflows to engage, not resist. And you need client-facing teams on board too, because automated outreach goes to clients, and the tone and timing of those messages reflect on the brand.

**NORA:** What about the technology foundation? Are firms like this ready to build?

**JAMES:** It varies a lot. Many have a homegrown platform built over years by a small internal team, plus a lot of spreadsheets. That's not a criticism. It's how most of these firms grew. But it means any modernization has to work with the people who own that platform, not around them. The platform owner can either be your biggest accelerator or your biggest blocker.

**NORA:** So you'd build inside their system, with their engineers.

**JAMES:** That's how I'd do it. Build in their codebase, have their engineers review and merge every change, so the knowledge stays in the company. Put in basic modern engineering practices along the way, automated testing, proper deployment pipelines, code review. AI coding tools can speed that up dramatically now, but only if the discipline is there.

**NORA:** And cost control. I've heard people worry that AI tools are their own cost problem.

**JAMES:** That's a real concern. A lot of AI vendors charge per user or per run, and costs can creep up quietly. If you're automating work to protect margin, you need to track what the AI itself costs per engagement. Otherwise you swap a labor cost for a software bill and call it progress.

**NORA:** How would you measure whether it's working?

**JAMES:** I'd keep it simple. Three measures, one per workflow. For outreach, staff hours spent chasing clients for missing data. For verification, the number of data errors caught before analysis starts. For preparation, the time from when a client's data arrives to when it's ready for analysis. Measure them before you start, measure them in shadow mode, measure them after go-live. And keep the actual results separate from the original assumptions, so nobody fools themselves.

**NORA:** That's a very private equity way to think about it.

**JAMES:** It is, and I think that's appropriate. This is a private equity-backed business. The owners will want to see the value show up in the numbers, not in a slide.

### Walking through one engagement

**NORA:** James, I want to make this even more concrete. Can we walk through a single engagement, start to finish? An illustrative one, not a real client.

**JAMES:** Let's do it. Let's invent a client. Call them Harbor Logistics. Four thousand employees, warehouses in six states, a European subsidiary in the Netherlands, and a couple of federal contracts for moving government freight.

**NORA:** Good. So what did Harbor buy from a firm like Berkshire two years ago?

**JAMES:** Two years ago, Harbor probably bought a bundle. A race and sex affirmative action plan for each of its establishments, a Section 503 plan, a VEVRAA plan, EEO-1 and VETS-4212 filing support, and maybe a pay equity study every couple of years, because their general counsel was nervous about a class action.

**NORA:** And how did that work, mechanically?

**JAMES:** Sometime in the first quarter, the consultant sends Harbor's HR team a data request. Here's the template, please send us your employee snapshot as of your plan date, plus applicants, hires, promotions and terminations for the year. Harbor's HR analyst pulls exports from their HR system and their applicant tracking system, and uploads a handful of spreadsheets to the portal.

**NORA:** And then the back-and-forth begins.

**JAMES:** Then the back-and-forth begins. The consultant opens the files and finds that the applicant file is missing disposition codes for a third of the records. Two warehouses use a different job code for forklift operators. There are forty employees with no job group. The terminations file doesn't match the headcount change. So the consultant emails a list of questions. Harbor's analyst is busy, so it takes a week to get answers, and half of them raise new questions.

**NORA:** I can feel the frustration from here.

**JAMES:** Everybody on both sides feels it. And here's the thing: none of that is the expert work Harbor is paying for. It's plumbing. But it can easily take weeks of elapsed time, and a lot of consultant hours, before anyone runs a single analysis.

**NORA:** So now replay that with the modern workflow.

**JAMES:** Okay. Same client, a year from now. Harbor's analyst uploads the same files. Within minutes, the system checks them against a set of rules and against last year's data. It sees the missing disposition codes, the unmapped job codes and the forty employees with no job group. It remembers that last year those two warehouses used the alternate job code, and it proposes the same mapping. It drafts one clear request back to Harbor's analyst, listing exactly what's missing, with examples, and tracks the answers as they come in.

**NORA:** And the consultant?

**JAMES:** The consultant reviews the proposed mappings and the draft request before it goes out, approves them, and moves on to something else. When the answers arrive, the system reruns the checks automatically. The consultant gets pulled back in only when something truly needs judgment, like a strange pattern in terminations at one site.

**NORA:** And then the analysis.

**JAMES:** Then the expert work starts, on clean, standard data, much sooner. And here's where the business changes. Because Harbor's data now flows in cleanly, it's easy to offer Harbor more. A quarterly pay equity check. A pay gap report for the Netherlands subsidiary under the EU directive. A bias review of the AI resume screener Harbor's recruiting team just bought. Same data, same relationship, new services.

**NORA:** That's the flywheel.

**JAMES:** That's the flywheel. Clean data in makes delivery cheaper, and cheaper delivery makes new products possible. And every new product makes the client stickier.

**NORA:** What does Harbor's HR analyst think about all this?

**JAMES:** Honestly, I think they love it. Fewer vague emails, one clear list of what's needed, faster turnaround and fewer surprises. Client experience matters more now than it used to, because nobody is forced to buy anymore. If buying is optional, the experience of working with you becomes part of the product.

**NORA:** That's a really important shift. The customer used to be a captive. Now they're a buyer.

**JAMES:** Exactly. When the rule required the plan, you could get away with a clunky process. When the client is choosing whether to buy a pay equity monitor at all, every bit of friction is a reason to say no, or to go to a software vendor with a slicker interface.

**NORA:** Let me ask a skeptical question. Couldn't Harbor just buy a pay equity software tool and skip the consultants entirely?

**JAMES:** Some will, and that's fine. But a lot of employers find that the software tells them there's a gap and then leaves them wondering what to do. Is the gap explained by legitimate factors? Is the model specified correctly? If we adjust pay, how much, for whom, and how do we document it? Would this hold up if a plaintiff's expert attacked it? That's where an expert firm earns its fee. The ideal is software that gets you to the question fast, and an expert who answers it.

**NORA:** And for a firm like ResEcon, whose experts actually testify in pay cases, that answer carries weight.

**JAMES:** It carries a lot of weight. If the people reviewing your pay analysis are the kind of people who defend these methods in court, that's a different level of assurance than a dashboard.

### Future opportunities

**NORA:** Okay. Let's spend some real time on the future, because I think this is the fun part. If you were advising Berkshire, what would you build?

**JAMES:** I'd think about it in stages. The first stage is defensive: cut the cost of delivery, so the business stays healthy while revenue shifts. That's the automation we just talked about. But that only buys time. The second stage is offensive: new products that grow.

**NORA:** And the foundation for both is the same thing.

**JAMES:** That's what I find elegant about it. The same data pipeline that makes delivery cheaper, the intake, the outreach, the verification, the standard prep, is the foundation for every new product. Once you can reliably get a client's HR data in, clean and standard, you can run many different analyses on it.

**NORA:** So walk me through the products. Our own ideas, to be clear.

**JAMES:** Our own ideas, working titles only. I'd look at four. The first is what I'd call Compliance Lite. The remaining Section 503 and VEVRAA plans are simpler now. Instead of a consultant-built plan, offer a mostly self-serve plan in the client portal, with expert review available. Lower price, lower cost to deliver, and it keeps those clients in the family rather than losing them to a cheaper rival.

**NORA:** That's more about keeping revenue than adding it.

**JAMES:** Exactly. It's a retention play. The second idea is more exciting. A Pay Equity Monitor. Instead of a one-off pay equity study every few years, run the analysis every quarter on the client's latest data, flag new gaps early, and have an expert review the results. Berkshire already has pay equity software in BALANCEpay, so it's building on something real.

**NORA:** Why quarterly?

**JAMES:** Because pay gaps don't wait for an annual study. Every raise, promotion and new hire changes the picture. A monitor turns a project into a subscription, and it gives the client a continuous early warning. That's valuable on its own and even more valuable as pay transparency laws make pay data public.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Pay transparency reporting. The state laws and the EU directive create recurring reporting obligations. Berkshire already does some of this as a service. The opportunity is to turn that into software: the client's data comes in through the same pipeline, the reports are generated for each jurisdiction, and an expert reviews them before they go out. The EU's first wave of large-employer reports in June 2027 is a natural deadline to build toward.

**NORA:** And the fourth?

**JAMES:** AI hiring audits. Bias audits for automated hiring tools under rules like New York City's Local Law 144, and impact assessments under laws like Colorado's. This is where ResEcon's statistical depth and Victoria Lipnic's regulatory credibility really matter. An audit is only valuable if people trust the auditor. It's a newer market, so I'd put it a bit later in the sequence, but the fit is very strong.

**NORA:** I notice a pattern in all four.

**JAMES:** There is one. In every case you take something that's sold today as expert project work, put the repeatable part into software, charge for it as a subscription, and keep expert review as the premium tier. Software for the routine. Experts for the judgment.

**NORA:** Why does the subscription part matter so much?

**JAMES:** A couple of reasons. For the business, recurring revenue is smoother and more predictable than project revenue, which also tends to be valued more highly by investors. For the client, it's often better too, because they get continuous coverage instead of a snapshot.

**NORA:** And who buys first?

**JAMES:** The former affirmative action clients. That's the hidden asset here. Berkshire has relationships with thousands of employers who have been sending it sensitive HR data every year. That trust took decades to build. Those clients don't need the race and sex plan anymore, but many of them still have pay risk, multi-state transparency obligations, European operations or AI hiring tools. They're the natural first customers for the new products.

**NORA:** That's a much easier sale than starting cold.

**JAMES:** Much easier. The data relationship is already there. The security reviews may already be done. The client already knows the consultants. You're not selling a new vendor, you're offering a new service from a trusted one.

**NORA:** How big could this be?

**JAMES:** I want to be careful here, because nobody outside the company knows the true numbers. But you can do simple math. If even a modest slice of a two thousand client base took a recurring pay equity monitor, you're talking about a meaningful recurring revenue line. The exact size depends entirely on pricing and take-up, which are things you'd test with real customers, not guess at on a podcast.

**NORA:** Fair. What would you need to believe for this to work?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, that clients will pay for continuous monitoring rather than occasional studies. Second, that Berkshire can deliver software that feels as good as the pure software players, at least for the routine parts. And third, that expert review is genuinely worth a premium in the client's eyes. If those three hold, the strategy holds.

**NORA:** And the order matters.

**JAMES:** The order matters a lot. Cut delivery cost first, because that funds everything else and keeps the business healthy. Reprice and simplify the remaining compliance plans next. Then build pay equity and transparency products. Then AI audits. Trying to do everything at once is how you end up doing nothing well.

### Going to market

**NORA:** Let's talk about selling. Because building the products is one thing, and getting customers to change how they buy is another.

**JAMES:** It's a big shift. For forty years the sales motion was essentially renewal. The client needs a plan every year, and the account team makes sure they renew. Now the sales motion has to be about risk and value. Why should you look at your pay data every quarter? What happens if you don't? What does the EU directive mean for your Dutch subsidiary?

**NORA:** So the sales team becomes more like advisors.

**JAMES:** More advisory, more educational. And marketing matters more. The firms that win the next few years will be the ones that explain the new rules clearly. Webinars on the EU directive, simple guides on state pay transparency laws, practical checklists on AI hiring audits. Berkshire already publishes a lot of this kind of content, and that's a strength.

**NORA:** How would you price?

**JAMES:** I'd think in tiers. A software-led tier where the client does most of the work in the portal and the price is low. A standard tier where Berkshire runs the analysis on a schedule. And a premium tier with expert review, a defensible written opinion, and support if a regulator or a plaintiff comes calling. That way you can compete with software on price at the bottom and with big firms on depth at the top.

**NORA:** And you sell annually.

**JAMES:** Annual subscriptions, ideally. They match how clients budget, and they give the firm predictable revenue. But I'd be careful to test the pricing with real clients before committing. The worst mistake would be setting prices based on what software vendors charge without understanding what clients value about expert review.

**NORA:** Who inside the client is the buyer now? Is it still HR?

**JAMES:** That's a great question, and I think it's changing. For affirmative action plans, the buyer was often an HR compliance manager. For pay equity, the buyer is more often the head of total rewards or compensation, and the general counsel has a big say. For AI hiring audits, you add the head of talent acquisition and sometimes the chief information security officer or the head of data. **NORA:** Which is another reason the expert credibility matters. General counsel care a lot about who signed off.

**JAMES:** They care enormously. If you're the general counsel, the question isn't "is this dashboard pretty?" It's "if this ends up in litigation, will I be glad we used these people?" That's a question ResEcon's bench is well placed to answer.

**NORA:** What about partnerships? Could Berkshire partner with the software companies instead of competing with them?

**JAMES:** That's a real option. Some pay equity software vendors partner with expert firms for the review layer, because they don't want to employ testifying economists themselves. A firm like Berkshire could be the expert partner behind a software platform, or it could build its own. I'd probably do a bit of both, depending on the client segment.

### Lessons for other services businesses

**NORA:** Before we wrap, I want to step back. You said at the start that this is really a story about a whole category of businesses. What are the lessons for other firms, and for the investors who own them?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out five.

**NORA:** Five. Okay, go.

**JAMES:** Lesson one: know which of your revenue is required and which is wanted. Required revenue feels safe, but it's only as safe as the rule behind it. Wanted revenue, where clients buy because they see value, is harder to win but more durable. Every firm should know its split.

**NORA:** Lesson two?

**JAMES:** Lesson two: your data relationships may be worth more than your products. Berkshire's most valuable asset may not be any single plan or report. It's the fact that thousands of employers trust it with their most sensitive workforce data. That relationship can carry new products. Protect it, and use it.

**NORA:** Three.

**JAMES:** Lesson three: fix delivery before you diversify. It's tempting to rush into shiny new products. But if your delivery model is expensive and manual, every new product inherits that cost. Rebuild the engine first, then add the new cars to the train.

**NORA:** Four.

**JAMES:** Lesson four: use AI where the work is messy and repetitive, and keep humans where the work is judgment. In this industry, that line is fairly clear. Reading strange spreadsheets, chasing missing data, checking consistency, drafting routine messages, all good candidates. Deciding whether a pay gap is legally defensible, not something you hand to a model.

**NORA:** And five.

**JAMES:** Lesson five: measure it, honestly. Pick a small number of measures before you start. Hours spent, errors caught, turnaround time. Run new tools alongside the old process until the numbers prove them. And report actual results separately from the original promises. That discipline is what turns an AI project from a demo into a business result.

**NORA:** I like that last one a lot. A lot of AI projects die in the gap between the demo and the results.

**JAMES:** They do. And in a private equity context, where owners have a defined hold period and want to see value created, that gap is where credibility is won or lost.

**NORA:** Do you think private equity ownership helps or hurts here?

**JAMES:** I think it can help a lot, if it's used well. Private equity owners can bring capital, outside talent and urgency, and they've seen these playbooks across many companies. They can also share lessons from one portfolio company with another. The risk is short-termism, cutting costs without investing in what comes next. The best outcome is both: cut delivery cost to protect margin today, and invest some of those savings in the products that grow tomorrow.

**NORA:** So, defend and grow at the same time.

**JAMES:** Defend first, grow right behind it. That's the whole strategy in four words.
