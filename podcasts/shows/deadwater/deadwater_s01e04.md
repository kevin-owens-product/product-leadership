# DEADWATER
## Episode 4: "Twelve Years of Silence"
### Season 1

**Duration:** ~34 minutes

---

[NARRATOR] Cold cases do not freeze evenly.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The common understanding of a cold case - that it reaches a point where the evidence runs out, the file closes, and nothing happens until some dramatic new development breaks it open - is accurate in its broad outline and misleading in almost every specific detail. Cases go cold gradually. They breathe. They surface and subside. They live in the minds of specific people who refuse to put them down, and in the evidence preserved by specific other people who understood that the science would eventually catch up to what they had in storage, and in the institutional bureaucracy of departments that can neither close the case nor fully resource it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And they live in the lives of families, who do not have the option of putting them down under any circumstances, and whose sustained presence in the public consciousness of the case is sometimes the only thing keeping it from becoming completely invisible.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This episode is about the years between the discovery of the bodies and the formation of the task force. About what those years cost. About what persisted through them. And about the very specific, very human refusal to let something be over before it was actually over.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater, Episode Four.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh keeps a calendar. Not unusual for a working detective - the job runs on deadlines and court dates and evidence submission windows. But the calendar Carol Marsh keeps for the Deadwater case has one recurring entry that has nothing to do with professional requirements. Every year, on the anniversary of the discovery of the bodies, she takes the case file home.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The complete file. By year five this is four physical binders and two external hard drives. By year ten it is seven binders, a dedicated external archive she has built on personal equipment, and a separate folder containing every piece of journalism about the case she has clipped or saved over the decade. She takes it home on a Friday and reads through it over the weekend. All of it. From the beginning. Every page.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Her husband has developed his own ritual around these weekends. He makes sure the refrigerator is stocked. He clears his own plans. He checks in periodically but does not interrupt. He has never asked her to stop doing this. He has never suggested that twelve years is long enough.

[MARSH] He calls it the obsession weekend. Affectionately. Mostly. I've thought about whether what I'm doing those weekends is actually obsession and I've decided it isn't - or at least that obsession implies something that is hurting me rather than doing something useful, and I don't think that's what this is. What I'm doing is applying a year's worth of new experience and new information to an existing evidence base. Looking for connections that didn't exist when I first read those pages, or that I missed because I was looking somewhere else, or that emerged as significant only in relation to something I learned later. It is real investigative work. It just happens on a Saturday.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She has found things on those Saturday reviews. Not always major things, but things that matter.

[MARSH] In year four I found a discrepancy in a victim's timeline that I had accepted as accurate and shouldn't have. The discrepancy changed my understanding of her route on the night she disappeared - not dramatically, but enough to shift how I thought about where the killer encountered her. In year seven I found a note in my own handwriting from the first month that mentioned a vehicle description I had marked as possibly relevant and then failed to properly follow up on. It wasn't the truck that eventually connected to Voss - different vehicle, different context. But finding it reminded me that vehicle descriptions from that corridor in that period deserved comprehensive review, and I added that to the task list for the eventual task force that I never stopped believing was coming.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese, meanwhile, is maintaining his thread book. The small handwritten notebook he carries alongside the official case management system. By year twelve the thread book has forty-seven entries - observations, name fragments, tangential geographic references, thin connections encountered in other investigations that ring some bell too faint to articulate but loud enough to write down.

[REESE] The thread book was never authorized. It was also never concealed. It is simply the working layer underneath the official documentation layer - the place where the thinking happens before it becomes formal. I have always worked this way. Every significant case I have ever run has its own thread book. Most of them are boring. Most entries lead nowhere. But the discipline of writing things down - of not allowing peripheral observations to simply evaporate from the end of a long day - has produced results often enough that I have never stopped doing it.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Entry seven in the Deadwater thread book: a building inspector, encountered in a property case, mentions unusual permit activity near the barrier island corridor. Entry eleven: a reference to an architecture firm with commercial work in the target area. Entry nineteen: a witness in a financial fraud case mentions an architect. Entry twenty-nine: a woman describes a neighbor who drives a large dark truck and commutes daily to Manhattan and who she once thought she recognized from a news photograph of the Deadwater case. Entry thirty-one points to a neighborhood. The neighborhood is Massapequa Park.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Seven. Eleven. Nineteen. Twenty-nine. Thirty-one. Five entries across nine years, each generated by a completely different case, each pointing in roughly the same direction. I do not know I am pointing at Roland Voss. I won't know that for several more years. But I am pointing, and I am writing it down, and the notebook is there when I finally need it.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The victim families, during these years, are doing something that has no name in the official investigative framework but that matters enormously to how the case eventually resolves.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They are holding on.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This sounds simple and it is not. Holding on to the memory and the grief and the ongoing need for answers through years when the investigation produces no results, when the media coverage dries up, when the official process generates no public updates - that is active, sustained, effortful work. It requires making choices every day about whether to continue engaging with a system that has consistently failed you, whether to invest emotional energy in a process that has provided mostly disappointment, whether to keep your loved one's case alive in the public consciousness even when nothing is happening and the doing of it costs you something significant.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The families do this in different ways. Some maintain public-facing advocacy. Some work within the SSPD's formal family liaison process, attending briefings and providing information when requested. Some withdraw from the official process almost entirely and focus their energy on the private grief of the loss itself. There is no right way to do it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey does it by building something.

[DONNA] I did not set out to build an organization. I was trying to survive. I was trying to help Renee's son survive. I was trying to stay connected to the truth of what happened to her. What grew out of that - the network, and eventually the formal organization, and eventually the legislative work - grew because there was a gap and people needed someone to stand in it and I was already standing there.

[NARRATOR] The organization reaches its tenth anniversary during the cold years of the investigation. At that point it serves the families of Deadwater victims and also families connected to other unsolved cases across the state and beyond. It has a staff of four, a legal advisory panel, a research team that tracks legislative developments across multiple jurisdictions, and an annual conference that brings together family advocates, investigative professionals, and policy makers for two days of working conversations about what is failing and what could be improved.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna chairs the conference every year. She attends every session. She reads every policy brief and every piece of academic research about cold case investigation, victim advocacy rights, and missing persons protocol reform that her research team produces. She is, by the time the task force is formed in 2022, one of the most knowledgeable non-practitioners in the country on the specific intersection of law enforcement procedure and family advocate rights in long-duration homicide cases.

[DONNA] People sometimes ask me if the work is healing. If building this thing has helped me process the loss of Renee. The honest answer is that I don't experience it that way. I don't think in terms of healing, as a frame. What the work does is give me somewhere to put the energy that the grief generates. It gives the anger somewhere productive to go. Whether that is healing is a question I'm not sure I'm qualified to answer. What I know is that I am functional, and I am useful, and Renee's son is fourteen and doing well and knows his mother was someone worth fighting for. That feels like enough.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In year eight, the investigation reaches what Carol Marsh describes as the moment the long patience of the case was most severely tested.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The SSPD conducts an internal resource assessment of all open cases. The assessment categorizes cases by probability of resolution and uses those categories to guide resource allocation in the following budget cycle. Deadwater is rated a two on a five-point scale. A two means low-to-moderate probability of resolution with current evidence. It means the skeletal resource base is maintained but not augmented.

[MARSH] I learned about the rating through a colleague who thought I deserved to know. It was never communicated to me officially. But knowing that the department had formally, in its budget language, classified this case as having low probability of resolution - that was a specific kind of hard. Because it meant that the institutional belief in the case had eroded in a way that even my sustained advocacy had not been able to prevent.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] My response was a memo. Eleven pages. Addressed to the interim deputy commissioner. I described exactly what the case had, what it lacked, what it would take to move it, and what I believed the result would be if those resources were provided. I attached the cell cluster analysis. I attached a summary from Dr. Park of the forensic genealogy methodology and its potential applicability to the hair sample. I attached a list of the specific investigative actions that had been identified and flagged and not resourced.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I sent it knowing it was unlikely to produce immediate results. The interim leadership was in survival mode, managing the Holden fallout. But I also sent it knowing that memos have a way of outlasting the administrations that receive them. I was writing the document the right person would read when the right moment came. I just didn't know when that was.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The right moment comes in February 2022. The right person is a new commissioner who has read Carol Marsh's eleven-page memo and who calls her into his office and spends four hours asking her questions about it.

[MARSH] At the end of those four hours, he asked me what I needed. I told him. He said: you have it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Twelve years. Two words.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] I want to end this episode with something Danny Reese said to me in our third and last interview, in the spring of last year. He has been retired for several months at that point. He is sitting in the kitchen of his house in central Long Island, the thread book on the table in front of him, open to a specific page he wants to show me.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The page shows entries twenty-nine through thirty-five. Each entry is two or three lines in his precise, angular handwriting. Each one has a date in the left margin and a brief descriptor and then a note about what it might connect to and why he wrote it down.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Entry thirty-one, dated nine years before the arrest, reads: "woman in Wantagh describ. neighbor: large, commutes city, dark truck, poss. recog. from news photo. Massapequa Park area. Geographic fit. Thin."

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Thin. He wrote "thin" when he made the entry. He knew it was a thin thread. He wrote it down anyway.

[REESE] My wife has been asking me to get rid of the notebook. She says it's time. It's over. Put it away. And she's right - it is over, and I should be able to put it down now. I haven't been able to. I keep looking at these entries and thinking about the process that produced them. Not the conclusions - the process. The discipline of writing down the thin things. Of not letting peripheral observations evaporate. Of trusting that the thin things would eventually become relevant or not, and that you couldn't know which in advance.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I think the thin things are what this case was about, ultimately. Not any single piece of dramatic evidence. A hair. A parking citation. A letter to the editor. A woman walking her dog. None of those things is decisive alone. Together, with time and with the right tools and with two people who refused to stop reading the file and one woman who refused to stop calling Albany, they add up to enough.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They add up to enough.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: The Surveillance. The task force begins its work on Roland Voss - and what they find when they start looking is exactly as complete and as deliberate as they feared it would be.

## Extended Act: The Carrying

[NARRATOR] There is a particular quality to twelve years. Not as an abstraction - as a lived thing. Twelve years is the age of a child born on the day the bodies were found. It is the distance between one decade of a life and the beginning of another. It is long enough to complete a graduate education, build a career from scratch, watch a marriage form or fail, see a parent age significantly. In twelve years the world changes around you while you are standing still in a single problem, still holding the same question, still waiting for an answer that is not there yet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The people who carried the Deadwater case through those twelve years each found their own way of understanding what they were doing and why. Carol Marsh frames it as work - as an ongoing investigation that never formally stopped, that she happened to be the primary custodian of, and that she was going to continue to do correctly regardless of the institutional support available. Danny Reese frames it as discipline - the thread book discipline, the geographic analysis discipline, the practice of not losing things even when you cannot immediately use them. Donna Kelsey frames it as obligation - not to the case in the abstract, but to Renee specifically, to Marcus, to the other eleven families who had less capacity for sustained engagement and who needed someone to be the consistent presence in the process.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Dr. Nina Park frames it in terms of the evidence.

[DR. PARK] I think about the hair every year. Not constantly - I have other cases, other work, a full career outside the Deadwater file. But every year, when I do my evidence audit, I handle the storage container that holds that sample and I think about what it represents. A piece of evidence that was collected correctly, processed correctly, stored correctly, and is waiting for a question it cannot yet be asked. My job is not to have the answer. My job is to make sure the evidence is ready when the question becomes possible.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] The question becomes possible in 2022. And the evidence is exactly where I left it, in exactly the condition it needs to be in. Twelve years of annual audits, twelve years of monitoring storage conditions, twelve years of tracking the development of forensic genealogy methodology in the academic literature, twelve years of being ready. When Carol calls me and asks if I can run the analysis, I say yes without hesitation. I have been ready for this for twelve years.

[NARRATOR] The forensic genealogy methodology that Dr. Park has been tracking through its development is, by 2022, a mature investigative tool. It has been used in a handful of high-profile cold cases across the country. It has survived legal challenge in several jurisdictions. The databases it relies on - the commercial ancestry platforms - have grown to a size that makes the analysis reliable in ways it was not when the hair was first preserved. The science has caught up with the evidence, and the evidence is ready for the science.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This convergence - evidence preserved correctly for twelve years meeting a methodology that matured on its own timeline - is not an accident. It is the product of specific decisions made by specific people. Nina Park's decision to preserve the hair with the care she preserved it with. Her decision to monitor the developing methodology and remain connected to the case over twelve years. Carol Marsh's decision to maintain the integrity of the full evidence archive through multiple institutional changes and resource constraints.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] These are not dramatic decisions. They do not look like courage from the outside. They look like routine professional practice. But routine professional practice, sustained over twelve years without institutional support or external reward, is its own form of something that deserves a name.

[MARSH] I don't think about it in terms of courage or persistence or any of the other words people tend to use. I think about it as: you have a case, you work the case, you do not stop working the case because it is inconvenient or unsupported or because the institutional momentum has shifted away from it. That is just what the work requires. The families did not have the option of the case becoming inconvenient. The least I could do was not take that option either.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In year eleven, the year before the task force is formed, an investigative journalist named Rachel Chen publishes a long-form piece in a national magazine about the Deadwater case. It is the most substantive piece of journalism about the investigation that has appeared in years, and it is excellent - rigorously reported, carefully sourced, honest about both the scale of the tragedy and the scale of the institutional failure.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The piece prompts an unusual volume of response. Letters from readers who have cases of their own, cases that share specific characteristics with Deadwater - missing women from economically vulnerable backgrounds, inadequate investigative response, families who have been fighting for years without result. A senator's office reaches out to Donna Kelsey after reading the piece and asks for a briefing. Two legislators on the relevant appropriations committees read it and ask their staffs to look into the resource allocation history of cold cases in their jurisdictions.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The piece also prompts a response from Roland Voss.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Not a public response - he never makes a public statement of any kind about the case. The response is internal, visible only in the digital record recovered years later from his devices. He reads the piece twice on the day of publication. He returns to it three more times over the following week. His monitoring of the case intensifies for approximately two months after the piece runs, and then gradually returns to the baseline level he has maintained for years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is always watching. He is never not watching.

[REESE] When we saw in the digital record that he had read the Chen piece five times, I thought about what that reading experience must have been like. To read a detailed, accurate account of the investigation's failures and successes - an account that comes very close to describing the investigative direction that would eventually find him, without knowing it - and to do it from the inside. To read a story about the hunt for yourself.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I asked a forensic psychologist about this. About what the experience of that kind of reading would be like for someone with the psychological profile that Voss presents. She said: for someone with extreme compartmentalization, reading coverage of their own crime from the outside might feel less like proximity to danger and more like reading about a character in a story. Detached. Almost academic. The connection between the external narrative and the internal reality is suppressed by the compartmentalization.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Whether that is what Voss experienced, I have no way to know. What I know is that he read the piece five times and then continued with his life. He coached youth soccer that fall. He attended a professional conference in Chicago in October. He drove to the barrier island corridor twice in December, in the early morning, in his dark truck.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is always there. Patient. Certain. Undetected.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Not for much longer.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The winter before the task force is formed, Carol Marsh does her annual case file review. It is the twelfth such review. She reads through the archive over a long weekend in December. She makes notes in her personal binder. She reads Dr. Park's original report about the hair sample and the note about the potential future application of genealogical methodology. She reads Danny Reese's cell cluster analysis and the subsequent memo requesting resources that was acknowledged and not acted on. She reads Priscilla's witness account of the man and the dark truck.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] On the last night of the weekend, she sits at her kitchen table with all of it in front of her and she writes a single line in her personal case notes.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She does not share the line with anyone. She does not put it in the official case file. It is for her, a marker of where she is and what she believes.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The line is: I know this is over. I know what comes next.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She does not yet know his name. She does not yet know what the task force will find in the parking citation or the letter to the editor or the cell cluster analysis. She does not yet know the name Roland Voss. But she knows that the evidence is there, that the question is formed, that the tools exist to ask it. She knows that when the right person gives her the resources she has been asking for, the answer will be there.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She is right.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: The Surveillance. The task force assembles, the picture of Roland Voss comes into focus, and the twelve years of carrying comes to the edge of finally meaning something.

## Extended Act: The People Who Didn't Stop

[NARRATOR] I want to spend the remaining time in this episode on a question that feels small and is not. The question is: what made them continue?

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Twelve years is a long time to carry something. It is long enough for careers to end and marriages to shift and children to grow from infants to teenagers. It is long enough for the world to change in ways that make the beginning of the twelve years feel remote. It is long enough for institutional priorities to rotate through multiple full cycles, for the political landscape to change, for the specific people who were in charge in year one to have retired or been fired or moved on and been replaced by people who have no personal stake in a case they inherited.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Against all of that - against time and institutional turnover and the natural tendency of attention to move toward the new and the immediate - three people kept the Deadwater case alive for twelve years. Kept it from becoming truly cold in the sense that would have made resolution significantly harder. Kept its threads intact, its evidence preserved, its analytical work documented and available, its families in contact with the process.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh. Danny Reese. Donna Kelsey.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] I've asked each of them what made them continue. The answers are different in their surface language and identical in their underlying logic.

[MARSH] I don't experience it as continuation. I experience it as work. The case is not solved. Therefore the case is being worked. That is how I think about it. The size of the gap between "not solved" and "solved" is not something I find useful to contemplate. The only question is what the next right step is and whether I am taking it.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] The thread book. Every time I thought about whether to keep going, the thread book was there. Forty-some entries, each of them representing a specific moment when I noticed something and chose to write it down instead of letting it go. Those entries represent a bet - a bet that accumulation matters, that thin things add up, that the act of not losing anything is itself a contribution to the eventual solution. I couldn't abandon the bet midway through. The bet was still live.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] Renee. Always Renee. And Marcus. And my mother. And the other families, once they were part of it. You cannot stop when you are the person holding things together for people who have less capacity to hold things together themselves. You cannot stop because stopping means abandonment, and abandonment is not something I am capable of with respect to these specific people.

[NARRATOR] What strikes me about these three answers is that none of them involve hope in the conventional sense. None of them say: I believed it would be solved, and that belief kept me going. Belief in the outcome is not, for any of these three people, the foundation of their persistence.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The foundation is obligation. To the work. To the evidence. To the families. To the accumulation of thin threads. To the specific, individual human beings whose names are in a file that deserves to be worked until it is finished.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] That kind of obligation does not depend on hope. It does not fluctuate with the probability of success. It is constant in the way that the evidence is constant, patient in the way that the hair sample in Nina Park's storage is patient.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In the spring before the task force is formed, Donna Kelsey receives a letter from Sandra's older daughter - the woman who has been in intermittent contact with Donna's organization for years. The letter is handwritten on two pages of plain white paper and it arrives in Donna's personal mailbox, not the organization's address, because by this point Donna and Sandra's daughter have a relationship that exists alongside but also outside the advocacy structure.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The letter is not about the case specifically. It is about a specific event: Sandra's daughter got married in October. A small ceremony, immediate family only. Her husband is a teacher. They live in the town where she grew up - the same town she grew up in without her mother, the same town where her sister still lives, a few blocks from where they grew up.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She writes to Donna about the wedding. About what it was like to get married without her mother there. About the specific way her father cried when he walked her down the aisle and how she understood that he was crying for her mother as much as for himself. About the empty seat she and her sister placed in the front row with a photograph and a single flower.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And she writes that she is writing because she wanted someone outside her immediate family to know that her mother was there in the ways her mother could be there. That she felt, in specific moments during the ceremony, a proximity to her mother that she cannot explain and does not try to. That the absence and the presence exist in the same space.

[DONNA] I read that letter four times. And then I wrote back. I told her that Renee's son Marcus had recently been in a school performance - not anything significant, a small thing, a science presentation he was particularly proud of. And I described how Marcus was at that performance. The way he stood in front of his project and explained it with the kind of specific, serious enthusiasm that Renee had about things she cared about. How he has Renee's eyes. How sometimes when I look at him at a specific angle in a specific light I see her so clearly it stops my breath.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I told her that the absent people are not only absent. That they continue in the people who loved them. That is not comfort exactly. It is just true.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] On the anniversary of the discovery, in year eleven - one year before the task force is formed - Carol Marsh is at her kitchen table at ten in the evening, reading through the case archive for the eleventh time. Her husband is asleep. The house is quiet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She gets to the section of the file containing the cell cluster analysis from year five. She has read this section every year. She knows it by heart. But she reads it again.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And this year, for the first time, she does something different. She opens her laptop and she runs a search. She is looking for new cases - cases from the past year - that used forensic genealogy methodology to identify suspects. She finds three. She reads each of them carefully, noting the specific technical approaches used and the legal processes that authorized them.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She writes a note in her personal case file: genealogy methodology now operationally reliable in multiple jurisdictions. Coordinate with Nina Park re: hair sample. Spring.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She closes the file. She makes a cup of tea. She goes to bed.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Spring comes. The new commissioner comes. The task force comes. And Carol Marsh has the memo ready because she has been writing it for two years.
