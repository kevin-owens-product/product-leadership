### THE SIX-METRIC BOARD PACK

**ASHA:** Six metrics at Q0. Portfolio ARR: three hundred ten million dollars. Portfolio LTM EBITDA: thirty-two million. Portfolio net debt: two hundred eighty-five million. Sponsor equity paid in: seven hundred fifty million. Uncalled capital: two hundred fifty million. Aggregate debt divided by aggregate EBITDA: eight point nine times.

**COLE:** That last number is arithmetically correct and operationally misleading. Divide two hundred eighty-five by thirty-two and you get about eight point nine. But no portfolio bank account receives all thirty-two million, and no single lender has a claim on all three companies.

**ASHA:** Keystone Claims has one hundred forty million of ARR, twenty-two million of LTM EBITDA, and one hundred eighty million of net debt. Its own debt-to-EBITDA ratio is about eight point two times. Retention is weak, organic growth is negative, and managed operations hold gross margin down.

**COLE:** Vela Supply has one hundred million of ARR, negative four million of LTM EBITDA, and forty million of net debt. It grows twenty-eight percent with one hundred eighteen percent NRR. Aggregating Vela with profitable companies makes the fund ratio look calculable. It does not give Vela positive EBITDA.

**ASHA:** Aegis Control has seventy million of ARR, fourteen million of LTM EBITDA, and sixty-five million of net debt. It grows fifteen percent, retains well, and earns an eighty-one percent gross margin. Its main asset is not an average. It is customer trust in regulated industrial environments.

**COLE:** The seductive metric is the eight point nine times. It creates the feeling of one lever attached to one machine. In reality, Keystone cannot use Aegis cash to cure a covenant. Vela cannot borrow Keystone EBITDA. Aegis customers did not consent to put their telemetry into a portfolio data lake.

**ASHA:** Portfolio reporting is still useful. It shows concentration, reserve consumption, aggregate exposure, and where operating attention sits. The error is moving from “these companies share an owner” to “these companies share economics.” Ownership creates governance rights. It does not erase legal entities.

### THREE ASSETS, THREE JOBS

**COLE:** Start with Keystone. It sells claims software plus managed operations to regional insurers. Its AI workflow extracts and organizes evidence, drafts a triage rationale, and routes exceptions. It cannot make an unreviewed coverage, reserve, or payment decision.

**ASHA:** Keystone’s problem is not a shortage of pilots. Twelve teams can produce twelve demos while the company loses customers. ARR is larger than software revenue because managed operations are included. That makes a clean comparison with pure SaaS dangerous. The operating job is to fix retention, simplify the product line, restore delivery discipline, and protect liquidity.

**COLE:** Martin Vale, Keystone’s CEO, wants fifteen million dollars. He proposes a claims intelligence platform, one customer data model, and an automated adjudication roadmap. His deck says the addressable workflow is four times larger than the current product.

**ASHA:** His evidence says something narrower. Two insurers will test evidence assembly. Neither has approved automated coverage. Implementation queues are late. The highest-risk customers are asking for service reliability, not a platform vision. Martin’s ambition may eventually be right. It is not the next capital gate.

**COLE:** Now Vela. It orchestrates orders, suppliers, substitutions, approvals, and exceptions for manufacturers and distributors. Its agent can gather context, draft a resolution, and eventually execute low-risk reversible actions inside monetary and supplier-policy limits.

**ASHA:** Vela is the scale case. Growth and retention are strong. Gross margin is seventy-six percent. The company is also EBITDA-negative, dependent on founder Lina Park, and tempted to hire ahead of every opportunity. The capital question is not whether growth is good. It is whether each dollar expands a repeatable motion or purchases complexity.

**COLE:** Lina asks for eighteen million dollars. Eight for three bounded workflow proofs, six for go-to-market capacity, and four for a shared portfolio engineering team that reports to her because Vela has the strongest product organization.

**ASHA:** The first two requests may be connected to evidence. The third confuses competence with authority. Lina can lead a guild. She should not own Keystone’s claims ontology or Aegis’s security roadmap. A shared team controlled by the strongest company is still a local team with portfolio access.

**COLE:** Aegis assembles cyber, safety, and audit evidence for industrial operators. Its product can triage findings and propose remediation. It cannot change an industrial control system. Omar Haddad, the CEO, argues that the firm’s control environment is part of the product.

**ASHA:** He is right. Aegis is the fortify case. It has good economics and credible growth, but one avoidable trust failure could destroy more value than a faster feature cycle creates. Omar asks for nine million dollars for model evaluation, machine identity, audit evidence, and a portfolio telemetry service.

**COLE:** Again, the request mixes local necessity with a shared hypothesis. Aegis needs its controls. The portfolio may benefit from a common incident schema or a gateway contract. That does not mean Aegis should host every company’s telemetry.

**ASHA:** Three CEOs have requested forty-two million dollars. The investment committee has a forty-five-million-dollar common-platform proposal beside them. If Kevin funds the requests as written, he has not allocated capital. He has added decks.

### FUND CASH IS NOT COMPANY CASH

**COLE:** Let’s separate four ledgers. First, company operating cash. That pays employees, vendors, interest, and local investment. Second, company debt. Each borrower has its own documents and claim on cash flows. Third, sponsor equity already invested. That is seven hundred fifty million of paid-in capital.

**ASHA:** Fourth, uncalled fund capital. Two hundred fifty million remains committed by investors but has not been drawn. It is not EBITDA, free cash flow, or a casual contingency line. Calling it changes fund liquidity and the timing of investor cash flows. Allocating it to one company does not make it available to the others.

**COLE:** A reserve is an option to respond to uncertainty. It can fund a proven growth loop, cure a downside, support an acquisition, or protect trust after an incident. Spend it early on an architecture hypothesis and the option disappears before the evidence arrives.

**ASHA:** That does not mean “never invest.” An unused reserve does not create enterprise value by itself. The discipline is to price the option you give up. What future decision becomes impossible if this call is wrong? How much new information will the first tranche buy? Can the investment stop without stranding customers?

**COLE:** Northstar’s minimum reserve objective is one hundred eighty million dollars through the season. That number is not a universal private-equity rule. It is a case-specific guardrail based on the fund’s remaining ownership plan and plausible follow-on needs.

**ASHA:** So Kevin begins with two hundred fifty million uncalled and a promise to preserve at least one hundred eighty. Seventy million is the maximum cumulative call envelope under the canonical plan. It is not today’s budget. Each call still needs a decision memo.

**COLE:** Concentration matters too. Keystone holds one hundred eighty million of the fund’s two hundred eighty-five million of company net debt. That is sixty-three percent. It also has the weakest retention and highest immediate leverage pressure. The fund cannot average away that concentration.

**ASHA:** Vela holds the strongest organic momentum and the largest initial sponsor equity check, three hundred million. Aegis holds the smallest equity check, one hundred ninety million, but a trust event could be nonlinear. Operating attention should follow exposure and decision urgency, not equal airtime.

### WHAT ACTUALLY TRANSFERS

**COLE:** The investment committee keeps returning to the phrase “common flywheel.” Let’s replace it with a transferability test. A capability is potentially shared only if its inputs, outputs, control assumptions, customer, economics, and exit path survive a second company’s adoption.

**ASHA:** Start with model and vendor procurement. Three companies negotiating compatible gateway terms can create leverage. The shared object is the contract pattern and commercial volume, not customer prompts or retrieved data. Each company still chooses approved models inside its product and legal constraints.

**COLE:** Minimum evaluation and incident schemas can transfer. Every company can record model version, workflow, decision class, outcome, reviewer, severity, and response time. The thresholds should remain local. A false positive in order substitution is not equivalent to a false positive in industrial cyber evidence.

**ASHA:** Telemetry interfaces can transfer. The portfolio can require that each system emits agreed control events. The event data stays within the company’s boundary unless an explicit purpose and legal basis permit aggregation. Interface standardization is not a license to centralize raw data.

**COLE:** Benefit-accounting rules can transfer. Baseline, owner, target, timing, cost, confidence, evidence, and finance sign-off mean the same thing at all three companies. The benefit itself remains in the company that created it. A dollar saved at Keystone cannot also appear as a portfolio-platform saving.

**ASHA:** Specialist guilds can transfer knowledge. A security engineer at Aegis can teach threat modeling. A Vela product leader can teach exception-workflow design. A guild advises, reviews, and improves standards. It does not take the local executive’s decision right by stealth.

**COLE:** Customer data does not transfer by default. Tenant identity does not transfer. Permissions, ontology, retrieval indexes, workflow actions, roadmap, P&L, legal basis, and kill authority remain company-local. The reason is not organizational taste. Those are the objects that bind a product to its customer promise and risk.

**ASHA:** “Reusable” therefore remains a hypothesis until the second company adopts a component without importing the first company’s assumptions. The first implementation is local proof. The second is transfer proof. Only then can the fund discuss scale economics.

### THE ECONOMICS OF A SHARED CAPABILITY

**COLE:** A shared service needs five things before code. A customer, an adoption test, an allocation rule, a service owner, and an exit path. Without them, central cost appears while local teams keep doing the work.

**ASHA:** The customer is a named company leader who chooses the service over a credible alternative. “The portfolio” is not a customer. Kevin may mandate a minimum control, but the operating service still needs someone accountable for its usefulness.

**COLE:** The adoption test says what behavior proves demand. A signed architecture slide is not adoption. A company routing a production workload through the gateway, using the incident schema, and accepting the service level is adoption.

**ASHA:** The allocation rule answers who pays. Fixed equal shares are simple but punish smaller users. Pure usage pricing is fairer but may underfund fixed controls. A sensible early design can place the minimum control layer at the fund, then charge variable vendor usage to each company. The exact rule must be visible.

**COLE:** The service owner has authority over reliability, vendor management, and support. The owner does not control local product prioritization. Service ownership and roadmap ownership are different roles.

**ASHA:** The exit path matters because shared teams become permanent through inertia. If only one company adopts, can the service return to that company? Can contracts be assigned at sale? Can a buyer operate independently? A capability that makes the companies unsellable separately has a hidden negative option value.

**COLE:** Here is the proposed forty-five-million-dollar platform. Twelve million for a data lake. Eleven for a common agent runtime. Nine for a shared ontology. Seven for central engineering. Six for portfolio analytics. It assumes all three companies adopt within twelve months.

**ASHA:** The economics fail before the architecture does. No second-company adoption evidence. No chargeback rule. No local customer owner. No stranded-cost plan. No data-rights map. No proof that a common ontology can represent claims, supplier exceptions, and industrial controls without becoming meaningless.

**COLE:** And the benefit forecast double-counts. Each company has included its local productivity claim. The platform team has included the same claims as shared-service value. Forty-five million of cost is real. The reported benefit is not yet additive.

**ASHA:** OpenAI’s July 14, 2026 guidance on managing AI investments argues for treating capabilities as a portfolio of staged investments rather than mistaking isolated use cases for transformation. That is a useful design prompt, not evidence that this fictional fund should centralize anything.

**COLE:** Blackstone’s May 7, 2026 discussion of AI at scale similarly emphasizes operating systems around adoption and value. It describes one practitioner’s approach. It does not provide a transferability result for these three companies or a benchmark Kevin can paste into the memo.

### CONFLICTING EVIDENCE

**ASHA:** The case against common infrastructure is strong. Now the uncomfortable evidence for it. All three companies are separately negotiating access to the same two model families. All three lack an agreed severe-incident definition. All three have built slightly different benefit spreadsheets. And the same four specialists are being recruited by each company.

**COLE:** Pure decentralization duplicates scarce work. Vela can outbid Keystone for talent. Aegis can design the best control schema and keep it local. Vendor terms fragment. The fund then owns three inconsistent ways to discover the same class of failure.

**ASHA:** The case for Vela’s larger growth request is also strong. Its retention and growth support investment. But its negative EBITDA and founder dependence mean speed can outrun integration. The company can add ARR while consuming cash and increasing key-person risk.

**COLE:** The case for directing most capital to Keystone is emotionally strong. It has the largest debt and the visible fire. But capital cannot repair a management system that has not accepted the diagnosis. Funding a broad platform before product simplification would increase fixed cost around a shrinking base.

**ASHA:** The case for harvesting Aegis is financially neat. It is profitable, growing, and trusted. Reduce investment, pay down debt, and use the cash buffer. But starving control investment at the company whose product promise is control can manufacture the downside the harvest plan claims to avoid.

**COLE:** Conflicting evidence is not noise to remove. It is why the allocation must be asymmetric. The question is which constraint defines each mandate and which small common investment reduces duplicated risk without erasing local accountability.

### STAGE GATES AND OPERATING-PARTNER RIGHTS

**ASHA:** “Stage it” is easy advice and often meaningless. A stage gate needs a decision, an evidence package, a date, an owner, and a consequence. Otherwise the first tranche becomes the down payment on a project nobody is willing to stop.

**COLE:** Keystone’s first gate is not model accuracy in a lab. It is whether two named insurers activate evidence assembly inside the existing claims process, whether complete packets arrive faster without a rise in material omission, and whether the workflow helps the retention plan rather than distracting from it.

**ASHA:** Finance also needs the cost of proof. Dedicated product labor, model usage, implementation, review, and support all enter the denominator. If Keystone removes fifteen minutes of handling but adds twenty minutes of exception review, the demo worked and the service did not.

**COLE:** The release after that gate is not automatically more equity. Keystone first identifies which operating cost, customer risk, or contracted expansion changes. If the proof only establishes usability, the next decision may be redesign rather than scale.

**ASHA:** Vela’s first gate combines product and commercial evidence. Three bounded workflows must reach activated customers, stay inside quality and latency guardrails, and show that implementation capacity can absorb the next cohort. Signed pipeline alone cannot release growth hiring.

**COLE:** Vela’s second gate asks whether contribution survives full workload cost. Model calls, retrieval, orchestration, exception handling, customer success, and incremental sales capacity all count. A high gross-margin company can still buy low-quality ARR if activation cost and support complexity are hidden.

**ASHA:** Aegis’s gate is deliberately asymmetric. It does not wait for a security incident to prove control value. Omar must demonstrate separated machine identities, tested revocation, complete audit evidence, and a customer-accepted boundary on proposed remediation. Prevention evidence is a control test, not an invented loss-avoidance number.

**COLE:** The common layer has its own gates. Gate one: a named company uses the component. Gate two: a second company adopts it without changing the first company’s local assumptions into portfolio law. Gate three: the service has an allocation rule and exit path. Only then does “shared” become an operating fact.

**ASHA:** What rights does Kevin need? Information rights to the agreed ledger and evidence. Challenge rights at the capital gate. The right to recommend or withhold fund equity. The right to require minimum controls that protect the owner’s exposure. And board rights defined in the investment documents.

**COLE:** What rights does he not acquire by job title? The right to order a product release, move customer data, borrow from another company, override a CEO outside governance, or insert a central team into every decision. An operating partner works through owners and boards. He does not become an unofficial group CEO.

**ASHA:** This distinction matters most when the evidence is mixed. Kevin can require Lina to show activation economics before more fund capital. He cannot claim that this gives him daily control of Vela’s roadmap. He can require Aegis to meet an incident standard. He cannot make Omar transfer customer telemetry to Keystone.

**COLE:** Escalation should be designed before disagreement. A missed local target goes to the company operating review. A request for additional equity goes to the fund allocation gate. A breach of a minimum control goes to the company CEO, relevant board, and portfolio incident path. Different problems travel through different authorities.

**ASHA:** The quarterly portfolio review therefore has two pages. Page one compares mandates, capital, evidence, and reserve. Page two lists only cross-company dependencies and minimum controls. It does not merge the operating reviews into one giant scorecard where Vela’s growth can turn Keystone green.

**COLE:** Chargebacks follow the same principle. During proof, the fund can pay the small fixed cost of the common contract and schema. Company-specific model usage, implementation, and workflow support remain local. After transfer proof, a transparent fixed-plus-usage charge can replace subsidy.

**ASHA:** Why not charge everything immediately? Because a proof layer creates learning for the owner as well as service for the company. Why not subsidize forever? Because subsidy hides demand and makes divestiture harder. The allocation rule should change when the evidence changes.

**COLE:** Finally, record reversibility. Vendor terms should be assignable or separable. Telemetry interfaces should work without the central service. Company identities should never depend on a fund-wide administrator. Shared expertise should leave documentation behind. The fund owns options, not an empire.

### THREE CREDIBLE CHOICES

**ASHA:** Choice one: one playbook, one reserve. Allocate five million to each company now and begin the forty-five-million common platform. It maximizes apparent fairness and creates a visible portfolio program. It also ignores different starting economics and consumes sixty million before transfer is proven.

**COLE:** Choice two: protect the fund by harvesting Aegis and Vela, direct operating cash toward debt, and reserve new equity for Keystone. It prioritizes liquidity and the largest downside. It may underinvest in the two companies where controlled investment has the best evidence.

**ASHA:** Choice three: fix Keystone, scale Vela, fortify Aegis, and call only fifteen million now. Allocate three million to Keystone, eight to Vela, and four to Aegis. Fund common proof contracts, procurement, minimum controls, interfaces, and benefit rules. Refuse the data lake and the platform promise.

**COLE:** All three are credible because each protects something real: fairness and speed, fund liquidity, or asymmetric value creation. Your job is not to identify the option with no downside. It is to choose the downside you can bound and reverse.

**ASHA:** Kevin, choose before we make the canonical call. Write one sentence for the mandate of each company. Then write the maximum Q1 call, the uncalled reserve after the call, and the evidence required before a second tranche.
