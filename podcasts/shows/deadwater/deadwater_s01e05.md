[PAUSE]

The lot she identifies is the same lot.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Third: the cell cluster. The pattern of burner phone activity connecting multiple victims to an unregistered handset whose hardware signature appears most frequently in two geographic areas: the barrier island corridor and a residential cluster fifteen miles away in Nassau County. The cluster includes Massapequa Park.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] None of this is conclusive individually. Together it is a coherent picture with a specific person at its center. Coherent enough to justify the next step. Not enough to make an arrest. Not even close to enough. But enough to look.

[MARSH] The surveillance decision required a specific kind of patience. Not the endurance patience of the long investigation - the patience of continuing to work something over years without resolution. This is precision patience. The patience of a surgeon. Every action has to be exactly right, because if you make a mistake you alert the subject. And alerting Roland Voss, at this point, was not an option I was willing to entertain.

[NARRATOR] The warrant applications for electronic surveillance begin in the second week of the task force operation. DA Frank Tully handles them personally and argues each one before a federal magistrate who reviews them carefully and returns two of the initial applications with detailed questions requiring additional documentation.

[TULLY] The probable cause in those early applications was real and solid but it was all circumstantial and it was old. A parking citation, a witness account, a cell cluster. None of those things individually constitute direct evidence of the crimes. Together they establish a reasonable basis for investigation - which is the legal standard for a surveillance warrant - but the magistrate needed to see that basis demonstrated rigorously. I was glad of the scrutiny. Surveillance warrants argued rigorously in front of a demanding magistrate produce evidence that holds up later, when the defense attorney looks for the seams.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The first surveillance warrants are granted in the third week of March 2022. They authorize the monitoring of cell tower data for devices registered to Voss's home and business addresses, E-ZPass transponder records from his highway travel, and security camera footage from public locations on his regular commute route.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] There are no unmarked vehicles outside his house. No physical follows on his commute. No human presence in his life that could register as anything different from the ordinary. The task force is watching him through the data he generates simply by existing in the modern world.

[DR. PARK] The most valuable surveillance data is rarely dramatic. It is not intercepted calls about crimes. It is the pattern data - the quiet, consistent record of where a person is and when and for how long. That data, built over months, tells you more about a person's actual behavior than anything they consciously share with you. Behavior is honest in a way that speech rarely is.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The pattern that emerges from Roland Voss's data in the first month of surveillance is entirely consistent with the open-source profile. Five days a week on the commuter train, office arrival and departure logged by building security at consistent times, weekends mostly local with activities matching the known civic and family commitments. The pattern of a completely ordinary person living a completely ordinary life.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And then the exceptions.

[DR. PARK] What we are looking for, in pattern analysis, is the deviation. The thing that does not fit the established rhythm. The drive that does not match the known routes. The time the pattern has a gap. Because deviations are where the real information lives. The ordinary pattern tells you who someone presents as. The deviations tell you who they actually are.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In the second month of surveillance, Dr. Park flags something to Marsh. On three occasions in the prior calendar year - three separate dates spread across four months - Roland Voss's E-ZPass transponder records show travel on the south shore barrier island highway. Not in the direction of Manhattan. Not on routes consistent with any known personal or professional activity. South. Toward the ocean.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] All three dates are winter dates. All three are weekday mornings, before his normal commute begins. All three place him on the same stretch of road where the bodies were found.

[MARSH] He's still going back. After twelve years. After the bodies being found, after the media attention, after the investigations, after the anniversaries and the families in the news and everything else. He is still driving to that road. That told me something very specific about who we were dealing with. This is not someone who is haunted by guilt and cannot stay away. This is someone for whom that place is, in some deep and specific way, their own territory. And they go back to their territory the way certain animals go back to theirs.

[REESE] The E-ZPass data changed how I understood him. Not the evidence it provided for the case - though that was significant. What it changed was my psychological model of who he was. I had been thinking of him, unconsciously, as someone in a state of suppression. Someone who was managing his behavior under pressure. What the return visits showed me was that he was not managing under pressure. He was living in comfort. He felt entirely safe. And that safety had made him complacent in a way that was going to cost him.

[NARRATOR] The cell tower analysis, run with the full resources of the task force's digital forensics team, confirms and extends what the earlier investigation had found. The pattern of burner phone activity is clear, the hardware signature is consistent across years of victim contact events, and the tower clustering in the Massapequa Park residential area is precise.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] More specifically: the handset is most frequently active within a radius of approximately one and a half miles centered on the block where Roland Voss's house is located.

[DR. PARK] The statistical probability of that overlap occurring by coincidence is small enough that we do not need to present it as impossible - we present it as extraordinarily unlikely. We run the numbers multiple ways. The result is consistent across all models. This phone is being operated by someone who lives in Voss's neighborhood and who makes regular trips to the barrier island corridor. Combined with everything else we have, that picture has only one person in it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] But this is not yet enough. Not for the direct evidence standard. They need his DNA. They need to tie him physically, irreducibly, to the crime scenes. And for that they need a sample.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They need him to discard something.

[MARSH] I told the team we were going to wait for the right opportunity. No shortcuts. No contact with him, no interaction, nothing that could put him on alert. We were going to continue the surveillance until he gave us what we needed voluntarily, without knowing he was giving it. He had been careful for twenty years. We were going to be more patient than he was. We had the resources and we had the time and we had twelve years of evidence behind us. We could wait.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The genealogical DNA analysis runs parallel to the cell and E-ZPass work. Dr. Park begins the extraction from the hair sample in April 2022. The extraction takes two weeks - slow, careful, each step documented with the forensic rigor that will eventually need to survive legal challenge in a courtroom.

[DR. PARK] The sample is in exceptional condition. Twelve years of correct storage has preserved the genetic material with almost no degradation. When I complete the extraction and look at the profile quality, I feel something I would not describe as professional, exactly. It is more personal than that. I have been waiting twelve years to ask this hair a question. It is ready to answer.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The genealogical analysis - building a family tree from the database matches to the hair's genetic profile and tracing it forward to the unknown contributor - takes six weeks. At the end of six weeks, Dr. Park has a short list of candidates. Two men who sit at the right intersection of family branches. One lives on the opposite coast. The other lives in Massapequa Park.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She brings the results to Carol Marsh and puts them on the desk. Marsh looks at the name at the top of the list.

[MARSH] I looked at it for a moment. And I said: now we get the DNA. Now we make it certain.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They are waiting for him to throw something away. They are watching every movement he makes. They are patient in a way that twelve years of practice has made into something almost like a skill.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is going to give them what they need. He just doesn't know it yet.

## Extended Act: The Architecture of Surveillance

[NARRATOR] The surveillance operation that the task force runs on Roland Voss between February and July 2022 is one of the most carefully constructed investigative operations I have encountered in twenty-two years of covering law enforcement. The care is not accidental. It is the product of twelve years of knowing exactly what kind of person they are dealing with and understanding that a single error - a single moment of visible attention directed at him - could produce a flight response in someone who had the resources and the self-discipline to make himself genuinely difficult to find.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese describes the operational challenge this way.

[REESE] The person you are surveilling has spent twenty years successfully evading an investigation that, at certain points, had hundreds of people working it. He has done this not through luck but through extreme behavioral discipline. He maintains rigid routines that make him predictable. He does not make spontaneous decisions that could expose him. He monitors the investigation constantly and adjusts his behavior when the investigation appears to be intensifying. He is, in short, one of the most operationally aware subjects I have ever worked against.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] To surveil someone like that without detection, you cannot do anything that deviates from the normal patterns of the world around him. You cannot park a car in front of his house. You cannot follow him on the train. You cannot make any change to his environment that his trained attention might register. You have to be invisible in a way that is more complete than surveillance usually requires.

[NARRATOR] The solution is data. Pure, impersonal, machine-generated data that Voss himself produces and that the task force collects without any physical presence in his life.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The E-ZPass system records every time his vehicle's transponder passes through a toll point on the highway network. Every time. The records are automatic, legally accessible through appropriate process, and completely invisible to him. Every commute to Manhattan. Every weekend drive. Every early-morning trip to the south shore barrier island corridor. All of it in the data. All of it waiting to be assembled into a picture.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The commuter rail system records every use of his transit card. When he boards. Which station. Which train. The records go back years, available through legal process. Six months of commute data assembles a picture of a man of extraordinary routine. Same station, same time, same train, five days a week for months on end. The monotony of it is its own kind of information.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The cell tower records show every time his registered phone connects to the network. Every tower it pings. The approximate location that implies. Combined with the E-ZPass records, the cell data produces a near-complete spatial portrait of his daily movement - where he is, within a reasonable approximation, at almost every moment of the day.

[DR. PARK] What we are building is a behavioral baseline. The pattern of his ordinary life, documented with enough precision that the deviations from that pattern become clearly visible. The ordinary pattern is not interesting to us except as context. The interesting data is the exception. And the exceptions, when we start to see them, are very specific.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Three early-morning drives to the barrier island corridor in the year before the task force formed, visible in the E-ZPass records. A pattern of weekday absences from the normal commute data that cluster around specific dates in winter months. And the burner phone handset, active in the corridor on several of those dates, pinging the same towers that Voss's registered device is pinging.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Not the same device. The burner is a different handset - smaller, prepaid, no registered identity. But it is in the same place as Voss's registered phone, within minutes of the same time, on multiple occasions across multiple years. That overlap is not coincidence. That overlap is a person who has two phones and who uses one of them in a specific location for specific purposes.

[MARSH] When the cell overlap analysis came in from the digital forensics team, I laid it on the table next to the E-ZPass route data and the genealogical candidate list and I looked at all three of them together. And I thought: we have him. Not in a legal sense - not yet. In the investigative sense. The picture is complete. The picture has only one possible person in it. Now the job is to make the legal case that reflects what the picture shows.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] DA Frank Tully is in the task force operations center twice a week throughout the surveillance period. He is not there to supervise the investigation - that is Marsh's domain. He is there to make sure that every piece of evidence being developed is being developed in a way that will be legally unassailable.

[TULLY] My role in the surveillance phase is to be the person asking the hardest questions the defense will eventually ask, and to ask them early enough that the answers can be built into the evidence collection process rather than retrofitted after the fact. If I am going to stand up in court and argue that this evidence is valid and this conclusion is warranted, I need to understand every link in the chain between the evidence and the conclusion. Every link. No assumptions. No shortcuts.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] I pushed back on the task force several times during that period. Not because I doubted the investigation - I didn't, not for a moment - but because the defense attorney in this case was going to be very good, and I was not going to hand them anything to work with. Every warrant application bulletproof. Every chain of custody documented to a standard that leaves no room for argument. Every piece of digital evidence accompanied by the technical affidavits that explain to a judge exactly how it was collected and what it means.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] Carol Marsh's team met that standard. Completely. Every time I pushed back, they addressed the issue and came back stronger. The case they built during those five months of surveillance is the cleanest investigative case file I have seen in nineteen years of prosecution. That is not an accident. It is the result of twelve years of waiting for the right resources being applied by people who understood exactly what they were doing.

[NARRATOR] The warrant applications are a specific and significant piece of the legal architecture. Each one is a legal document arguing before a federal magistrate that the evidence assembled justifies the surveillance being requested. The magistrate reviews them and pushes back on several, requiring additional documentation and more precise statements of the probable cause. This is adversarial in the best sense - a process that stress-tests the investigative logic before it gets to a courtroom.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The magistrate ultimately approves each application. The approvals are narrow and specific - exactly what was applied for, nothing more. This narrowness is deliberate. A surveillance warrant that is too broad is a vulnerability. A warrant that is precisely scoped to the specific evidence being sought is harder to challenge.

[TULLY] We were thinking about the defense from day one of the task force operation. Not defensively - we were not letting fear of challenge shape what we investigated. But we were building the case for the courtroom as we built the investigation. Those two things were running in parallel from the first week. That is how you build a case that holds together under the scrutiny it will eventually receive.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] While the digital surveillance builds its picture, the task force archive review continues. Three detectives spending their days reading twelve years of case records, looking for what was logged and not followed, what was started and abandoned, what was seen and not understood.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The detective who finds the two critical documents - the parking citation and the tip log entry from Priscilla's call - is a twelve-year veteran named Julia Saenz, who joined the task force on Marsh's specific request. Saenz specializes in archive analysis. She has an extraordinary memory for case document structure and an ability to hold complex evidentiary timelines in her head simultaneously that Marsh describes as "the best analytical mind I have ever worked with."

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Saenz finds the documents on a Wednesday afternoon in the fourth week of the archive review. She has been working through a section of the case management archive that was not digitally indexed - physical files requiring manual review, page by page. The parking citation is in a folder labeled "Miscellaneous Vehicle Records, Year 1." The tip log entry from Priscilla's call is on the page immediately following it, filed in the same folder apparently by accident during an archive reorganization years earlier.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She brings both documents to Carol Marsh's office. She puts them on the desk side by side without saying anything. She has been in the task force for four weeks. She knows the case well enough to know what she is looking at.

[MARSH] She put those two pages on my desk and I looked at them and I looked at her and I said: find me everything there is on Roland T. Voss. All of it. Today.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Twenty-two minutes later, Saenz comes back with a three-page printout. The public record profile. She has already highlighted three things: the Massapequa Park residential address, the Manhattan business address matching the parking citation, and the letter to the editor from 2003 about the access road lighting.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Marsh reads the letter. She reads it twice.

[MARSH] There you are.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Twelve years. Six words. Not dramatic - just certain. The certainty of someone who has been looking for a very long time and finally has something real in front of them.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They still need to prove it. But they know where to look now. And they know how to look. And for the first time in twelve years, they have everything they need to do it properly.

[PAUSE]

## Extended Act: The Legal Architecture

[NARRATOR] The task force surveillance operation is built on a legal architecture that Frank Tully describes as the most carefully constructed set of warrant applications he has ever submitted in nineteen years as a prosecutor. The architecture matters not just procedurally - it shapes what evidence is obtainable and how it can be used. It is also the thing that the defense will try hardest to dismantle.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Every warrant application in the case is written by Tully personally, with input from the task force investigators on the specific evidence being sought and from a federal legal specialist on electronic surveillance who has experience with the particular legal questions that digital forensics raises. Each application is structured to answer, in advance, the objections that a sophisticated defense attorney would raise.

[TULLY] I was thinking about Mitchell Graves from the beginning. Not because I knew he would be the defense attorney specifically, but because I knew whoever it was would be very good. A case like this, with this much at stake, with a defendant who has the professional success and the financial resources that Voss has - the defense is going to be excellent. My job was to make the case against which that excellence would be deployed as airtight as I know how to make it.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] The E-ZPass records are legally straightforward. Those are commercial records, accessible through appropriate legal process, with well-established precedent on their admissibility. The cell tower records are somewhat more complex - there are jurisdiction-specific rules about how tower data can be obtained and used that require careful navigation. The forensic genealogy is the most complex of all, for reasons that the defense makes very clear in its challenge.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The genealogical DNA challenge is argued over a period of months in what becomes known as the Frye hearing - a process by which the court determines whether a scientific methodology meets the standard of general acceptance in the relevant scientific community required for admissibility. The hearing requires Dr. Park and several of her colleagues in the forensic genealogy field to testify extensively about the methodology, its development, its accuracy rates, its limitations, and its consistency with established principles of genetics and genealogical analysis.

[DR. PARK] The Frye hearing was, professionally, one of the most rigorous examinations of my work that I have ever undergone. The defense retained their own experts who challenged the methodology on specific technical grounds. I respected the challenge - the questions they raised were real scientific questions, not bad-faith objections. The methodology is newer than most forensic DNA techniques and it deserves to be scrutinized carefully.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] My testimony took three days. I was cross-examined for two of those days by an attorney who had clearly spent months educating himself in the technical literature. He asked good questions. The answers were clear. The methodology is sound, the accuracy rates are well-documented, the legal process we followed was appropriate.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] The court agreed. The evidence is admitted.

[NARRATOR] The ruling is issued in September 2025, two and a half years after the arrest. It is comprehensive and carefully reasoned, addressing both the scientific validity of the methodology and the constitutional questions about consent and privacy. The ruling holds that the investigative process in this case met the legal standard for admissibility - that the warrants were properly obtained, the methodology scientifically valid, and the privacy interests balanced appropriately against the investigative need.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Graves files a notice of intent to appeal the admissibility ruling. He is still within his rights to do so even as the plea negotiations proceed. The appeal notice is, in effect, a negotiating position - a signal that if the case goes to trial, the admissibility question will continue to be contested.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The plea agreement resolves the question by making it moot. Voss pleads guilty. The constitutional question of forensic genealogy admissibility remains active in the legal landscape, to be resolved by future cases. The Voss case contributes to that landscape but does not fully settle it.

[TULLY] The legal questions around forensic genealogy are going to be litigated for years. That is appropriate - important legal questions should be litigated carefully. What I am confident of is that in this specific case, with this specific warrant process and this specific methodology, the evidence was valid and the process was proper. The court agreed. That matters.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Among the things the task force finds in the warrant-authorized review of Voss's professional records: three separate projects, in the years following the discovery of the bodies, that involved site work near the barrier island corridor. All three are legitimate projects - commercial renovations, permit filings, the ordinary professional work of an architect with a practice that includes work in that geographic area.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] All three involve visits to the corridor. All three are in the relevant files as explained professional activity. They provide, in effect, a cover story for his presence in the area in the years after the investigation became public - professional reasons to be near the barrier island that were real and documentable and that, if anyone had asked, would have explained why an architect from Massapequa Park was driving the south shore highway.

[REESE] When we found the professional file evidence, the first thing I thought was: of course. Of course he made sure there was a legitimate reason to be there. He was not going to stop going back to that road, but he was going to make sure that if anyone ever noticed him there, there was a professional explanation. He arranged the professional explanation before he needed it.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That is extraordinary foresight. Not moral foresight - I am not interested in dignifying it with that word. Operational foresight. The foresight of someone who has been thinking about risk management in this specific way for long enough that it has become automatic. It is just what he does. He sees a potential vulnerability and he creates a cover for it before the vulnerability is even identified.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The professional projects are real. The permit applications are real. The client relationships are real. He did not fake them. He created them, through his professional connections and his architectural practice, specifically to serve a purpose that his clients never knew about and that his colleagues never suspected.

[NARRATOR] He is building things professionally and building something else privately, using the same skills, in the same geographic area, for two decades.

[PAUSE]
