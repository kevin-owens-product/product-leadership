### Who Schülerhilfe is

**NORA:** Let's start with the basics. Where is it, and how big?

**JAMES:** It's based in Gelsenkirchen, in the Ruhr area of western Germany. It was founded in 1974, so it celebrated fifty years in 2024. The name roughly means "help for pupils".

**NORA:** And the size?

**JAMES:** Here the numbers vary a little depending on the source and the year. The company's own fiftieth anniversary press release, from January 2024, said one thousand and sixty-four locations. A law firm's note from 2023 said around eleven hundred. Levine Leichtman, in October 2024, said about twelve hundred. And the previous owner, Oakley Capital, gave a split of seven hundred and twenty company-owned and four hundred and thirty-one franchise centres, which adds up to about eleven hundred and fifty. So call it eleven to twelve hundred centres.

**NORA:** And pupils?

**JAMES:** The company says more than a hundred and twenty-five thousand pupils a year. Oakley's page said over a hundred and forty thousand, but that page is undated. So again, a range, and both are company or owner figures.

**NORA:** What about revenue and staff?

**JAMES:** Not public for recent years. The only revenue figure we found is from 2016, before the previous owner bought it: sixty-three point three million euros of revenue and sixteen point six million euros of EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a common measure of operating profit. That's ten years old, so it tells you only that the business was solidly profitable then. We didn't find headcount.

**NORA:** Who runs it?

**JAMES:** The chief executive is Dieter Werkhausen. He's notable because he invested alongside the previous owner in 2017 and again alongside Levine Leichtman in 2024, so he has personal money in the business. We didn't find a named technology or AI executive.

**NORA:** And the company makes some claims about results?

**JAMES:** It does. It says ninety-two percent of pupils improve their grades and over ninety-four percent of customers are very satisfied. Those are the company's own claims and we couldn't verify how they're measured, so treat them as marketing figures.

### Fifty years and three owners

**NORA:** Tell me the ownership story.

**JAMES:** Before 2017, the majority holders were DBAG, which is Deutsche Beteiligungs, a listed German private equity firm, and one of its funds. In May 2017, Oakley Capital, a British private equity firm, bought Schülerhilfe through its third fund, with the chief executive investing alongside. The deal was partly funded by a type of loan called a unitranche from a lender called Alcentra, and Oakley's equity was reported at about forty-two million euros.

**NORA:** Unitranche. Another finance word.

**JAMES:** It just means a single loan that combines what would traditionally be several layers of debt into one, usually from a private lender rather than a bank. It's common in mid-sized buyouts.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** In August 2023, the company refinanced its existing loan, with the German law firm GÖRG advising. The lenders and amount weren't disclosed. Then on the twenty-fourth of October 2024, Levine Leichtman announced it would invest in Schülerhilfe alongside the chief executive and management, with Oakley exiting. Terms weren't disclosed. The private equity firm lists the investment as December 2024, so our reading is an October announcement and December completion, after regulatory approval.

**NORA:** Anything else around the deal?

**JAMES:** One interesting detail. A technology advisory firm, Code and Co, says it advised Levine Leichtman on technology and product due diligence before the investment. That covered IT systems, infrastructure, the technology stack, cybersecurity, the product roadmap, and the team and development processes. The results weren't published, of course. But it tells you the new owner looked hard at the technology side.

**NORA:** And the people involved on the owner's side?

**JAMES:** The announcement named Josh Kaufman, partner and head of Europe, and Matthias Tabbert, managing director and head of the German-speaking region.

**NORA:** So what was the investment thesis?

**JAMES:** In its announcement, Levine Leichtman pointed to three demand drivers: a growing school-age population, a rising share of pupils getting tutoring, and a shift towards institutional providers, meaning organised companies rather than individual tutors. Those are the owner's own arguments, of course.

**NORA:** Any acquisitions since?

**JAMES:** None that we found. So under this owner, it's about two years in, with no add-ons yet and one notable product, KIRA.

**NORA:** Three private equity owners in a row. Is that a good sign or a worrying one?

**JAMES:** It's common for a steady, cash-generating service business, in my view. Each owner sees a different chapter. Fifty years of history and a recognised brand are real assets. Parents who were tutored at Schülerhilfe themselves may now be sending their children. But each new owner also pays a higher price and usually adds debt, so each one needs a growth story. For this owner, the obvious chapter is digital and AI, because that's the thing that has changed most since the last deal.

### How tutoring works in Germany

**NORA:** For listeners outside Germany, explain why tutoring is such a big deal there.

**JAMES:** In general, private tutoring, called Nachhilfe in German, is very common in German-speaking countries. A big part of the reason is the school system. In much of Germany, children are channelled into different types of secondary school at a fairly young age, and grades matter for which track they follow and for later options. So parents feel strong pressure when a child's grades slip.

**NORA:** Are there particular pressure points in the year?

**JAMES:** In general, yes. Report cards, the transition from primary to secondary school, the end of the school year when a pupil might have to repeat a year, and final exams like the Abitur, which is the German school-leaving qualification needed for university. Those are the moments when families look for help. So demand tends to peak around them, and that seasonality shapes how a tutoring business staffs and sells.

**NORA:** And then drops off.

**JAMES:** And then drops off, especially in the summer holidays. That's another reason monthly cancellable contracts are double-edged. Easy to join when the pressure is high, easy to leave when it eases.

**NORA:** How big is the market?

**JAMES:** The German teachers' and education union, the GEW, cites a study from the Hans-Böckler Foundation saying parents spend about four billion euros a year on private tutoring. The year of the study isn't stated in the summary we saw. And the GEW says that two and a half to three billion euros of that goes to unregistered providers.

**NORA:** Meaning?

**JAMES:** Meaning a large part of the market is informal. University students, retired teachers, neighbours. Cash in hand, no company involved. That's important for Schülerhilfe, because its competition isn't just other chains. It's the student down the road charging a lower hourly rate.

**NORA:** And the union is critical of the whole thing.

**JAMES:** Yes. The GEW's framing is about creeping privatisation of education. Its concern, in general terms, is that if families have to pay for tutoring to keep up, children from wealthier families get an advantage. The union notes that tutoring use rises with parents' income and education. That's a social debate, and we're not taking a side on it, but it's part of the context any tutoring company operates in.

**NORA:** It also tells you who the customers are.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Families with the means and motivation to pay. And it suggests a risk: if household budgets are squeezed, tutoring is a discretionary spend that can be cut.

**NORA:** Which brings us to Austria.

**JAMES:** It does. The Vienna Chamber of Labour, known as AK Wien, publishes a regular tutoring barometer. The 2026 edition, with fieldwork from March to May and published in August 2026, estimates the Austrian paid tutoring market at about a hundred and forty-four million euros for the school year 2025 to 2026, down from about a hundred and fifty-three million the year before.

**NORA:** That's a fall of around six percent.

**JAMES:** Right. Nineteen percent of pupils had paid tutoring, and average spend per paying pupil dropped to about seven hundred and twenty euros, from about eight hundred. Only three percent of pupils used paid online tutoring. And interestingly, the survey didn't measure AI tools at all.

**NORA:** So we don't know whether AI is part of the reason for the decline.

**JAMES:** We don't. It could be household budgets, it could be AI, it could be other factors. But the direction matters. In at least one of Schülerhilfe's markets, spending on paid tutoring is shrinking, and families are spending less each.

### Inside the service

**NORA:** How does Schülerhilfe actually work for a family?

**JAMES:** From the company's site, the core offer is tutoring in small groups of three to five pupils, or one to one, either at a local centre or online. Prices start from eight euros seventy-six per lesson, on monthly contracts that can be cancelled. There are free trial lessons to get families started. And there are adult courses as well, which the private equity firm described as a growing part of the offer.

**NORA:** Small groups. Why groups rather than one to one?

**JAMES:** In general, it's an economic model. A tutor teaching four pupils at once can charge each family much less than a private one-to-one tutor, while earning the centre more per hour. And for many pupils, a small group with a tutor circulating works well. They do exercises, the tutor helps whoever is stuck.

**NORA:** Is there a complication in serving all of Germany? It's not one school system, is it?

**JAMES:** Right, and this is general background that matters a lot here. In Germany, education is mainly the responsibility of the sixteen federal states. Curricula, school types, textbooks and exam formats differ from state to state. Austria and Switzerland have their own systems again. So a tutoring company with a national content library has to map its material to many different curricula and exam formats.

**NORA:** That sounds like a lot of maintenance.

**JAMES:** It is, and it's an underrated AI opportunity. Tagging two hundred and thirty-two thousand items by state, school type, grade and topic, spotting gaps, and drafting new exercises aligned to a specific state's exam format, is exactly the kind of structured, high-volume work AI can speed up, with teachers reviewing. And it makes KIRA better, because it can give a pupil in Bavaria practice that matches what a pupil in Bavaria will actually be tested on.

**NORA:** And the monthly cancellable contract?

**JAMES:** That's a big deal, in my view. It lowers the barrier to joining, which helps sales. But it also means a family can leave whenever the immediate crisis passes. A child's grade recovers, the exam is over, and the family cancels. So retention, keeping pupils month after month, is probably one of the most important numbers in the business.

**NORA:** You said owned and franchised centres.

**JAMES:** Yes, roughly seven hundred and twenty owned and four hundred and thirty-one franchised, according to the previous owner. A franchise model lets a network grow with less capital, because the franchisee pays for the local centre and runs it, and the franchisor provides the brand, systems, content and marketing in return for fees. The trade-off is less direct control over quality.

**NORA:** And the online side?

**JAMES:** There's online tutoring, plus what the company calls the Online-LernCenter, an online learning centre. In a 2024 press release, the company said it holds over two hundred and thirty-two thousand worksheets, tasks, interactive tests and videos. That's a substantial content library, and it's the foundation for KIRA.

**NORA:** And it says homework help is free in the app?

**JAMES:** The company site says daily homework help is free in its app. That's an interesting move in itself. It suggests Schülerhilfe has already accepted that basic homework help is becoming free, and is using it as a way to bring families in.

**NORA:** And the adult courses? What are those?

**JAMES:** We didn't find much detail beyond the private equity firm describing adult education as a growing part of the offer. In general, adult learning in Germany covers things like language courses, preparation for qualifications and job-related skills. For Schülerhilfe, the appeal is obvious: the same centres and online platform can be used in the daytime, when school pupils are in class. Centres that are busy in the afternoon and evening often sit empty in the morning.

**NORA:** So adult courses fill the empty hours.

**JAMES:** That's our inference, yes. Better use of fixed rent is always attractive in a centre-based business, and AI-supported language practice is a natural fit there too.

**NORA:** Who are the tutors?

**JAMES:** We didn't find details on Schülerhilfe's tutor workforce. In general, tutoring chains in Germany tend to rely on a mix of students, teachers and other qualified people, often working part time. Recruiting and scheduling those tutors well is a big operational challenge.

### Running a franchise network

**NORA:** I want to spend a bit more time on the franchise side, because I think it shapes everything about how this company can use technology.

**JAMES:** I agree. With roughly four hundred and thirty franchise centres, a large part of the network is run by independent business owners. In general, a franchisee buys the right to use the brand, pays an initial fee and ongoing fees, often linked to revenue, and in return gets the brand, training, marketing, systems and content. We didn't find Schülerhilfe's specific franchise terms, so I'm describing the model in general.

**NORA:** What does a franchisee care about?

**JAMES:** Their own profit, first and foremost. How many pupils they can fill into groups, how much they pay tutors, rent and fees, and how much effort the admin takes. A franchisee is usually a local entrepreneur, sometimes a former teacher, running one or a few centres with a small team.

**NORA:** And what does the franchisor want?

**JAMES:** Growth in the network, consistent quality so the brand stays strong, and fees. The tension is that the franchisor wants standards and data, and the franchisee wants freedom and low overhead. Any new system, including AI tools, sits right in the middle of that tension.

**NORA:** So if head office says, here's a new AI dashboard, use it?

**JAMES:** In my view, it works only if the franchisee sees a clear benefit. More enquiries converted, fewer cancellations, less time on scheduling. If it feels like extra work or monitoring, adoption will be patchy. The best franchise systems treat franchisees as customers of head office.

**NORA:** And the owned centres?

**JAMES:** Those are easier to change, because the company controls them directly. They're a natural place to pilot new tools, prove the benefit, and then offer them to franchisees with evidence. That's the sequence I'd follow.

**NORA:** Is there anything about the mix of owned and franchised that matters to an owner like Levine Leichtman?

**JAMES:** In general, franchise income tends to be more capital-light and steadier, while owned centres give more revenue but carry more cost and risk. A network that can grow through franchising without heavy investment is attractive. And if technology makes franchisees more profitable, it also makes it easier to recruit new ones.

### Competitors

**NORA:** Who does Schülerhilfe compete with?

**JAMES:** The closest rival in physical tutoring is Studienkreis, another German network with more than a thousand centres. And Studienkreis is now owned by GoStudent, the Vienna-based online tutoring company that became one of Europe's best-known education technology unicorns, backed by venture capital. GoStudent bought Studienkreis from a private equity firm called IK Partners, with the deal reported in December 2022.

**NORA:** So the online challenger bought the offline incumbent.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Which means GoStudent now competes with Schülerhilfe on both channels, online and in centres. And with venture backing, it may be able to spend heavily on online marketing.

**NORA:** What's GoStudent's model, in simple terms?

**JAMES:** In general, it started as an online marketplace and platform matching pupils with tutors for one-to-one video lessons, often sold as packages. It grew very fast with heavy marketing and large venture funding rounds, and like many education technology companies that boomed during the pandemic, it has had to adjust since then. Buying Studienkreis gave it a physical presence, which suggests even online-first players think in-person tutoring still matters to many families.

**NORA:** That's an interesting signal in itself.

**JAMES:** I think so. If the online challenger decided it needed physical centres, that's a vote of confidence in the hybrid model Schülerhilfe already has. The fight is now about who runs the better hybrid.

**NORA:** Any AI-native competitors?

**JAMES:** A couple of names came up. Hermify, a German company, positions an AI agent for private tutors, though we only saw it in search results. And there's a US company called Kira Learning, an AI-native platform for schools and teachers that launched in April 2025. That's not the same as Schülerhilfe's KIRA, and the names are easy to confuse, so I want to be clear about that.

**NORA:** And schools themselves?

**JAMES:** Yes. Schools are starting to get AI assistants of their own. For example, a sponsored item on a German teachers' news site in March 2026 described a provider developing AI assistants for teachers and learners, positioned as data-protection compliant. If schools give every pupil a good AI learning assistant, that could reduce demand for paid tutoring, or, more optimistically, it could make tutoring more effective. It's genuinely unclear.

**NORA:** And then the biggest competitor.

**JAMES:** Free general AI, plus the informal tutor down the road. Those two together set the price ceiling for basic help. Schülerhilfe has to offer something neither can.

**NORA:** Which is what?

**JAMES:** In my view, three things. Accountability, meaning a person who knows the child, notices when they're struggling and keeps them going. Structure, meaning a plan tied to the school curriculum and exams. And reassurance for parents, meaning someone they can talk to and progress they can see. A chatbot gives answers. It doesn't, on its own, make a fourteen-year-old show up and do the work.

### KIRA and AI today

**NORA:** Let's talk about KIRA.

**JAMES:** KIRA is Schülerhilfe's AI learning assistant. The product page is labelled "new", but we couldn't find a launch date. It covers maths, German, English, French and Latin, and pupils can upload homework for it to check. Crucially, the page says it's only for Schülerhilfe tutoring customers, and that it's built on the company's vetted Online-LernCenter content.

**NORA:** So it's not a free product for everyone.

**JAMES:** Not as far as we can see. And notice the subject list, by the way. Maths, German, English, French and Latin. Those are the classic subjects where German pupils seek tutoring, and Latin in particular stands out. Latin is still taught at many academic secondary schools, and it's a subject where most parents can't help at home at all.

**NORA:** And where a general chatbot might be surprisingly good, actually.

**JAMES:** Possibly, for translation. But for teaching a fourteen-year-old the grammar step by step, in the way their textbook expects, a curriculum-aligned tool has an edge. That's the general bet behind KIRA.

**NORA:** So, back to access. Can anyone with the app use KIRA, the way they can use the free homework help?

**JAMES:** No. It's a benefit for paying customers. In effect, it extends the tutoring relationship between sessions. That's a smart design choice, in my view, because it positions AI as part of what you get with tutoring, rather than a cheaper substitute for it.

**NORA:** And "vetted content" matters why?

**JAMES:** Because general AI can make mistakes, especially in maths, and can give answers that don't match how the topic is taught in German schools. If KIRA draws on content that has been checked by educators and aligned to the curriculum, it should be more reliable and more useful. That's the theory. We haven't tested it.

**NORA:** There's a bigger debate here, isn't there? About whether AI homework help actually helps children learn.

**JAMES:** There is, and it's very live among educators in general. The worry is simple. If a child photographs a problem and gets the answer, they've finished the homework but learned nothing. The alternative approach is an assistant that behaves more like a good tutor: it asks what the child has tried, gives a hint, checks the next step, and only reveals the full solution when needed.

**NORA:** The Socratic approach.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Asking questions rather than giving answers. Several AI education products have moved in that direction. We don't know exactly how KIRA behaves in detail, beyond checking uploaded homework. But for a tutoring company, the hint-first approach is clearly the right one. Its whole promise is that the child actually improves, not just that the homework gets done.

**NORA:** And parents can tell the difference?

**JAMES:** Eventually, through grades. Which is why a tutoring company has a stronger incentive than a general chatbot to get this right. A general assistant is judged on whether the answer is correct. A tutoring company is judged on whether the child's next test goes better.

**NORA:** Anything else on AI?

**JAMES:** The technology due diligence we mentioned. Beyond that, we found no AI partnerships, AI hires, AI acquisitions or AI budget announcements. So KIRA is the one visible AI product.

**NORA:** How would you rate that, informally?

**JAMES:** I'd say Schülerhilfe is ahead of many traditional service businesses, because it has an AI product in market built on its own content. But it's one product, with no visible AI leadership, and the competitive pressure is intense. It's a good start, not a finished strategy.

### A family's school year, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through a family's year. Invented, not a real customer.

**JAMES:** Okay. Meet the Schneider family in a town near Dortmund. Their son, Leon, is fourteen and in his third year at a Gymnasium, the academic secondary school. In October, his maths grade drops sharply after a test on equations. His parents are worried, because a weak maths grade could affect his path.

**NORA:** What do they do?

**JAMES:** His mother searches online and finds three options: the local Schülerhilfe centre, a GoStudent online tutor, and a university student offering lessons on a neighbourhood noticeboard. She also knows Leon already uses a free AI app for homework, which he insists is enough.

**NORA:** Does she believe him?

**JAMES:** Not entirely, because his grades suggest otherwise. She books a free trial lesson at the Schülerhilfe centre. Leon joins a group of four pupils working on maths twice a week. The tutor, a maths student called Jana, notices quickly that Leon can follow worked examples but doesn't understand why the steps work.

**NORA:** So the AI app was giving him answers without understanding.

**JAMES:** In our invented example, exactly. That's a common worry about AI homework help in general. Jana works through the basics with him. Between sessions, Leon uses KIRA. Because it's built on the same content Jana uses, it gives him practice tasks at the right level and checks his homework, but it's designed, in our hypothetical, to guide him through steps rather than just give the answer.

**NORA:** And the parents?

**JAMES:** In November, his mother gets a short update from the centre on his progress. In December, his next test improves. And this is the critical moment for the business. In January, the immediate crisis is over. The family could cancel the monthly contract.

**NORA:** And many do.

**JAMES:** In general, that's the pattern for crisis-driven tutoring. But in our better version, the centre has a plan. Jana and the centre manager suggest reducing to one session a week, with KIRA keeping practice going, and a focus on preparing for the end-of-year exams. The family stays, at a lower cost, and Leon finishes the year well.

**NORA:** So the AI didn't replace the tutor. It made the relationship cheaper to keep going.

**JAMES:** That's the model I find most promising. A hybrid, where the human provides the accountability and diagnosis, and the AI provides practice and support between sessions, so the family gets more value for the money and stays longer.

**NORA:** Let me push back. Couldn't Leon's mother just cancel and let him use KIRA?

**JAMES:** She can't, because KIRA is only for tutoring customers. And that's the design choice again. The AI is a reason to stay, not a reason to leave.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's talk challenges.

**JAMES:** The first is the one we've been circling. Free or cheap AI homework help could cut the number of paid tutoring hours families buy. Schülerhilfe's own app offers free daily homework help, which tells you the company sees the same thing. The question is whether families still pay for the human part.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Market contraction, at least in Austria. A roughly six percent fall in paid tutoring spending in 2025 to 2026, and lower average spend per pupil, according to AK Wien. We don't have equivalent recent data for Germany. But it suggests pricing power may be weakening.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** A well-funded, hybrid competitor. GoStudent with Studienkreis competes in both online and physical tutoring, with venture capital behind it.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** The cost base. Physical centres need rent and tutors. If demand per centre falls, fixed costs bite. And in a franchise network, franchisees feel it directly, which can create tension with the franchisor.

**NORA:** And data?

**JAMES:** A big one. Schülerhilfe handles data about children: names, grades, learning difficulties, homework. Under European data protection law, the GDPR, children's data deserves special care. Adding AI, where pupils upload homework and ask questions, increases the amount of sensitive data. We didn't find public security certifications or an AI governance statement. For a child-focused brand, a data failure would be very damaging.

**NORA:** Anything about visibility?

**JAMES:** Revenue, margins and staff numbers aren't public for recent years, and we didn't find a named technology leader. That makes it hard to judge from outside how much is being invested in the digital side.

**NORA:** And the tutors?

**JAMES:** In general, finding and keeping good tutors is a constant challenge for tutoring businesses, especially in subjects like maths and in tight labour markets. If AI changes the tutor's role, that also means training tutors to work with AI tools well.

**NORA:** And their quality varies, presumably.

**JAMES:** In any large network, it will. A thousand centres means many hundreds, probably thousands, of tutors, with very different experience. Some will be excellent and some will be learning on the job. One of the quiet benefits of a good AI layer is consistency: every tutor gets the same quality of pupil summary and suggested focus, which raises the floor.

**NORA:** Without turning tutors into robots.

**JAMES:** Exactly. The tutor still decides. But a new tutor on her second week, faced with a pupil she's never met, walks in knowing what that pupil struggled with last time. That's a big help.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Okay, opportunities. You're on the board.

**JAMES:** First and biggest: use AI to improve retention. Monthly cancellable contracts make retention the most sensitive number. KIRA, plus the content library, plus data on each pupil's progress, can keep pupils engaged between sessions and help centres intervene before a family cancels.

**NORA:** How big could that be?

**JAMES:** Here's some simple arithmetic using public figures, not a forecast. If Schülerhilfe serves around a hundred and twenty-five thousand pupils a year, then each one percentage point improvement in annual retention keeps about twelve hundred and fifty more pupils. We don't know the revenue per pupil or the current retention rate, so we can't put a euro figure on it. But for a business like this, retention is where the money is.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Lead conversion, especially for the franchise centres. Parents enquire, often in the evening, often in a moment of worry. An AI-assisted process that responds quickly, answers questions, books a trial lesson and follows up could convert more enquiries. That's revenue for the franchisees and fees for Schülerhilfe.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Tutor scheduling. Matching pupils to groups of three to five by subject, level and time slot, and matching tutors to groups, is a hard puzzle across a thousand centres. Better scheduling, with AI help, reduces idle tutor hours and fills groups more efficiently.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Pricing and product mix. Test premium bundles, like online plus centre plus KIRA, against the entry price. Use data on which subjects and school levels retain best. Perhaps offer a lighter, cheaper "maintenance" plan for after a crisis, like the one in our Schneider example.

**NORA:** And growth beyond that?

**JAMES:** Acquisitions of regional or subject-specialist tutoring chains, folded into the franchise system. The category is consolidating, as the Studienkreis deal showed. Geographic focus too. Austria and Switzerland are in scope, but given Austria's contraction, the company should be selective. And adult courses could use the same online platform and AI tools.

**NORA:** What about families who can't afford it? The union's point about inequality.

**JAMES:** That's both a social question and, in general, a business one. In Germany there are public support schemes that can, in some circumstances, help lower-income families pay for learning support. I'm not going to describe the eligibility rules, because they're specific and can change. But making it easy for eligible families to use such support, and working with schools or local authorities where possible, could widen access and broaden the customer base at the same time.

**NORA:** And AI could make that cheaper to deliver.

**JAMES:** Potentially. A hybrid model with fewer human hours per pupil, supported by AI practice, could bring the cost per pupil down. That might make institutional contracts, for example with schools or municipalities, more viable. We found no evidence Schülerhilfe is doing this, so it's our idea, not a company plan.

**NORA:** What about the tutors themselves? Can AI help them?

**JAMES:** Very much, in my view. An AI assistant for tutors could summarise each pupil's recent work in KIRA before a session, suggest what to focus on, and draft a short progress update for parents. That makes every tutor more effective and saves preparation time. It also makes the hybrid model work, because the human and the AI share the same picture of the pupil.

**NORA:** And parent communication more broadly?

**JAMES:** I'd single that out. In tutoring, the person who pays is the parent, but the person who experiences the service is the child. Parents often don't see what happens in a session. A short, regular, honest update on what was covered, what improved and what's next builds confidence and makes the monthly fee feel worth it. AI can draft those updates from session notes and KIRA activity, with the tutor checking them. It's cheap, and in my view it could be one of the biggest drivers of retention.

**NORA:** Because a parent who can see progress doesn't cancel.

**JAMES:** Or at least cancels less often. And when the immediate crisis is over, the update can make the case for a lighter plan rather than a full stop.

**NORA:** What about recruitment, of tutors I mean?

**JAMES:** Another practical use. Screening applications, booking meetings with candidates, matching tutors' subjects and availability to centres' gaps. That's admin that centre managers spend a lot of time on. And in a tight labour market, responding to a good applicant quickly matters.

### The best AI bet and the first 100 days

**NORA:** One bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** AI-led retention and personalised learning plans. Use KIRA and the content library to keep each pupil progressing between sessions, give tutors and centre managers a view of who is at risk of leaving, and offer families the right plan at the right moment.

**NORA:** Why that over lead conversion?

**JAMES:** Because it uses assets already in place, KIRA and the content, and because retention on monthly contracts is the biggest sensitivity in the model. Lead conversion is a close second, and arguably quicker to win.

**NORA:** How would you run the first hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: pick around twenty centres, a mix of owned and franchised, in different regions. Measure the baseline: how long pupils stay, when they leave, how many use KIRA, and what happens around exam periods. Agree the privacy rules for using pupil data, with parents' consent and clear explanations.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Build a simple early-warning view for centre managers. Signals might include fewer KIRA sessions, missed lessons, a recent grade improvement, or the end of an exam period. Nothing fancy. Then design interventions: a call from the tutor, a suggested lighter plan, a progress report to parents. And give tutors a short pre-session summary from KIRA.

**NORA:** Sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Run it in the pilot centres, compare retention with similar centres not in the pilot, and listen to centre managers and franchisees. At day one hundred, decide whether to roll out, and what to change.

**NORA:** Let's do a second walk-through, from the centre manager's side. Invented again.

**JAMES:** Sure. Meet Sabine, who runs a franchise centre in a mid-sized town with about a hundred and twenty pupils. Today, on a Monday, she checks the schedule, deals with a tutor who's off sick, answers parents' emails, and discovers that three families cancelled last week. She didn't see it coming. One had a child whose grades had improved. One felt the lessons weren't helping. One was just tight on money.

**NORA:** And with the new tools?

**JAMES:** On Monday, Sabine opens a dashboard. It shows eight pupils flagged as at risk. Two have stopped using KIRA. Three have just had good test results, which in the past often preceded cancellation. Two have missed lessons. One family's contract is up for its quarterly review. For each, the system suggests an action. She asks the tutors to have a quick word with two pupils, sends a progress report with a suggested lighter plan to the families whose children improved, and calls the family that missed lessons.

**NORA:** And the tutor who's off sick?

**JAMES:** The scheduling assistant suggests a cover tutor and regroups two small classes for that afternoon, and drafts the messages to parents. Sabine checks and sends them.

**NORA:** So her week is about people, not admin.

**JAMES:** That's the aim. And the result in our hypothetical: perhaps she keeps four of those eight families who might otherwise have left. Multiply that across a thousand centres, and you have a meaningful business result.

**NORA:** What could go wrong?

**JAMES:** If the warning signals feel like surveillance to families, trust suffers. If franchisees see it as head office control, they won't use it. And if the AI makes wrong assumptions about a child, it could lead to clumsy conversations. All of that argues for transparency, consent, and keeping humans firmly in charge of decisions.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Widen out. What can other businesses learn here?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, when AI makes the basic version of your service free, give the basic version away and charge for what surrounds it. Schülerhilfe offering free homework help in its app is an example. The paid product is the human relationship and the structure.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, put AI behind the paywall when it strengthens the relationship. KIRA being only for customers makes it a reason to stay. If it were free to all, it might replace the paid service instead.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, in subscription businesses with easy cancellation, retention is where AI earns its keep. Many businesses spend heavily on acquisition and too little on understanding why customers leave. AI can help spot the signals early.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, in franchise networks, AI has to help the franchisee first. If the tools make franchisees' lives easier and their centres more profitable, adoption follows. If they feel like head office monitoring, they won't.

**NORA:** And for a small tutoring business or an independent tutor?

**JAMES:** Use AI to do the prep and the admin, so you can spend more time with the pupil. And tell parents clearly what you offer that a chatbot can't: you notice, you plan, and you hold the child accountable.

**NORA:** A fifth lesson?

**JAMES:** Yes, about trust. When your customers are children, or anyone vulnerable, the bar for AI is higher. Be explicit about what the AI does, what data it uses, and who is responsible. Parents will forgive a lot from a company that is open with them. They won't forgive a surprise.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-native disruption: free tools reducing paid hours. Two, market contraction: the Austrian data shows spending falling, and if Germany follows, pricing power weakens. Three, a strong, consolidated competitor in GoStudent and Studienkreis. Four, regulatory and reputational risk around children's data, especially with AI. Five, franchise relations, if the network feels squeezed.

**NORA:** And an AI-specific risk?

**JAMES:** Quality and pedagogy. An AI assistant that just gives answers could undermine learning, and parents and teachers will notice. One that makes mistakes in maths would damage trust quickly. KIRA's grounding in vetted content is the right approach, but it has to be maintained.

**NORA:** Is there a regulatory angle on AI in education?

**JAMES:** In general terms, yes. The European Union's AI Act treats certain AI uses in education as higher risk, for example systems that evaluate learning outcomes or influence access to education. Whether a homework assistant falls into those categories depends on what it does, and that's a question for lawyers, not for us. But any company using AI with pupils in Europe needs to keep track of those rules, alongside data protection law.

**NORA:** And schools getting their own AI tools could change parents' expectations.

**JAMES:** It could. If a school provides a free, approved AI assistant, parents will ask what the paid one adds. Schülerhilfe will need a crisp answer, probably the combination of a human tutor and content aligned to its own lessons.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, any KIRA updates, especially new subjects, better parent reporting, or evidence of impact on retention. Second, acquisitions, which would signal a buy-and-build phase under the new owner. Third, the next Austrian tutoring barometer and any comparable German data, to see whether the market is shrinking or stabilising.

**NORA:** And a warning sign?

**JAMES:** Price cuts across the network without a change in the offer. That would suggest the company is competing with free AI on price, which is a race it can't win.
