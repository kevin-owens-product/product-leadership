### Heat exchangers and why tubes leak

**NORA:** Let's do a little engineering background, because I suspect many listeners don't know what a heat exchanger is.

**JAMES:** Happy to. A heat exchanger moves heat from one fluid to another without them mixing. The most common type in refineries and chemical plants is the shell and tube exchanger. Picture a big cylinder, the shell, with hundreds or thousands of small tubes running through it. One fluid flows inside the tubes, another flows around them inside the shell, and heat passes through the tube walls.

**NORA:** And plants have a lot of them.

**JAMES:** As general background, a large refinery or chemical plant can have hundreds of heat exchangers. They heat, cool, condense and recover energy all over the process. Power stations have them too, condensers and feedwater heaters for example.

**NORA:** Why do the tubes leak?

**JAMES:** Corrosion, erosion, vibration, fatigue, deposits. The tube walls are relatively thin, and they're exposed to hot, sometimes corrosive fluids for years. Eventually some tubes develop holes or cracks. When a tube leaks, the two fluids mix, which can contaminate the product, reduce efficiency or create a safety hazard.

**NORA:** So you plug it.

**JAMES:** You plug it, at both ends, which takes that tube out of service. The exchanger keeps working with slightly less capacity. It's a standard maintenance practice. As more tubes get plugged over the years, eventually the exchanger needs re-tubing or replacement. But plugging buys time, often years, at a fraction of the cost.

**NORA:** How do they find the leaking tubes in the first place?

**JAMES:** As general background, inspection contractors use a few methods. Pressure or leak testing shows which tubes leak. Non-destructive testing, like eddy current inspection, sends a probe down each tube to measure wall thinning, so engineers can plug tubes that are about to fail, not just ones that already have. The inspection report then becomes the shopping list for plugs.

**NORA:** So there's a planning chain. Inspect, decide, plug.

**JAMES:** Exactly. And each step produces information. Which tubes, what wall loss, what decision. If that information flowed straight from the inspection report into a plug order, a lot of re-typing and guesswork would disappear. That's a thread we'll pull on when we get to AI.

**NORA:** Is there documentation involved?

**JAMES:** Usually. Plants keep records of which tubes have been plugged in each exchanger, and for regulated pressure equipment, inspectors and engineers want to know what was installed. So a supplier that provides clear documentation, the plug type, material, rating and certificates, makes the customer's record-keeping easier.

**NORA:** And the plug has to be right.

**JAMES:** It has to fit the tube's inside diameter precisely, be compatible with the tube material and the fluids, and hold at the operating pressure and temperature. A plug that leaks or blows out under pressure is a serious problem. So tube plugs are an engineered product, even though they're small.

**NORA:** And the pipe isolation side?

**JAMES:** That's related. When maintenance crews weld a new section of pipe or a new flange, they often need to pressure-test the weld. Filling and pressurising an entire piping system just to test one weld is slow and wasteful. An isolation tool seals the pipe on either side of the weld so you can test just that small section. Again, it has to fit exactly and be rated for the test pressure.

### The customer problem

**NORA:** So who's the customer, and what's their problem?

**JAMES:** The users are maintenance teams and contractors in refineries, chemical plants, power stations and similar facilities. They buy for planned turnarounds and outages, and for unplanned repairs. Their problem, in my view, comes down to three things: getting the right part, getting it fast, and getting technical help when something unexpected happens.

**NORA:** Start with the right part.

**JAMES:** Matching is genuinely complicated. Tubes come in many diameters and wall thicknesses, which together determine the inside diameter the plug must fit. Tube materials vary: carbon steel, stainless steels, copper alloys, titanium and others. Pressure and temperature ratings matter. Different plug designs suit different situations. A maintenance planner has to translate the exchanger's specification into the right part number, and the specification may be on an old data sheet, a drawing, or in someone's memory.

**NORA:** And fast.

**JAMES:** Turnarounds are planned months ahead, but inspections during the turnaround reveal surprises. You don't know exactly how many tubes are leaking until you open and test the exchanger. So there's a planned order, and then urgent top-up orders when the inspection results come in. Our inference, consistent with how the industry works, is that availability and engineering help matter more than price for those urgent orders.

**NORA:** And technical help?

**JAMES:** Installation questions, mostly. Which tool for this plug? What torque or force? What if the tube end is damaged? What if the inspector is asking for documentation of the plug's rating? A supplier that can answer those questions fast, at any hour during a turnaround, is far more valuable than one that only ships boxes.

**NORA:** So it's a product company that has to behave like a service company.

**JAMES:** That's a nice way to put it. The product is engineered hardware. The value is in getting the right hardware to the right place, with the right advice, at the right moment.

**NORA:** Who actually places the order? The plant, or a contractor?

**JAMES:** Both, in general. Some plants buy directly and keep stock. In many turnarounds, specialist contractors do the plugging or testing work and bring or order the products. And some orders flow through industrial distributors. We don't know USA Industries' channel mix. But it matters for strategy, because the customer experience, the data you have, and the pricing all differ by channel.

**NORA:** How so?

**JAMES:** If you sell direct to a plant, you can build a relationship and see its order history over many turnarounds. If you sell through a contractor, the contractor owns the relationship and may switch suppliers job to job. If you sell through a distributor, you may not even know which plant your plugs ended up in. In my view, the digital tools we'll discuss work best where USA Industries has a direct relationship, and they could also be a way to strengthen relationships with contractors, by making the contractor's life easier.

### A turnaround, step by step

**NORA:** Let's do the hypothetical walk-through. Invented plant, invented people.

**JAMES:** Let's say a refinery near Baton Rouge is in the middle of a planned turnaround. The maintenance planner, call her Kim, ordered a standard set of tube plugs months ago, based on the previous turnaround's results. On day six, the inspection contractor tests a large exchanger and finds forty leaking tubes, more than expected, and some are a different tube size from the ones she planned for.

**NORA:** What happens today?

**JAMES:** It's late afternoon. Kim pulls the exchanger's data sheet from the plant's document system. It lists the tube outside diameter and a gauge for wall thickness, and the material. She fills in the supplier's Quick Quote form or calls the sales team. An inside sales engineer, call him Luis, takes the details, works out the inside diameter, checks the material and pressure, looks up the right plug series and part number in the catalogue, checks stock, and emails a quote.

**NORA:** How long does that take?

**JAMES:** If Luis is at his desk and the data sheet is clear, maybe an hour or two. If the data sheet is ambiguous, or the material needs checking, or it's after hours, it could be the next morning. Meanwhile the turnaround schedule has the exchanger going back together in two days.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** Kim gets the quote approved, a purchase order is raised, and the order ships. If stock is in Louisiana, it arrives quickly. If it's in Houston, it's a drive. On site, the crew installs the plugs. One of the tube ends is damaged, and the crew isn't sure whether the standard plug will seal. Someone calls the supplier's technical team for advice.

**NORA:** Now replay it with AI.

**JAMES:** Kim opens the supplier's online part-finder. She uploads a photo of the exchanger data sheet, or types the key fields. The tool reads the tube size, gauge and material, calculates the inside diameter, and shows the matching plug options with part numbers, pressure ratings, stock at each branch and a price. It flags that two of the options suit her material and pressure, and explains the difference. She picks one and places the order in minutes.

**NORA:** And Luis?

**JAMES:** Luis still reviews anything unusual. If the tool isn't confident, because the material is uncommon or the pressure is near a limit, it routes the request to him with everything pre-filled. So he spends his time on the hard cases, not on routine lookups.

**NORA:** And the damaged tube end?

**JAMES:** The crew lead opens a technical support assistant on a phone, describes the situation and sends a photo. The assistant pulls the relevant installation guidance from the company's procedures and past support cases, and suggests the options, with a clear note to confirm with a technical specialist for anything outside the standard procedure. If needed, it escalates to a human expert with the photo and context attached.

**NORA:** So it's a first line of help.

**JAMES:** Exactly. For the common questions, it's instant. For the unusual ones, it gets the right expert involved faster, with better information. Safety-critical decisions stay with qualified people.

**NORA:** Could the inspection report itself feed the order?

**JAMES:** That's the step beyond, and I find it exciting. The inspection contractor produces a report listing which tubes need plugging, often with a tube map of the exchanger. If the part-finder could read that report, with the planner's permission, it could produce the full plug order automatically: how many, which sizes, which material, plus spares. The planner checks and approves. That removes a lot of the re-typing on day six, which is exactly when everyone is most tired and most rushed.

**NORA:** Would inspection contractors cooperate?

**JAMES:** Some might, especially if it makes their reports more useful to their own customers. It's speculative, but in my view a supplier that builds that link becomes very hard to dislodge.

### A second walk-through: the pressure test

**NORA:** Let's do a second example, on the isolation and testing side. Again, invented.

**JAMES:** Picture a chemical plant replacing a section of process pipe during an outage. The welders join a new flange onto an existing line. The quality inspector needs a hydrostatic test of the new weld, meaning it's filled with water and pressurised to prove it holds, before the line goes back into service.

**NORA:** And without an isolation tool?

**JAMES:** You'd have to blank off and fill a long run of pipe, maybe including valves and equipment you don't want to expose to water, then drain and dry it all afterwards. That can take a lot of time in an outage where every hour counts. With an isolation test tool, a crew inserts a device that seals the pipe on either side of the weld, and only the small volume around the weld gets pressurised.

**NORA:** So the question is, again, which tool?

**JAMES:** Exactly. The crew lead, call him Andre, needs a tool that fits that pipe's inside diameter and schedule, which is the industry term for wall thickness classes, that suits the flange type, and that's rated for the required test pressure. Today, he calls the supplier or checks a catalogue and a calculator, and hopes he's got the numbers right.

**NORA:** And with AI?

**JAMES:** He opens the part-finder on his phone, picks pipe size and schedule, enters the test pressure, and gets the matching tool, its rating, whether it's in stock at the nearest branch, and whether it can be rented or must be bought, if the supplier offers both. The assistant also pulls up the installation and safety procedure for that tool, with a reminder of the key steps before pressurising.

**NORA:** Safety reminders from a chatbot. Would crews trust that?

**JAMES:** They'd trust it if it's the supplier's own validated procedure, presented clearly, not something improvised. In my view, the assistant should quote and link the official procedure rather than paraphrase it creatively. Pressure testing can be hazardous if done wrong. The tool's job is to make the right procedure easier to find, not to replace training or the site's own safety rules.

**NORA:** And for USA Industries, what's the commercial benefit?

**JAMES:** Faster orders, fewer wrong tools shipped and returned, fewer emergency calls to the technical team, and a crew lead who reaches for the same app next time. Across many outages, that's loyalty built one job at a time.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's name the challenges. What's hard for USA Industries, in your view?

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Manual quoting. A Quick Quote form suggests that every request is handled by a person. That's fine at low volume, but it limits how fast the company can respond during turnaround peaks, and it ties revenue to the capacity of a small sales engineering team.

**JAMES:** Competition from larger groups with service scale. T.D. Williamson and similar companies can bundle isolation services with products. A refinery that hires a big contractor for its turnaround may simply use whatever products the contractor brings.

**NORA:** Fourth.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Key-person dependence during the ownership transition. A small leadership team, and a lot of product and customer knowledge probably concentrated in a few experienced people. It's not flashy AI, it's forecasting, but in a business that sells availability, it's directly tied to revenue and working capital.

### The first 100 days and the best AI bet

**NORA:** The new owner is about eleven months in, so the first hundred days have passed. But let's ask the question anyway. If you were starting the AI programme now, what would the first hundred days look like?

**JAMES:** I'd start with the part-finder, because it has the clearest value, a well-defined task, and data the company already owns. Days one to thirty: build the product data foundation. Gather every part number, dimension, material, pressure and temperature rating, and compatibility rule, from the catalogues, calculators and the sales engineers' knowledge, into one structured table. That's the boring, essential step.

**NORA:** And involve the sales engineers.

**JAMES:** Absolutely. They know which rules are written down and which live in their heads. Ask them, what are the ten questions you ask on every quote, and what are the five mistakes customers make? Their answers become the tool's logic. Also agree the measures: time from request to quote, quote-to-order conversion, and the share of requests that can be handled without a person.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Build an internal version first. Sales engineers use it to answer real requests, and it suggests the part and price while they check. You measure how often it's right and how much time it saves. Any wrong suggestion gets investigated and the data fixed. Nothing goes to customers yet.

**NORA:** And days sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Open it to a few trusted customers, maybe a handful of refineries with turnarounds coming up. Let them use the part-finder directly, with every order still reviewed by a sales engineer before it ships. Measure speed and accuracy. If it's working, plan a full launch before the next spring or autumn turnaround season, which are typically busy periods in the industry.

**NORA:** How would you get customers to actually use it? Maintenance planners are busy people with old habits.

**JAMES:** Meet them where they already are. Put the part-finder behind the existing Quick Quote form, so a request typed in the usual way gets an instant answer. Have sales engineers show it to customers on real orders rather than in a marketing email. And make it obviously faster than a phone call. If a planner gets the right part and a price in two minutes on a Friday night, they'll use it again. If it asks them for twenty fields, they'll pick up the phone.

**NORA:** So adoption is about time saved, not features.

**JAMES:** Always, in industrial settings. The test is whether a tired planner on day six of a turnaround reaches for it.

**NORA:** And inside the company? Would the sales engineers see it as a threat?

**JAMES:** They might, at first. So be explicit: the tool exists to take the routine lookups off their desks so they can spend time on the complex applications, the big turnaround accounts and the customer visits that actually grow the business. Measure them on customers won and kept, not on quotes typed. And give them the job of improving the tool, because nobody knows its blind spots better than they do.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** The best sales engineer, or the sales leader, paired with someone who can handle the data and the software. If there's no technology leader in the company, this is a good moment to hire one, or bring in a trusted partner for the build.

**NORA:** And governance?

**JAMES:** Three rules. The tool never recommends a part outside its validated data. Anything near a pressure or temperature limit, or with an unusual material, goes to a human. And every recommendation shows its reasoning, the inside diameter it calculated, the rating it matched, so a customer or sales engineer can check it. In a safety-relevant product, transparency is non-negotiable.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** The technical support assistant. It builds on the same structured data, plus procedures and support history. It's a bit harder, because field questions are more varied than part lookups, so it comes second.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from USA Industries?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, if your customers struggle to choose the right product, that's your first AI opportunity. Many industrial product companies have catalogues full of options and a handful of experts who help customers choose. A good part-finder scales those experts.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, structured product data is the foundation. Before any AI, get every product's specifications, compatibility rules and ratings into one clean, structured place. It's useful immediately, for the website, for sales and for operations, and it's essential for AI.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, availability is a strategy. The Louisiana branch is a reminder that in time-critical industrial supply, being close to the customer with stock on hand can matter more than any technology. AI should support that, for example by forecasting what to stock where, not distract from it.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, turn transactions into relationships. Order history can become planning help for customers. A supplier that helps you plan your next turnaround is much harder to replace than one you only call when something breaks.

**NORA:** I'd add one more. Be findable.

**JAMES:** Yes. When your name is shared with other businesses, make it easy for customers and recruits to find you, and to understand what you do. A clear website, clear product data, and a visible presence at events like PVP all help. In a niche, being the obvious name matters.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI disruption to quoting from outside. If large customers or engineering firms start using AI-driven parts search or procurement platforms that compare suppliers automatically, catalogue-based margins could erode. That's our inference. Two, larger competitors with service scale bundling products into their services. Three, cyclical end markets tied to energy prices. Four, key-person dependence during the ownership handover.

**NORA:** And AI-specific risks?

**JAMES:** The big one is a wrong recommendation in a pressure application. That's why the governance rules matter so much. A second is customer trust. If customers find the part-finder wrong even once on something important, they'll stop using it. So it's better to launch narrow and accurate than broad and unreliable.

**NORA:** Tell me more about the procurement platform risk.

**JAMES:** As general background, large industrial companies increasingly buy through digital procurement systems and marketplaces, and AI is starting to help buyers search catalogues across many suppliers at once. If a refinery's purchasing system can automatically match a plug specification against five suppliers' digital catalogues, the supplier with the cleanest, most complete product data gets found first. A supplier whose data lives in PDFs may not get found at all.

**NORA:** So clean product data is defensive as well as offensive.

**JAMES:** Exactly. It powers your own part-finder, and it makes you visible in your customers' systems. Either way, it's the same foundation.

**NORA:** And the long-term energy transition? Refining is the core market.

**JAMES:** In my view, that's a slow-moving factor rather than a near-term risk. Existing refineries and chemical plants will need maintenance for many years, and heat exchangers are everywhere in industry, including in power generation and newer processes. But over the long run, a company concentrated in refining should broaden its markets. Power, including nuclear, and other process industries are natural directions.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** It's early in the hold. The value creation story is likely to rest on geographic expansion, possible add-on acquisitions, better pricing, and a modern digital experience that makes USA Industries the easiest supplier to buy from in its niche. If AI quoting and support can be shown to increase win rates during turnarounds, that's a strong part of that story.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, the first add-on acquisition under the new owner, and what it adds. Second, any launch of an online part-finder, configurator or customer portal. And third, further branch openings along the Gulf Coast or elsewhere, which would show the availability strategy expanding.
