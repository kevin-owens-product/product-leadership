**MAYA:** Ask a clinician in a busy practice what they'd change about their job, and they rarely say the medicine. They say the notes. The forms. The calls to insurers. The hour of documentation after the last patient leaves.

**GRANT:** Welcome back to AI in the Real Economy. I'm Grant.

**MAYA:** I'm Maya. Today we're looking at healthcare services and other regulated businesses, where documentation isn't overhead. It's part of what you're paid for, and it's what an auditor will read. That makes AI both more valuable and more dangerous.

### Why healthcare services

**GRANT:** Why does healthcare get its own episode?

**MAYA:** Because it combines the two things we said matter most in episode one. A shortage of skilled people, and a huge amount of repetitive judgment work around them. And it's a big part of private equity portfolios: physician practice platforms, home health, behavioral health, dental and veterinary groups, labs, and the tech-enabled services companies that support them.

**GRANT:** And the margins are thin.

**MAYA:** Very. AlixPartners published an analysis last November on AI in healthcare private equity. Their figures for the prior three years had healthcare providers growing revenue around nine and a half percent with margins under two percent, and HealthTech with faster growth but EBITDA margins under three percent. When margins are that thin, a few points of labor efficiency changes the whole return.

---

### Where AI is working

**GRANT:** Where is AI actually working?

**MAYA:** Documentation is the clearest one. AI that listens to a visit or a call and drafts the note for the clinician to review. AlixPartners described one client that cut after-call work by more than half, which let clinicians see more patients. That's their client example, so it's one data point, but it matches what you hear across the sector.

**GRANT:** What else?

**MAYA:** Self-service for routine questions. The same analysis described a virtual assistant that handled questions about benefits, copays and coverage, and said that in one deployment more than seventy percent of activated members used it for most simple interactions. That frees the human advocates for the hard cases.

**GRANT:** And on the clinical-adjacent side?

**MAYA:** Targeted outreach. Models that combine claims, clinical and engagement data to flag people at risk, so a care team can call them before they end up in hospital. And in clinical research services, Permira described AI in adverse event case processing cutting intake time by a quarter while improving auditability.

**GRANT:** I notice every example has a human checking the output.

**MAYA:** That's not an accident. In regulated work, the value comes from AI doing the first draft and a qualified person signing off. The draft might be ninety percent right, but the person is still accountable for the other ten.

---

### The revenue cycle

**GRANT:** We can't talk about healthcare services without talking about getting paid.

**MAYA:** No. The revenue cycle is full of AI opportunity: coding visits correctly, checking claims before they're sent, predicting which claims will be denied, drafting appeals, and handling prior authorization paperwork. Denied and under-coded claims are revenue leakage, which is our first lever from episode one.

**GRANT:** But there's a line here.

**MAYA:** A bright one. Coding has to reflect what actually happened. An AI tuned to maximize reimbursement is a compliance risk. The right goal is accuracy: catch the under-coding and the missing documentation, and never invent anything. Your compliance team should own the rules the AI follows, and someone should audit samples every month.

---

### The rulebook

**GRANT:** Let's do regulation, because it changed this year.

**MAYA:** In the US, the baseline is unchanged. Patient data is protected health information, so any AI vendor touching it needs a business associate agreement, and you need to know where the data goes, how long it's kept, and whether it's used for training. Then there's a growing set of state rules on automated decision-making. Foley's piece mentioned California, Colorado and New York, covering decisions like hiring, pricing, credit and insurance.

**GRANT:** And Europe, for funds with European portfolio companies?

**MAYA:** The EU AI Act timeline moved. In May, the EU institutions agreed the Digital Omnibus on AI, which pushed back the high-risk obligations. For stand-alone high-risk systems listed in Annex III, things like recruitment and credit scoring tools, the date moved from August 2026 to December 2027. For AI embedded in regulated products, like medical devices, it moved to August 2028.

**GRANT:** So companies got a reprieve.

**MAYA:** On the high-risk rules, yes. But the transparency obligations in Article 50 still applied from August 2026, with a short grace period for some watermarking requirements. And the general-purpose model rules have applied since August 2025. My advice is to use the extra time to build the documentation and risk management you'll need anyway, rather than to stop.

---

### Beyond healthcare

**GRANT:** You said "other regulated work." What else fits this pattern?

**MAYA:** Anything where the paperwork is the product. Insurance claims administration. Inspection and testing, like environmental or safety testing labs. Compliance services, like the background check and workforce compliance world. Clinical research services. Even parts of financial services. The same rules apply: AI drafts, an accountable person signs, every output can be traced back to its sources, and the compliance team owns the guardrails.

**GRANT:** And a buyer at exit will check all of that.

**MAYA:** They will. And increasingly, so will the insurers. The Foley piece noted that representations and warranties insurers are starting to exclude AI-specific risks. If you can show clean controls, you're in a better position than a company that can't.

---

### A playbook for regulated work

**GRANT:** Give me the playbook.

**MAYA:** Five steps. One, start with documentation and administrative work, not clinical decisions. Two, keep a qualified human signing every output that goes to a patient, a payer or a regulator. Three, sign proper agreements with every vendor, and know where your data goes. Four, measure time saved, error rates, denial rates and audit findings, not just usage. And five, write down your controls now, because your next buyer, your insurer and possibly a regulator will ask for them.

**GRANT:** And the people side?

**MAYA:** Involve clinicians from day one. If the tool saves them an hour a day and they helped choose it, they'll champion it. If it's imposed and gets things wrong in front of patients, it's dead within a month.
