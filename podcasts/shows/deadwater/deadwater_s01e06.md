# DEADWATER
## Episode 6: "Pizza Night"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] On a Tuesday evening in January of the surveillance year, Roland Voss orders a large pizza from a delivery place two blocks from his Manhattan office. He orders it through a phone call, not an app - an old habit, or perhaps a deliberate choice in the direction of a man who is careful about his digital footprint in ways that extend even to his ordinary habits. He pays cash when the delivery arrives. He eats at his desk, working through the evening on a commercial project that is behind schedule. He eats most of the pizza. He puts two crusts and a handful of napkins and the empty parmesan packet into the box. He closes the box. He puts it in the wastebasket beside his desk.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The building's cleaning crew comes through at ten-thirty. The wastebasket is emptied into a large black contractor bag. The bag is tied and moved to the building's service corridor. At eleven, the service elevator carries it to the basement. At midnight, the building's nighttime maintenance worker moves it to the dumpster in the alley behind the building.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] At twelve-seventeen in the morning, a surveillance operative in plain clothes - jeans, a dark jacket, carrying a shopping bag - walks down the alley, opens the dumpster, removes the bag, and replaces it with an identical one. The original bag goes into a sealed evidence container in the back of an unmarked vehicle parked on the cross street. The operative gets in the passenger seat. The vehicle moves.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] At seven-fourteen the following morning, the pizza crusts are in Dr. Nina Park's laboratory.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Sam Wilder. Deadwater, Episode Six.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The retrieval operation is the product of six weeks of patience and preparation. Six weeks during which the surveillance team has been monitoring Roland Voss's movements, building the pattern data, cross-referencing the burner phone analysis, and waiting for the right moment to obtain a DNA sample without his knowledge and without any action that could alert him to being watched.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The right moment requires specific conditions. A discarded item that is clearly attributable to Voss - not shared with other people, not potentially contaminated by handlers before retrieval. A retrieval opportunity that does not expose the surveillance operation. A chain of custody that is clean and documented from the moment of retrieval to the moment the sample reaches the lab.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Several opportunities are identified and rejected in the six weeks before the pizza night. A coffee cup discarded in a public trash can - rejected because the chain of custody from discard to retrieval cannot be secured against multiple handlers. A water bottle left in the train car after his commute - rejected because the seat area creates contamination questions. A receipt discarded outside a restaurant - rejected because it lacks biological material.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The pizza box is different. Voss orders from the same place twice in three weeks, both times by phone, both times paying cash on delivery. The second time, the surveillance team has positioned an operative in the alley at the anticipated time. The chain of custody is planned in advance. The retrieval goes without a visible hitch.

[MARSH] The retrieval itself was four minutes from start to finish, on a quiet night with minimal foot traffic in the alley. The operative was experienced, the protocol was rehearsed, the replacement bag was prepared in advance. Everything went exactly as planned. I found out at twelve-thirty in the morning when I got a message from the team lead that said: we have the package. I said: good. Get some sleep. We'll start in the morning.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She does not sleep much herself that night.

[MARSH] I lay awake thinking about the chain of events. If the analysis comes back with what I believe it will come back with, we will have, for the first time in twelve years, direct physical evidence connecting a specific individual to the crime scenes. Not inferential. Not behavioral. Physical. His DNA on a victim. His DNA from a pizza crust. A match.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I made myself stop thinking about it. I got up at five and went for a run. That is my mechanism for not getting ahead of myself. Twelve years has taught me not to get ahead of myself.

[NARRATOR] The DNA extraction from the pizza crusts begins the morning after retrieval and takes eleven days to complete. The delay is not unusual - the process of extracting genetic material from food residue, generating a clean genetic profile, and running that profile against the reference material requires multiple stages of analysis, each of which has to be done carefully and completely.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] For the investigators on the task force, eleven days is a particular kind of waiting. They continue working - there are other investigative threads active, the file review is ongoing, the cell and E-ZPass analysis continues to generate data that is being documented for the eventual case file. But underneath all of it is the knowledge that eleven days from now, or thereabouts, they will know.

[REESE] I checked in with Carol twice a day during those eleven days. Not because there was anything to report. Just because that is what you do. You check in. You maintain the connection. You make sure the person who has been carrying this case for twelve years has someone to talk to while she waits for an answer she has been working toward for twelve years.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] She was fine. That is the remarkable thing about Carol Marsh. She was fine. She was doing her job. She was patient in a way that I find genuinely remarkable given everything she had invested in this case and everything she understood about what the result was going to mean.

[MARSH] I was not fine on the inside. On the outside I was functioning. I was at my desk, I was in briefings, I was reviewing documents and making decisions and running the task force the way it needed to be run. On the inside I was absolutely vibrating. But you learn, in this work, to put a wall between what you feel and what you show. It is a skill and it is also, sometimes, a problem. But it was useful in those eleven days.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] On the eleventh day, Dr. Park comes into the task force operations center at six-forty-three in the morning. The building is almost empty at that hour. She walks directly to Carol Marsh's office - the door is open; Marsh is already at her desk - and she says four words.

[DR. PARK] I said: Carol. It's a match.

[NARRATOR] She says it quietly. Not dramatically. Just those four words, standing in the doorway.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Marsh puts down whatever she was reading. She looks at Park.

[MARSH] I said: how strong?

[DR. PARK] As strong as I have ever seen. The sample from the pizza crust is Roland Voss. The DNA from the hair found at the crime scene matches the pizza crust profile at every marker we tested. He was there. He was at the scene. He is the contributor.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh says thank you. She says she will see Dr. Park at eight. She will need the analysis documentation in full by noon. She picks up her phone.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She calls Danny Reese. It is six forty-nine.

[MARSH] He picked up immediately. I said four words back to him: we have a match. He said: how long? I said: forty-eight hours to be safe. He said: I'll start now.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The forty-eight hours between the DNA confirmation and the arrest are the most operationally intense of the entire investigation. Everything that has been built over twelve years - and over the ten months of the task force work specifically - now needs to be assembled into an arrest package that is legally airtight, procedurally perfect, and ready to move at the moment Carol Marsh decides to move.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese builds the arrest case. He has, in fact, been building it in draft form for weeks - there is a version of the arrest narrative that dates back to the third week of the surveillance, when he was confident enough in the direction of the case to start writing it. What happens in the forty-eight hours is that the draft becomes final, every piece of evidence is formally documented in its proper place in the narrative, and every link in the chain of inference is tested against the hardest questions a defense attorney might ask.

[REESE] An arrest case is not just evidence. It is the story of the evidence - the sequence in which you present it so that the cumulative weight is clear and the conclusions are unavoidable. You start with what happened. You move to what the evidence shows. You build from there to who it points to and why. At every step the question is: what would the other side say about this? And if the answer is "nothing" or "very little," you move on. If the answer is "this," you fix it before you file.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I found two places in the initial draft where I thought the documentation needed strengthening. One was in the chain of custody documentation for the parking citation - there was a small gap in how it had been logged when it was first pulled from the archive, and I wanted that gap explicitly addressed. The other was in the cell tower analysis, where I wanted an additional statistical annotation to make the probability of coincidence clearer to a judge.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I sent the draft to Carol with those two notes. She had both issues addressed within twelve hours. The digital forensics team did the cell tower annotation overnight. The parking citation chain of custody was reconstructed from the archive logs by ten the following morning.

[NARRATOR] Frank Tully, the DA, reviews the final arrest case package on the afternoon before the arrest.

[TULLY] I read it twice. It took about four hours each time. I had a list of questions going in and I worked through each of them against the document. By the end of the second read the list was empty. Every question had an answer in the package. The case was complete, documented, and defensible.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] I have been a prosecutor for nineteen years. I have reviewed dozens of arrest case packages. The one Danny Reese built for the arrest of Roland Voss is the best I have ever seen. Bar none. It is the product of twelve years of investigative work assembled with the rigor and precision that the case deserved from the beginning. The people who built it should be proud of it.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] On the afternoon before the arrest, Carol Marsh begins calling families.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She has nine calls to make. Nine families - or, in two cases, surviving friends who are the primary next-of-kin contacts on the case file. Nine people who have been waiting, in some cases, for more than twenty-five years.

[MARSH] I had thought about what to say. I had thought about it for a long time, actually - not specifically for this moment, but in the general sense that I always knew this call was coming and I wanted to be prepared for it. You cannot prepare for it. Not entirely. But you can think about what matters.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] What I said to each of them was: there has been a significant development in the investigation. We will be making an arrest within forty-eight hours. I cannot give you details yet because the operation is active and I need to protect it. But I want you to know before anyone else outside this investigation does. And I want you to know that the work you have done has been part of this. You have mattered to this case.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Most of them cried. Several of them could not speak for a while. I waited. I stayed on the line. I did not rush any of them.

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey is the seventh call.

[DONNA] My phone rang at two-thirty in the afternoon on a Thursday. I was at my desk at the organization's office, working on a grant report. I saw it was Carol and I answered immediately.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] She told me there had been a significant development. That an arrest was coming within forty-eight hours. That she could not tell me who yet but that the evidence was strong. And then she said: Donna, I want you to know that what you have done has been part of this. The legislation. The advocacy. The pressure you have never stopped applying. That has mattered.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I said: tell me when it's done. She said: I will be the one who calls you.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I hung up and I sat at my desk for a long time. And then I called my mother. My mother is seventy-eight. She has been waiting for that call for twelve years. I said: Mom, there is going to be news soon. They have someone. She was quiet for a moment and then she said: Renee's person? And I said: yes. And she made a sound that I cannot describe and that I will never forget for the rest of my life. It was the sound of twelve years of holding something finally beginning to release.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] She made me promise to call her the moment the arrest was public. I promised. I kept the promise.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The arrest plan is simple and its simplicity is deliberate. Roland Voss leaves his house at five-forty in the morning on weekdays to walk to the train station. The walk takes fourteen minutes. The arrest is planned for the midpoint - far enough from the house that his family does not witness it from a window, far enough from the station that there are no civilian complications.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Two plainclothes arrest teams are positioned along the route. A uniformed unit is staged one block east. The arrest team commander is in contact with the task force operations center throughout.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh is in the operations center, not on the street. She made this decision deliberately. Danny Reese asked her about it.

[REESE] I asked her if she wanted to be there for the arrest. It would have been completely understandable. More than understandable - I would have wanted to be there. She said no. She said the team that did the surveillance work and built the case deserved to make the arrest. She said she didn't need to be the one with the handcuffs. She said she needed to hear the word.

[MARSH] I have thought about that decision. Whether I was being principled or whether I was protecting myself from something I didn't want to feel in public. The honest answer is probably both. The team earned the arrest. That is true. And also, I knew that whatever I felt when that call came over the radio, I did not want to feel it in front of a camera. This case has been about the victims and their families. The moment of the arrest should belong to the investigation, not to me.

[NARRATOR] At five-fifty-one in the morning, in the pale gray light of a winter sunrise on a quiet residential street in Massapequa Park, Roland Voss is stopped on the sidewalk halfway between his house and the train station. He is six foot four and two hundred and forty pounds and he has been hiding for more than twenty years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The arrest team commander comes over the radio.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Voss is in custody.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I was standing in the operations center with Danny when the call came. We had been standing in silence for about ten minutes, waiting. And then the radio said those words. And I exhaled.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] Twelve years. All of it came out in one breath.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] She picks up her phone and dials Donna Kelsey.

[PAUSE]

## Extended Act: The Forty-Eight Hours

[NARRATOR] The forty-eight hours between the DNA confirmation and the arrest are, in the accounts of everyone involved, both the fastest and the strangest of the entire investigation. Fast because the operational mechanics are complex and every hour before the arrest is an hour of exposure risk. Strange because of the cognitive dissonance of knowing something with certainty while the world around you continues normally. Roland Voss is going to work. He is sitting at his desk. He is reviewing architectural drawings and responding to client emails and doing all the things he does. And the people who are going to arrest him are working within two miles of his office, building the final case package, making calls to families who have waited twelve years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh makes nine calls in those forty-eight hours. Nine calls to nine families. Each one different. Each one calibrated to the specific person she is calling and the specific history she has with them. Some she has spoken to regularly throughout the twelve years. Some she has not spoken to in months. Some she knows very well. One she has never met in person, only communicated with through the family liaison process.

[MARSH] I had thought about those calls for years. Not specific words - you cannot script something like that. But the quality of them. What I wanted them to feel like. I wanted each person to feel, in that moment, that they were being told something by someone who understood what it meant to them specifically. Not a form call. Not a bureaucratic notification. A human being talking to a human being about something that mattered enormously.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I cried three times. I am not embarrassed about it. These are nine families who waited twelve years. What I felt when I made those calls was appropriate to the situation.

[NARRATOR] The calls go out between two in the afternoon and seven in the evening on the day before the arrest. Each family is told the same essential information: there has been a major development. An arrest is being made within twenty-four hours. The evidence is strong. They will be notified again as soon as the arrest is completed and before any public announcement is made. Carol Marsh's cell number is given to each person in case they need to reach her directly.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Several of the families call back within the hour. They have questions she cannot fully answer yet. She answers what she can and says she will have more after the arrest. She stays on the phone as long as each person needs. She does not rush anyone. She does not set a time limit on any of the conversations.

[DONNA] She called at three-fourteen in the afternoon. I know the exact time because I looked at my phone afterward and memorized it. She said the words I had been waiting to hear for twelve years and I could not speak for a full minute. She waited. She did not fill the silence. She just waited for me to be ready.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] When I could speak I asked: who? And she said: I can't tell you that yet. But I want you to know the evidence is strong. This is real. And I want you to know that what you have done over these twelve years has mattered to this investigation. The legislation. The advocacy. The fact that you have never stopped.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I said: it's for Renee. It's always been for Renee.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] She said: I know.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese spends the night before the arrest in the task force operations center. Not because there is specific work to do that requires his presence - there is, but it could be done in the morning. He stays because he cannot go home. He has been working this case for twelve years and the arrest is seventeen hours away and he cannot imagine being anywhere other than the place where the work has been happening.

[REESE] I sat at my desk in the operations center until about two in the morning. I had the thread book open in front of me. Not reading it - I know it by heart. Just having it there. Forty-seven entries across twelve years. I went through them in my head. Entry one. Entry seven. Entry eleven. Entry nineteen. Entry twenty-nine. Thirty-one. All the thin things that accumulated into something.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] At about one-thirty I called my wife and told her everything was fine and I'd be home in a few hours and she should sleep. She said she knew I wouldn't be home in a few hours. She said to make sure I ate something. She knows me. She has always known me better than I know myself.

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh goes home at eleven. She sets an alarm for four-thirty. She does not sleep significantly between eleven and four-thirty, but she is in her own house, in her own bed, in the specific way that being home means something even when sleep is not available.

[MARSH] I have a thing I do before significant operational days. I run through the case in my head from the beginning. Not looking for problems - there are no problems at this point, the case is complete, the operation is planned. I do it as a kind of... acknowledgment. Here is what happened. Here is what it took. Here is where we are now.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] I did that run-through between one and three in the morning. The discovery in December. The markers in the grass. Donna Kelsey's face in the corridor outside the medical examiner's office. The cell cluster lead and the resource request that wasn't granted. The annual reviews. Priscilla and her dog. The internal review summary in my inbox at eleven-thirty on a Tuesday night. Julia Saenz putting two pages on my desk. The commissioner saying: you have it. All of it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] And then I thought about the families. Every name. In order of identification. I went through all nine. And then I got up at four-thirty and I made coffee and I drove to the operations center.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] At five forty-seven in the morning, Roland Voss walks out his front door. He is wearing a gray jacket. He carries his briefcase. He walks toward the train station eight blocks away. The street is quiet. A few cars. A dog being walked by someone in a heavy coat. The normal rhythms of an ordinary morning in a quiet neighborhood.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The arrest teams are in position. The commander has been monitoring Voss's location through the cell data in real time. When Voss passes the midpoint marker, the commander gives the signal.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It takes approximately ninety seconds. Two plainclothes officers approach from the front. Two from behind. The commander identifies the department. Voss is very still for a moment. He does not run. He does not resist. He looks at the officer in front of him with an expression that the arrest report describes as "calm."

## Extended Act: What It Means to Know

[NARRATOR] In the days following the arrest, something happens that the investigators on the task force did not entirely anticipate. They have known for months, in the investigative sense, that Roland Voss is the person they are looking for. They have been building the case, running the surveillance, collecting the evidence, preparing the arrest. They have had certainty of the investigative kind - the certainty that the evidence points unmistakably at one person and that person is Roland Voss.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What they did not fully anticipate was what it would feel like to have the legal certainty. The certainty that comes when the handcuffs are on and the man is in a car and the case that has been hypothetical for twelve years becomes, irrevocably, real.

[MARSH] There is something that happens after a long case resolves to arrest that I have tried to describe and failed to describe adequately, because it doesn't have a name in ordinary language. It is not relief exactly - relief implies the removal of a burden, and the burden is not removed, it is transformed. It is not triumph - triumph implies you were in competition, and I have never thought of this work as competitive. It is something more like: verification. Like twelve years of holding a specific belief without proof finally having the proof arrive, and the proof and the belief being exactly what you expected them to be.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] And alongside that verification is the full weight of what it means. Not for the investigation - that weight is something you carry professionally and you have done so long enough to be able to carry it. For the families. Specifically for the families. Because that call - the one you make to nine people who have been waiting twelve years - is the moment when the investigative reality becomes their reality. And that transition belongs to them in a way it does not belong to you.

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese is at the operations center when the arrest call comes. He is standing beside Carol Marsh, both of them listening to the radio. When the word comes, he does something that surprises him.

[REESE] I sat down. I had been standing and I sat down. I did not plan to sit down. I just needed to sit. I have been in law enforcement for thirty-one years and I have been present for many arrests and I have never needed to sit down after one before. This was different. This was twelve years of something. And when it ended, the energy that had been holding me up for twelve years apparently had somewhere else to go.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] I sat for maybe thirty seconds. And then I stood up and I went back to work. There were things to do. The arrest is not the end of the case. It is the beginning of the prosecution. There was work.

[NARRATOR] Frank Tully receives the confirmation of the arrest in his office at the DA's office. He is on a call with the federal liaison when the notification comes. He finishes the call, puts down the phone, and closes his office door. He is alone in the room for three minutes.

[TULLY] I have handled cases that went to arrest and cases that didn't. Cases that I was certain should resolve to arrest and did not because the evidence wasn't sufficient or because decisions above my pay grade went a different way. This case I was certain about from the moment Carol Marsh showed me the complete evidence package. But certainty about the outcome does not eliminate the tension of waiting for it. When the arrest was confirmed, the tension went somewhere and I needed three minutes to process where it went.

[PAUSE]

[TULLY] And then I called my team in and we started working on the arraignment.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The arraignment is held five days after the arrest. The five days between are spent on two things simultaneously: the formal case preparation and the house search.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The house search runs in parallel with the legal process. A team of twelve forensic investigators works through every room, every storage space, every drawer and cabinet and shelf. They document everything before touching it. They remove items of potential evidentiary value following a strict chain of custody protocol that has been designed, like everything else in this case, to be legally unassailable.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] One of the items removed on day one is the burner phone. The prepaid handset, new in its packaging, found in a drawer in the home office. The moment the search team's communications specialist transmits the hardware identification to the task force operations center, Dr. Park runs the comparison. Forty minutes. The result is immediate and clear.

[NARRATOR] The handset in the drawer is the handset in the cell tower records. The handset that contacted victims. The handset that was in the barrier island corridor on specific dates. It has been in that drawer, in that house, in that neighborhood, through every year of the investigation. While the investigation ran at reduced resource and the tip line went quiet and the press coverage dried up and the families kept waiting - the phone was in the drawer.

[DR. PARK] When the hardware match came back I sent a message to Carol immediately. I said: we have the device. She responded within minutes. She said: make sure it is in the evidence chain correctly. I said: it is. She said: perfect.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Perfect. After twelve years. Perfect.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The week after the arraignment is a strange week for everyone connected to the case. The investigation is continuing - the house search is still ongoing, the digital forensics analysis has barely begun, the evidence review will take months. But the central fact has changed. Roland Voss is not a free man. He is in a cell in Riverhead waiting for the legal process that will take two more years to complete.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The families navigate this period differently. Some feel immediately, clearly better - the certainty that the person responsible is no longer free is a specific and real relief that they can name. Some feel unexpectedly flat - waiting for a feeling that doesn't arrive, or finding that the feeling arrives and is smaller than they expected, or finding that relief and grief are so thoroughly mixed that they cannot separate one from the other.

[DONNA] I expected to feel more than I felt. Not in a disappointed way - I just expected the emotional scale to match the thirteen-year scale of the waiting. And what I actually felt was specific and real but not enormous. It was quiet. Like something had been turned off that had been running in the background for twelve years.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I have a theory about this. The work - the advocacy, the legislation, the network, all of it - had been giving the energy somewhere productive to go for years. The arrest meant the energy no longer needed to go there. And what was left without that channeling was just the grief. Just Renee. Just the ordinary, permanent, daily absence of my sister.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] Which is, in a way, what was always underneath everything else. The work was real and it mattered. But it was always a structure built around an absence. And when the case is closed, the structure is still there, and the absence is still there, and they are the same size they always were.
