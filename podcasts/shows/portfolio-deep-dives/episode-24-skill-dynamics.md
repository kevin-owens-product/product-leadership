**JAMES:** Large corporates with procurement and supply chain functions. Think of the buying teams at a big manufacturer, a retailer, a pharmaceutical company or an energy business. Role-based means the training is organised around those jobs, rather than around topics.

**NORA:** So a new category manager gets a path, not a menu.

**JAMES:** That's the idea. And for the employer, it means they can describe what good looks like for each role and see who's on the way there. That's harder to copy than a single course, because it requires a model of the profession, not just content.

### Why procurement skills matter

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer problem. Why would a company pay for procurement training?

**JAMES:** Because procurement controls a huge share of a company's spending. In many large businesses, the money spent with suppliers is a big part of total costs. So a skilled buyer who negotiates a slightly better price, spots a risky supplier early, or avoids a compliance mistake can be worth a great deal. That's general background, but it's why procurement leaders care about capability.

**NORA:** And what makes it hard?

**JAMES:** Several things. First, the job has changed. Procurement used to be seen mainly as buying at the lowest price. Now it's expected to manage supplier risk, sustainability, resilience, and increasingly to work with AI tools. Skill Dynamics' own blog makes that argument, that procurement is being redefined as strategic. Second, the world has been volatile. Pandemic disruption, shipping problems, tariffs and sanctions all land on the procurement desk.

**NORA:** And third?

**JAMES:** People churn. Buyers move between companies, get promoted, or leave the profession. The company's own releases point to churn as a driver. Every time an experienced buyer leaves, the team loses knowledge, and a newer person has to get up to speed.

**NORA:** So what do companies do today?

**JAMES:** In general, a mix. Some send people for professional qualifications, like those from the Chartered Institute of Procurement and Supply, known as CIPS, which is a well-known professional body in the field. Some buy general e-learning libraries. Some rely on on-the-job learning from senior colleagues. And some buy specialist programmes like Skill Dynamics'.

**NORA:** And how do they know it worked?

**JAMES:** That's the weak spot of the whole industry. Most training is measured by completions. Did the person finish the course? Did they pass the quiz? Skill Dynamics' own report argues that this is the wrong measure, and that training should be judged on outcomes. Did negotiations go better? Were compliance errors avoided?

**NORA:** That's easy to say and hard to measure.

**JAMES:** Very hard. But if a training company can measure outcomes, it changes the conversation from a cost line in the learning budget to an investment in procurement performance.

**NORA:** And AI is changing the procurement job itself, isn't it?

**JAMES:** It is, in general. Procurement software vendors are adding AI that drafts sourcing documents, analyses spend, summarises contracts and flags supplier risk. Some are experimenting with automated negotiation for low-value, routine purchases. So the buyer of the future spends less time on admin and more on judgment, relationships and complex negotiations.

**NORA:** Which means the skills they need shift.

**JAMES:** Exactly. They need to know how to use AI tools well, how to check their output, and how to do the human parts of the job better. That's why it's notable that Skill Dynamics' own survey found AI and automation to be the top skills gap. Whatever you think of vendor surveys, that finding fits the direction of the profession.

**NORA:** So there's a double effect. AI threatens the content business, but it also creates a whole new thing to teach.

**JAMES:** That's the paradox at the heart of this company. AI makes some of what it sells cheaper, and makes some of what customers need more urgent. Teaching procurement teams how to work with AI could be one of its biggest opportunities, and we'll come back to that.

### Inside the product

**NORA:** What does Skill Dynamics actually sell?

**JAMES:** From the public descriptions, three main layers. First, role-based programmes. Instead of a library of disconnected courses, the idea is that a category manager, a buyer, a supply chain planner or a trade compliance specialist gets a structured path for their role. Second, skills assessment. Learners are assessed against a framework of skills, so the company and the employer can see where the gaps are. Third, the platform, which it calls SDX, that delivers the content and the assessments.

**NORA:** And how do the pieces fit together?

**JAMES:** The general logic is: assess, then assign, then learn, then reassess. You assess a team, you see who's strong in negotiation but weak in contract management, for example. You assign learning to fill the gaps. Over time you reassess to show improvement. Clearwater's description of an AI-powered assessment tool that builds tailored learning plans fits that loop.

**NORA:** And the trade compliance content?

**JAMES:** That came largely through Content Enablers, with its hundred and fifty plus courses. It adds a topic area where the content goes out of date fast, because rules change. That's both a challenge, because keeping it current costs money, and an opportunity, because customers need someone to keep them current.

**NORA:** Who's the buyer inside the customer?

**JAMES:** In general, it's often the chief procurement officer or head of supply chain, sometimes working with the learning and development team. The procurement leader cares about capability and results. The learning team cares about the platform, integration with the company's other systems, and reporting. Selling to both is part of the job.

**NORA:** Is it sold per learner?

**JAMES:** We didn't find pricing. In general, corporate training is often sold as an annual subscription based on the number of learners, sometimes with professional services on top for custom programmes. I'd assume something along those lines, but that's an inference.

**NORA:** And how does it fit into the customer's other systems?

**JAMES:** In general, big companies have a learning management system, the central place where employees find and record training, plus HR systems that track roles and performance. A specialist provider usually has to connect into those, so that completions and assessments show up in the employer's records. That integration work is unglamorous but important, because the learning team won't buy something that creates a separate island of data.

**NORA:** And for AI tools, that gets more complicated.

**JAMES:** It does. A role-play session with an avatar produces a lot more data than a quiz score. Transcripts, feedback, scores on different skills. Deciding what flows back into the employer's systems, and what stays private to the learner, is a design question with real privacy implications.

**JAMES:** For corporate training overall, there are two estimates we found, and they disagree. Grand View Research, in a report published in June 2026, puts the global corporate training market at about four hundred and twenty-seven billion dollars in 2025, growing to about seven hundred and seventy-eight billion by 2033, a compound growth rate of nearly eight percent. Research and Markets, in January 2026, puts it at about four hundred and eighteen billion in 2025, growing to about five hundred and forty-one billion by 2030, at around five percent a year.

**NORA:** So similar starting points, different growth.

**JAMES:** Right, so treat the range as wide. And importantly, that's all corporate training: technical, compliance, soft skills, sales and more. We didn't find a reliable size for procurement and supply chain training specifically. Skill Dynamics plays in a small, specialised slice of that big number.

**NORA:** And competitors?

**JAMES:** The one we could verify in our sources is Skillsoft, a large enterprise learning company with a broad catalogue. According to a summary of its release, it launched a personalised AI coach on its Percipio platform in early access in August 2026. Skillsoft is horizontal, meaning it covers many topics, not procurement specifically.

**NORA:** Others?

**JAMES:** From general knowledge, and we didn't verify these in our sources. CIPS, the professional body, whose qualifications compete for the same development budgets. LinkedIn Learning, owned by Microsoft, a broad low-cost library. Coursera and Udemy Business, big online learning marketplaces. And various trade compliance training specialists, the space Content Enablers came from.

**NORA:** And then there's the competitor that isn't a company.

**JAMES:** General AI. That's the one I'd watch most closely. A buyer can now ask a general AI assistant to explain incoterms, draft a negotiation plan or summarise sanctions rules, for free or for the price of a subscription they already have. That doesn't replace structured training, but it does reduce the perceived value of a content library.

**NORA:** So how does a specialist defend itself?

**JAMES:** In my view, three ways. Depth, meaning content and scenarios specific to procurement that general tools don't do well. Measurement, meaning assessments and data that show capability improving. And practice, meaning realistic rehearsal that a general chatbot doesn't offer out of the box. Notice that the AI suite is aimed at the second and third.

**NORA:** How does a procurement leader actually choose between these options?

**JAMES:** In general, it often comes down to a few questions. Is it specific to our people's jobs, or generic? Can we see who has improved? Does it connect to our learning systems? Is it recognised, in the way a professional qualification is? And what does it cost per person compared with the library we already pay for?

**NORA:** And the professional qualification is a tough competitor on recognition.

**JAMES:** It is. A qualification travels with the person and appears on their CV. A vendor programme mostly lives inside the employer. Specialists tend to position themselves as complementary, focused on applied skills inside the job rather than a credential. That's a reasonable position, but it means the specialist has to prove impact on the job, which brings us right back to outcomes.

**JAMES:** Usually, yes. Though I'd say the AI and tariff findings are consistent with what you'd expect from general news. Procurement teams have had a rough few years, and AI is arriving in their tools whether they're ready or not.

### The AI Learning Suite

**NORA:** Let's get to the launch. What did they announce?

**JAMES:** On the eighth of July 2026, Skill Dynamics launched an AI Learning Suite inside its SDX platform. Three tools. Coach is AI avatar role-play, for practising things like supplier negotiations and compliance disputes. Pulse turns material into AI-generated podcast-style audio and PDF insights. And Expert is a chat assistant grounded in the company's own content library.

**NORA:** Is it available now?

**JAMES:** The release said wider market availability would come later in 2026, and no pricing was given. So as we record in November, we'd describe it as launched, with general availability expected around now, but we haven't seen confirmation of pricing or broad rollout. It was also covered in the trade press, including SupplyChainBrain.

**NORA:** Take them one at a time. Coach first.

**JAMES:** Coach is the most interesting, in my view. The idea is that a learner sits down with an AI avatar playing, say, a supplier's sales director, and practises the negotiation. The avatar pushes back, makes arguments, maybe refuses to move on price. The learner tries different approaches. Afterwards, presumably, they get feedback.

**NORA:** Like a flight simulator for negotiation.

**JAMES:** That's a good analogy. Pilots don't learn emergencies by reading about them. They practise in a simulator. Negotiators have traditionally practised with role-plays in workshops, which are expensive, infrequent and awkward. An AI role-play partner available any time could make practice far more frequent.

**NORA:** Pulse?

**JAMES:** Pulse is about format. Turning content into audio and short summaries, so people can learn on a commute. Useful, but in my view, the least differentiated of the three, because many tools now do that.

**NORA:** And Expert?

**JAMES:** Expert is a chat assistant that answers questions using the company's content library. The key word is grounded. Instead of a general chatbot that might invent an answer, it's meant to draw on curated procurement and compliance material. That's valuable if the content is good and the answers point back to sources.

**NORA:** Is anyone else doing this?

**JAMES:** Yes, and quickly. Skillsoft's AI coach came out in early access within weeks of Skill Dynamics' launch. So AI role-play and coaching is becoming a product category across corporate learning. That's validation that the idea is right, and a warning that it won't stay unique for long.

**NORA:** Any other public evidence of AI at the company?

**JAMES:** The Clearwater mention of an AI-powered assessment tool, and the private equity firm describing the company as AI-enhanced. We found no AI acquisitions, no named AI hires and no AI partnerships. And we didn't find a published AI governance or security statement, though we didn't check every trust page.

**NORA:** Do we know how they built it? In-house, or on someone else's models?

**JAMES:** We don't. The release doesn't say which underlying AI models or avatar technology are used. In general, companies of this size usually build on large models from the major AI providers and on specialist avatar and voice vendors, and put their own content, scenarios and feedback logic on top. If that's the case here, the differentiation isn't the model. It's the scenarios, the scoring and the procurement expertise behind the feedback.

**NORA:** Which is fine, as long as they're honest about it.

**JAMES:** It's not just fine, it's sensible. Building your own large model would be a poor use of money for a training company. But it does mean costs and capabilities depend partly on suppliers, and that's worth managing. A procurement training company, of all businesses, should be good at negotiating with its AI suppliers.

### A negotiation rehearsal, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make Coach concrete. Walk me through it. Invented example.

**JAMES:** Let's go back to our buyer from the cold open. Call her Elena. She's a category manager at a mid-sized European manufacturer, responsible for a family of machined components. Her biggest supplier has announced a twelve percent price increase, citing tariffs. She has a call on Thursday.

**NORA:** What does she do today, without the tool?

**JAMES:** In a typical company, she reads the supplier's letter, pulls the spend data, maybe asks her manager for advice. She might remember a negotiation module from a course two years ago. She writes some notes. On Thursday she goes in, and the supplier's sales director, who does this every week, sets the pace. Elena concedes eight percent and feels she could have done better, but she isn't sure what she'd have done differently.

**NORA:** And with Coach?

**JAMES:** On Tuesday, Elena opens Coach and sets up a scenario: supplier price increase, tariff justification, long relationship, alternative suppliers exist but switching is slow. The AI avatar plays the sales director. In the first run, the avatar opens hard: the increase is non-negotiable, everyone's paying it. Elena accepts the framing too quickly and starts negotiating the number down.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** At the end, she gets feedback. Something like: you didn't ask what share of the increase is actually tariff-related, you didn't mention the volume commitment you could offer, and you made the first concession. She runs it again. This time she asks for the cost breakdown, separates the tariff element from the rest, and proposes a phased increase linked to a longer contract.

**NORA:** And she can do it a third time.

**JAMES:** As many as she likes. And the trade compliance angle comes in too. Elena asks Expert whether the tariff the supplier is citing actually applies to these components, given their origin. Expert answers from the compliance content and points her to the relevant course module. She realises part of the supplier's justification may not apply.

**NORA:** So on Thursday she walks in prepared.

**JAMES:** Better prepared. She knows the questions to ask and she's practised her responses. Maybe she settles at five percent with a longer contract, rather than eight. In this invented example, that difference on a large spend category could be worth far more than the training costs.

**NORA:** That's the outcome story.

**JAMES:** Exactly, and it's the story Skill Dynamics needs to be able to tell with real customers. Not "Elena completed three modules", but "Elena's team negotiated better outcomes after using Coach". If it can measure that, even roughly, the product sells itself.

**NORA:** Let me push back. Can an avatar really play a tough negotiator?

**JAMES:** Fair challenge. It won't be as good as an experienced human coach. But it's available at eleven at night, never gets tired, and can be set to be difficult. The question is whether it's good enough to make practice useful, and whether the feedback is accurate. That's what customers will judge in the first year.

**NORA:** Let's do a compliance example too. Same company, different person.

**JAMES:** Sure. Imagine Elena's colleague, Tomasz, who handles trade compliance for imports. On a Monday morning, news breaks that a new set of sanctions or tariff measures will apply to certain goods from a particular country in thirty days. Tomasz has to work out which of his company's purchases are affected, what documentation changes, and what to tell the buyers.

**NORA:** And today?

**JAMES:** Today he reads the official notices, which are long and technical, checks industry newsletters, maybe calls an external adviser, and builds a spreadsheet. Meanwhile, four buyers email him asking, does this affect my supplier? He's the one person in the company who really knows this area. Remember the company's own survey finding that only seven percent of organisations say trade compliance expertise is well spread.

**NORA:** He's the bottleneck.

**JAMES:** He's the bottleneck. In the better world, the training provider's compliance content is updated quickly, with AI drafting a summary of the change and experts reviewing it before it's published. A short module goes out to the relevant buyers. And Expert, the assistant, can answer the routine questions, like what does this change mean for goods classified under this heading, with a link to the updated material and a clear note to check with Tomasz for specific decisions.

**NORA:** So Tomasz handles the hard cases, not the hundredth repeat of the same question.

**JAMES:** Exactly. That's the value. Not replacing the expert, but spreading the basics so the expert can focus. And I want to stress, for a regulated area like sanctions, the assistant should never be the final word. It's a guide to the material, with a human accountable for decisions.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's talk challenges.

**JAMES:** The biggest is the one we started with. General AI reduces willingness to pay for content libraries. If a company's revenue has historically been based on access to content, and content becomes cheap, prices come under pressure. That's our inference, but the chief executive's own framing suggests the company sees it too.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Monetisation of the AI tools. The release positions them as complementary to the core programmes. That's sensible for adoption, but it raises the question of how they're priced. Are they bundled in, which helps retention but adds cost? Or sold as a premium, which adds revenue but needs a clear value story? We don't know yet.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Competition from horizontal platforms. Skillsoft and others can bundle AI coaching into big enterprise deals covering every topic. A procurement leader might get "good enough" negotiation practice as part of a company-wide learning contract. Skill Dynamics has to be clearly better for procurement specifically.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Integration and focus. A buy-and-build strategy, funded by a refinancing, means more acquisitions to integrate, more content to align, and more platform work, all while building new AI products. That stretches a mid-sized team.

**NORA:** And the cost of running AI?

**JAMES:** That's a real one, in general. Avatar role-play with voice and video, and chat assistants used heavily, cost money every time they run. Content libraries are cheap to serve once built. AI features have ongoing compute costs. The pricing has to cover that.

**NORA:** Anything about trust?

**JAMES:** Yes. Corporates will ask where their data goes, especially when learners describe real supplier situations to an AI coach. Elena might type in real prices and supplier names. We didn't find public statements on AI governance or security certifications. For enterprise buyers, that's increasingly a requirement, not a nice-to-have.

**NORA:** And visibility generally?

**JAMES:** Revenue, margin and growth aren't public, so we can't test the economics. And the headquarters question, small as it is, shows how hard it can be to get a clear picture from outside.

**NORA:** What about making content in the first place? Has AI changed that?

**JAMES:** It's changed it a lot across the industry, in general. Drafting scripts, generating quizzes, producing video with synthetic presenters, translating. That lowers the cost of producing content for everyone, including competitors and customers' own learning teams. So a company like Skill Dynamics gets cheaper production, but so does everyone else. The advantage only lasts if its subject experts and scenarios are better.

**NORA:** So the cost advantage gets competed away.

**JAMES:** Largely, in my view. The durable advantage is the expertise, the role models of the profession, the assessments and the customer relationships. Not the production cost.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Okay, opportunities. Where would you push?

**JAMES:** First, make Coach a product in its own right, for negotiation and compliance practice, priced per seat, and tied to measured outcomes. The company's own argument is that completions are the wrong metric. So build the metric. Track practice sessions, assessment improvements and, with customer permission, negotiation results.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Use Expert internally and externally to cut support costs. Many learner questions are routine: where do I find this, what does this term mean, which course covers that. A grounded assistant can answer them and free up the team for higher-value work.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Use AI to update and localise content faster. Trade compliance rules change with sanctions and tariffs. Keeping a hundred and fifty plus courses current, in several languages, is expensive. AI can draft updates and translations for experts to review. That's a cost saving and a speed advantage.

**NORA:** And growth?

**JAMES:** Cross-selling trade compliance into procurement accounts, and the reverse. If sixty percent of organisations in the company's survey were hit by tariffs, there's an obvious conversation to have with every procurement customer.

**NORA:** Acquisitions?

**JAMES:** The refinancing signals capacity. Natural targets might be skills assessment specialists, or training in adjacent functions like sales negotiation, contract management or supplier risk. But each one adds integration work.

**NORA:** You said you'd come back to teaching AI itself.

**JAMES:** Yes. If the company's own survey is right that AI and automation is the top skills gap, then a programme on AI for procurement is an obvious product. How to use AI in sourcing and spend analysis. How to check AI output. How to negotiate with a supplier who is using AI. How to buy AI software and manage its risks, which is itself a procurement task. That's content general tools can't easily replace, because it's about judgment in a specific profession.

**NORA:** And it's timely.

**JAMES:** Very. Every procurement leader is being asked by their chief executive what they're doing about AI. A credible, role-based programme with assessment gives them an answer. And it fits neatly with Coach, because you can practise working with AI tools in realistic scenarios.

**NORA:** Geography?

**JAMES:** The customer base is global, but the team appears to be centred on the UK and Europe. The United States and Asia-Pacific could be growth areas, though we don't know current revenue mix.

**NORA:** And the data?

**JAMES:** In my view, an underrated opportunity. Skills assessment across hundreds of companies, aggregated and anonymised with proper consent, could become a benchmark. A chief procurement officer would love to know how their team's skills compare with peers. That's a product, and it's one general AI can't copy, because the data is proprietary.

### The best AI bet and the first 100 days

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet?

**JAMES:** Coach, for supplier negotiation, sold on outcomes. It sells something that content can't: practice. It already exists. It fits the company's core audience perfectly. And it can be measured, which is the key to pricing.

**NORA:** How would you run the next hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: pick five to ten design partner customers with active negotiation programmes and procurement leaders who care about measurement. Agree what success looks like: practice frequency, assessment scores before and after, and a simple, privacy-respecting measure of negotiation outcomes, even if it's self-reported to start with.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Deploy Coach to those teams with a handful of high-value scenarios. Tariff-driven price increases, single-source supplier risk, contract renewals. Collect feedback on realism and on the quality of the AI's feedback. Have experienced negotiators review a sample of sessions to check the coaching is sound. And settle the data rules: what learners can type in, where it's stored, how long it's kept.

**NORA:** Sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Compare the Coach users with similar teams who didn't use it. Look at assessment scores and any outcome data. Then decide pricing: a premium tier, a per-seat add-on, or an outcome-linked package. At day one hundred, publish an anonymised case study with customer permission. Nothing sells corporate training like a peer saying "this worked for us".

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** One product leader, with the authority to pull in content, technology and sales. And a customer-facing success lead for the design partners.

**NORA:** What could go wrong?

**JAMES:** Two things. If the AI feedback is generic or wrong, experienced buyers will dismiss it quickly. And if the tool is bundled for free to win renewals without any pricing discipline, it adds cost without adding revenue. The test, in my view, is whether these tools drive renewals and higher prices, or just become a discount in disguise.

**NORA:** Let me do a second quick walk-through, from the customer's side. A procurement director deciding whether to renew.

**JAMES:** Sure, invented again. Call him Martin, chief procurement officer at a consumer goods company with a hundred and twenty buyers. His Skill Dynamics renewal is coming up. His finance director asks: what did we get for this money? Today, Martin can show completion rates and some assessment scores. That's a soft answer.

**NORA:** And in the better world?

**JAMES:** In the better world, Martin can show that his team's negotiation scores improved after a quarter of Coach practice, that his category managers ran a hundred and fifty practice sessions before major supplier talks, and that, by their own reporting, they achieved better terms on several big contracts. He can also show that the compliance gap identified in the assessment was closed for the team handling imports. That's a renewal conversation about performance, not about a library.

**NORA:** And what does that do to the price conversation?

**JAMES:** It changes it completely. If the only evidence is completions, the finance director asks for a discount, or suggests switching to a cheaper general library. If the evidence is better negotiation results, the question becomes whether to extend it to more people. In our invented example, Martin might even ask to add the trade compliance programme for his logistics team.

**NORA:** So the AI tool becomes the reason for the upsell, not the reason for the discount.

**JAMES:** That's the whole game. And it's worth saying, a lot of companies listening will recognise this pattern. Whatever you sell, the renewal conversation is easier when you can show the customer what changed because of you. AI makes that measurement cheaper to collect, if you design for it from the beginning.

**NORA:** Is there a privacy issue with tracking negotiation results?

**JAMES:** There can be. Employees may not like the idea that every practice session is scored and reported to their boss. A sensible design separates private practice, where the learner sees their own feedback, from aggregated team reporting, where the manager sees trends. Getting that balance right affects whether people actually use the tool.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Widen it out. Lessons for other businesses?

**JAMES:** Four. First, if your product is information, AI is coming for your pricing. The response isn't to deny it. It's to move towards things AI makes more valuable, like practice, assessment, data and outcomes. Skill Dynamics' own chief executive essentially said this.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, launch AI as a complement, then prove it can stand alone. Positioning new tools as supplements reduces risk with existing customers. But at some point, you need to show the AI product earns its own revenue.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, measure outcomes, not activity. Training companies have lived on completion rates for decades. Any business, whether it sells software, services or training, is stronger when it can show the customer's result.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, speed matters in a new category. Skill Dynamics and Skillsoft launched AI coaching within weeks of each other. When a category forms that quickly, the advantage goes to whoever builds the best proof with customers first, not whoever made the first announcement.

**NORA:** Any lesson for a small consultancy or training provider listening?

**JAMES:** Yes. Your expertise is the asset, not your slides. Use AI to give clients more practice and more personal feedback between sessions. That's something they'll pay for that a free chatbot doesn't provide.

**NORA:** One more lesson?

**JAMES:** A fifth one, about the customer you sell to. Know how your buyer evaluates vendors. Skill Dynamics sells to procurement people, who are professionally sceptical and trained to ask for evidence. That's a high bar, but it's also an advantage. If you can satisfy the toughest buyer in the company, your case studies carry weight everywhere else.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI disruption to the content business, which we've covered. Two, horizontal platforms bundling AI coaching into big deals. Three, budget pressure. The company's own survey says budgets remain low, and tariff disruption could cut training spend as well as raise the need for it. When margins are squeezed, training is often one of the first lines to be cut.

**NORA:** Four?

**JAMES:** Leverage and timing. A refinancing that funds more acquisitions adds debt, and the owner has held the business for about six and a half years. That raises the stakes on execution. If the AI tools don't show traction in the next year or two, the story at a sale is weaker.

**NORA:** And AI-specific risks?

**JAMES:** Quality and trust. A coach that gives bad negotiation advice, or an assistant that misstates a sanctions rule, could cause real harm. In compliance, a wrong answer isn't just embarrassing. So grounding, source citation, human review of content, and clear governance matter a lot.

**NORA:** Is there a regulatory angle?

**JAMES:** Potentially, and I'll keep this general rather than legal. The European Union's AI Act places extra obligations on some AI systems used in education and in employment decisions. Whether a given training or assessment tool falls into those categories depends on how it's used, for example whether assessment results feed decisions about people's jobs. A company selling AI assessment and coaching to European employers needs to understand where its products sit and be ready to explain that to customers.

**NORA:** And customers will ask.

**JAMES:** Large customers increasingly do. Procurement teams, ironically, are the people who run supplier due diligence on AI vendors. Skill Dynamics is selling AI to the very function that writes the AI vendor questionnaires. It needs excellent answers.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, general availability and pricing of the AI Learning Suite. Second, customer evidence: named case studies or outcome data for Coach. Third, the next acquisition funded by the refinancing, and whether it strengthens the outcome story, like assessment or analytics, or just adds more content.

**NORA:** And a warning sign?

**JAMES:** If the AI tools end up bundled for free in every renewal without any change in price or retention. That would suggest they're defending the base rather than growing it.
