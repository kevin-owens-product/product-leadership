### THE Q6 BOARD PACK

**ASHA:** Six metrics. No blended comfort. First, portfolio ARR at Q6: three hundred ninety-nine million dollars. Second, portfolio LTM EBITDA: sixty-three million. Third, portfolio net debt: two hundred fifty-five million. Fourth, fund paid-in capital: eight hundred million. Fifth, uncalled capital: two hundred million. Sixth, Relay's headline entry multiple: nine times reported EBITDA, but fourteen point two times normalized EBITDA.

**COLE:** The positive number that needs interrogation is the nine times. Fifty-four divided by six equals nine. The arithmetic is flawless. The denominator is not yet underwritten.

**ASHA:** The portfolio numbers need interrogation too. At Q6, Keystone is one hundred forty million of ARR, thirty-four million of EBITDA, and one hundred sixty-one million of net debt. Vela is one hundred seventy million of ARR, twelve million of EBITDA, and thirty-eight million of net debt. Aegis is eighty-nine million of ARR, seventeen million of EBITDA, and fifty-six million of net debt.

**COLE:** Add them and you get three hundred ninety-nine of ARR, sixty-three of EBITDA, and two hundred fifty-five of debt. But Relay would not be acquired by a mathematical average. Keystone would own it. Keystone's leadership would integrate it. Keystone's borrower would carry any acquisition financing. Vela's EBITDA cannot absorb a Keystone miss. Aegis's cash cannot cure a Keystone covenant.

**ASHA:** The same separation applies to fund reserves. Northstar has two hundred million of uncalled capital at Q6. That is not two hundred million sitting in Keystone's bank account. Calling twenty million requires an investment decision. Once called, it becomes paid-in capital and changes the fund denominator. It also consumes optionality that cannot be spent again.

**COLE:** Which is why the right board pack contains both the asset and the fund. Relay may be affordable at fifty-four million. The portfolio may have two hundred million left. Neither fact proves Relay is the best use of the next dollar.

**ASHA:** One more distinction. Enterprise value is the price of the operating business before considering how it is financed. Fifty-four million is not necessarily fifty-four million of sponsor equity. You can fund some with new debt and some with fund equity. Financing changes who bears risk. It does not make the purchase price smaller.

**COLE:** And reported EBITDA is not cash. Seller adjustments and revenue whose continuation depends on one customer can overstate durable earnings. Separately, favorable working-capital timing can flatter cash conversion without changing EBITDA. Before we call Relay cheap, we need to find out what the six million is made of and how it turns into cash.

### WHY RELAY LOOKS RIGHT

**COLE:** Start with the strategic evidence in favor. Relay's field adjusters collect photographs, repair estimates, policy evidence, and witness notes. Keystone receives many of those artifacts later, often as unstructured attachments. A clean connection could shorten the path from first notice to a review-ready claim file.

**ASHA:** Customer interviews support the workflow. Three Keystone insurers say adjuster evidence arrives inconsistently and creates rework. Two Relay customers say Keystone's document and correspondence layer is stronger than Relay's. A combined service could reduce duplicate intake, improve exception routing, and give supervisors one accountable queue.

**COLE:** Product diligence also finds useful assets. Relay's mobile capture works offline. Its evidence checklist is configurable by claim type. Its adjuster network has response-time data by region. Those are real capabilities, not a generic artificial-intelligence story.

**ASHA:** Commercially, Keystone and Relay have adjacent customer sets but little direct contract overlap. That creates plausible cross-sell. Relay's field-adjusting operation could also supply labeled workflow outcomes that improve Keystone's evidence triage, provided the contracts actually permit that purpose.

**COLE:** There it is: provided. A strategic fit is a mechanism with dependencies. The workflow must integrate. Customers must consent where required. Identity and claim permissions must map. Product teams must preserve the evidence chain. Operations must handle more volume. Sales must reach the right buyers. Until those dependencies clear, "fit" is not a number in the return model.

**ASHA:** Relay's management argues speed is the advantage. Buy now, consolidate back office, cross-sell the installed base, and use Keystone's agent workflow to reduce adjuster administration. They project five million dollars of annual run-rate synergy within eighteen months.

**COLE:** That five million creates a seductive sentence: fifty-four million divided by eleven million of pro forma EBITDA is less than five times. But that calculation adds six million of reported Relay EBITDA to five million of forecast synergy and treats all eleven as if it exists on closing day.

**ASHA:** It also ignores Keystone's own cost and productivity plan. Two million of Relay's proposed five million comes from claims workflow automation already present in Keystone's turnaround forecast. Counting it again would let one initiative support two valuations.

**COLE:** Another one point four million assumes immediate vendor and management overlap. Some of those contracts cannot be canceled for a year. Some roles own customer relationships that cannot safely disappear at close. The remaining one point six million is cross-sell contribution described as revenue. It has not been reduced for implementation, sales commission, support, or churn.

**ASHA:** Strategic logic survives that correction. The synergy valuation does not. That is exactly what diligence is supposed to do: preserve the reason to care while removing the reasons to overpay.

### THE QUALITY-OF-EARNINGS BRIDGE

**ASHA:** Relay reports six million dollars of adjusted EBITDA. Quality of earnings asks a narrower question: what earnings power is supported by the historical ledger and likely to recur under the buyer's ownership?

**COLE:** The diligence team does not begin with the six. It begins with revenue by customer and month, payroll, contractor expense, technology cost, insurance, facilities, and the general ledger. Then it rebuilds the adjustment schedule.

**ASHA:** Two point two million comes out. The largest item is labor presented as temporary. Relay moved experienced adjusters from payroll to a contractor pool during a claims surge, then added back part of the contractor premium as unusual. The surge may be unusual. The work required to serve contracted customers is not.

**COLE:** A second item is a vendor rebate recognized during the measured period. The cash was real. It is not recurring operating performance. A third is vacant-role savings. Management adds back salaries for roles it says will not be refilled, while service-level data shows the remaining team is already missing review targets.

**ASHA:** A fourth item is an owner-related expense that can legitimately be normalized, but the seller replaces it with no cost for a professional leader. The buyer may remove the owner expense. It still needs someone to run the business.

**COLE:** After those corrections, normalized LTM EBITDA is three point eight million. Fifty-four divided by three point eight equals fourteen point two times, rounded. The target did not become worse because the spreadsheet changed. The price became visible.

**ASHA:** Normalized does not mean guaranteed. Three point eight is still exposed to customer concentration, consent risk, wage inflation, and integration cost. It is a better starting denominator, not a promise.

**COLE:** Now look at working capital. Relay bills some customers after catastrophe-response milestones. The latest quarter benefited from collection of older receivables while contractor payments lagged. That improves cash temporarily without increasing EBITDA. A buyer who sees only recent cash conversion could mistake timing for durable economics.

**ASHA:** And remember the evidence labels. Six million is seller-reported adjusted EBITDA. Three point eight is diligence-adjusted normalized EBITDA. Neither is Keystone's underwritten post-close EBITDA. That third number must include customer risk, standalone cost, integration cost, and only the synergy supported by a mechanism and owner.

**COLE:** If you let those three numbers share the word EBITDA without the label, the highest one will quietly win every meeting.

### FORTY PERCENT CAN BE THE WHOLE DEAL

**ASHA:** Relay's largest customer contributes forty percent of revenue. Concentration does not automatically make an acquisition wrong. It changes the shape of diligence and the price of uncertainty.

**COLE:** The relationship is six years old. Usage is high. Operational contacts are positive. That is evidence for persistence.

**ASHA:** The contract has nine months left at the expected closing date. Pricing resets at renewal. The customer has not agreed to the proposed change of control. Procurement is actively reviewing its supplier roster. That is evidence against treating permanence as a fact.

**COLE:** Relay management says the customer has never left and has nowhere else to go. That is not a retention analysis. It is a story about the past.

**ASHA:** Build the downside. If the customer leaves, revenue falls sharply. Some field labor flexes down, but central supervision, technology, insurance, and regional coverage do not fall in proportion. Normalized EBITDA does not decline by forty percent. It can disappear.

**COLE:** The same customer also contributes a large share of Relay's most useful evidence data. Losing the contract could remove both economics and the legal basis for continued use. Concentration therefore affects revenue, margin, product value, and data rights at once.

**ASHA:** A buyer can respond in several ways. Require renewal before close. Use a contingent payment. Reduce price. Obtain customer consent. Or walk. What the buyer cannot do is acknowledge the risk verbally and leave the full customer value in the base case.

**COLE:** The seller offers an earn-out tied to retention. That improves alignment, but it does not solve service continuity. If the customer leaves, the lower price is some compensation. It does not restore the leadership time Keystone spent, the debt it raised, or the product work it delayed.

**ASHA:** This is why a concentration adjustment is not simply probability times revenue. You need the nonlinear operating consequence. Which costs flex? Which capabilities remain useful? Which debts remain? What else breaks?

### DATA RIGHTS ARE PART OF THE PRODUCT

**COLE:** The teaser calls Relay's claim evidence proprietary. The contract review says something narrower. Relay may process the evidence to deliver adjusting services. Several agreements restrict reuse. Key permissions do not assign automatically in a change of control. Customer consent is required.

**ASHA:** Nonassignable means Keystone cannot assume the permission transfers because it buys the company. Depending on the contract and transaction structure, a consent may be required before Relay can continue the same use, expand the use, or place the data inside Keystone's workflow.

**COLE:** This is not a semantic legal issue outside product. The integration thesis requires claims, identities, evidence, and actions to pass across a new boundary. If the rights do not cross, the product cannot cross as designed.

**ASHA:** The team maps each important dataset by source, customer, purpose, permitted use, retention period, assignability, and consent. The result is not binary. Some data can support the current tenant-contained service after consent. Some can support operational analytics. Very little can support pooled model improvement without new terms.

**COLE:** So the purchase model assigns no value to a universal claims-data moat. It can still value the software, network, workflows, and customer relationships. Removing an unsupported asset from the thesis does not mean the rest is worthless.

**ASHA:** It does mean the customer-consent work becomes a closing dependency, not a Day One checklist item. The most important consents touch the concentrated account. That couples two major risks.

**COLE:** And consent has a customer cost. You have to explain what changes, why data moves, who controls it, and what the customer receives. A plan that says "obtain consents" without an owner, message, timeline, and refusal path is not a plan.

### THE INTEGRATION FACTORY TEST

**COLE:** A roll-up machine needs repeatable work, not repeated announcements. Northstar creates an integration-capacity model with five production lines: leadership, customer and revenue, finance and controls, identity and security, and product and data.

**ASHA:** Each line has throughput. How many customer migrations can happen safely per month? How many legal entities can finance close? How many identity domains can security absorb? How many product interfaces can teams build without missing core commitments? How many leadership transitions can Priya supervise?

**COLE:** At Q6, four lines are constrained. Priya is still Keystone's interim CEO. Her first job is to complete the turnaround, not become a roaming integration office. Keystone's customer team is repairing retention. Its data team is finishing workflow redesign. Its finance team is still improving cash and covenant visibility.

**ASHA:** There is no spare integration leader. The proposed answer is to appoint a program manager from Northstar. But a central program manager can maintain a plan. They cannot own Keystone customer decisions, approve claim-workflow changes, or replace a company executive with authority over resources.

**COLE:** That distinction matters. Templates reduce setup time. They do not create decision rights. A Day One checklist can tell you to unify privileged access. Someone still has to understand which access is safe to revoke without stopping claim operations.

**ASHA:** The capacity model uses load, not sentiment. Keystone's leadership line is already at one hundred percent committed capacity. Customer and revenue is at ninety-two percent. Product and data is at eighty-eight. Identity and security is at eighty-five. The model reserves surge capacity because incidents and customer escalations do not book appointments.

**COLE:** Relay would require a dedicated integration executive, customer-consent team, finance conversion, identity remediation, and workflow mapping in the first hundred days. The plan has named tasks. It does not have named people with free capacity.

**ASHA:** Management proposes doing it faster. Faster than available capacity is not speed. It is queue growth. Work waits, quality falls, escalations rise, and the operator discovers the bottleneck after customers do.

**COLE:** This is the portfolio version of technical debt. Integration debt accumulates when acquisitions close faster than systems, contracts, roles, and customer ownership converge. The next target inherits the unfinished work from the last one.

### ACQUISITION COHORTS, NOT TROPHIES

**ASHA:** Programmatic M&A is often described by frequency: several small deals instead of one large deal. Frequency is an output. The machine starts with a thesis and a cohort ledger.

**COLE:** Northstar's funnel groups targets by the job they add, not by industry label. Workflow adjacency. Customer access. Data or rights asset. Geographic capability. Talent. Cost scale. Every target needs one primary job and a small number of secondary benefits.

**ASHA:** Then the cohort scorecard. Entry multiple on verified earnings. Revenue quality. Gross retention. Concentration. Rights transfer. Security readiness. Integration effort. Leadership dependency. Capital source. Time to first proof. And performance against the original case after close.

**COLE:** The purpose is not to create one score that hides the risks. Some fields are gates. If required rights cannot transfer, the target does not pass because its weighted average looks attractive. If no accountable integration executive exists, timing does not pass because the purchase price is low.

**ASHA:** Acquisitions also need spacing rules. No second close while the prior deal has unresolved critical access, unreconciled ARR, unnamed renewals, or integration spending outside the approved envelope. Pacing follows demonstrated throughput.

**COLE:** And the cohort ledger protects learning. If three acquired companies all miss cross-sell, stop calling cross-sell a base-case synergy. If identity migration consistently takes twice the plan, change capacity assumptions before the next deal. If leaders leave after earn-outs, redesign retention and succession.

**ASHA:** A machine should make the next decision better. It should not make the next decision automatic.

**COLE:** McKinsey's *How Private Equity Is Using M&A Integrations to Overcome Headwinds*, published May eighth, 2026, describes roll-ups as dependent on integrating core processes, retaining talent, and investing in cross-functional M&A capability. It also warns that buying at low multiples without doing the integration work can turn expansion into dilution. That is experience-based guidance, not causal proof or a Relay valuation benchmark. Here it sharpens one question: does Keystone have the capacity the strategy requires?

**ASHA:** Exactly. A current market report can describe pressure. It cannot decide whether a customer consent transfers or whether Keystone has an integration leader.

### SOURCES, USES, AND OPPORTUNITY COST

**ASHA:** Now finance the hypothetical acquisition so the risk cannot hide inside the word affordable.

**COLE:** Relay's enterprise value is fifty-four million. Assume the business is delivered debt-free and cash-free. Northstar considers twenty million of new fund equity and thirty-four million of new Keystone debt for the purchase price.

**ASHA:** Fees and first-year integration cash add another seven million, funded from Keystone cash and its revolver in the downside. Net debt would rise from one hundred sixty-one million at Q6 to roughly two hundred two million before post-close cash generation.

**COLE:** Keystone has thirty-four million of Q6 LTM EBITDA. Add Relay's normalized three point eight, not the seller's six and not forecast synergy. Pro forma EBITDA is thirty-seven point eight million. Two hundred two divided by thirty-seven point eight is about five point three times net leverage, versus Keystone's standalone four point seven times.

**ASHA:** The acquisition makes leverage worse on the underwritten denominator. Using reported EBITDA would make the ratio look closer to five times. Using forecast synergy could make it look lower still. Neither can service debt at close.

**COLE:** Fund math changes too. Paid-in capital would move from eight hundred to eight hundred twenty million. Uncalled capital would fall from two hundred to one hundred eighty. That is exactly the same fund call available for Vela's organic scale plan. The choices compete.

**ASHA:** Vela's request is not risk free. It wants to fund European implementation capacity, partner enablement, model routing, and working capital after the regional cell passed its launch gates. But its evidence is current. At Q6 it has one hundred seventy million of ARR, twelve million of EBITDA, and thirty-eight million of net debt. Its country economics now reflect the higher inference cost and narrower workflow from Episode Fourteen.

**COLE:** Relay's base case depends on adjusted earnings, a concentrated renewal, consents, an absent leader, and synergies. Vela's case depends on scaling a bounded system that has already produced contracted demand and contribution evidence. One is not certain. One has a shorter path to disproof.

**ASHA:** Opportunity cost belongs in every acquisition memo. The question is not whether Relay can produce a positive return. It is whether Relay produces a better risk-adjusted return than the best other use of capital, management attention, covenant capacity, and time.

### THE DECISION GATE

[MUSIC STING]

**ASHA:** Three credible choices, Kevin.

**COLE:** Choice one: buy Relay into Keystone at fifty-four million of enterprise value. Fund twenty million with sponsor equity and thirty-four million with new debt, then absorb about seven million of fees and integration cash. You gain an adjacent claims workflow, customer access, mobile evidence capture, and a potential operating network. You accept fourteen point two times normalized EBITDA before integration cost, one customer at forty percent of revenue, uncertain consent timing, and no free integration leader.

**ASHA:** Choice two: create a central Northstar roll-up platform and buy Relay there. A central team could standardize diligence, controls, procurement, and reporting, while supplying services to Keystone. You preserve Keystone's immediate management focus. But you create a fourth operating perimeter, unclear customer accountability, related-party pricing, a new data boundary, and a central team that still does not own the company workflow. **COLE:** Choice three: decline Relay and call twenty million for Vela's proven organic scale plan. You lose the chance to own Relay and a competitor may buy it. You also preserve Keystone's turnaround, avoid new acquisition debt, and put capital behind a system with current evidence. The fund still consumes twenty million and reaches the reserve floor.

**ASHA:** Grade them. Value created: Relay may offer more strategic surface, Vela offers the stronger underwritten path, and the central platform offers theoretical portfolio option value. Cash consumed: all three require money if pursued seriously; the central platform could require the most before it proves a customer. Risk added: Keystone acquisition adds leverage, concentration, consent, and integration risk. Central ownership adds governance and boundary risk. Vela adds scale and geographic execution risk.

**COLE:** Speed to evidence: Vela can show activation, contribution, and retention inside the next quarter. Relay's first clean proof depends on close, consents, baseline capture, and integration. Reversibility: the Vela plan can be staged. A central platform can be stopped but may strand overhead. An acquisition cannot be unwound at the original price because a customer leaves.

**ASHA:** Make the call. Buy into Keystone, create the portfolio roll-up platform, or walk and fund Vela. Write down the one fact that would make your decision change. We will give you a moment.

### WHAT THE WALK TEACHES THE MACHINE

**COLE:** Northstar sends the seller a concise no. It does not invent a financing excuse. The price does not compensate for normalized earnings, customer concentration, consent uncertainty, and integration capacity.

**ASHA:** The seller responds that another bidder is willing to pay the fifty-four. That may be true. Another buyer may have customer diversification, existing consents, cheaper integration, or a strategic value Northstar does not. A different bid does not make Northstar's constraints disappear.

**COLE:** The acquisition team then performs a loss review before the auction result. Not to reverse the decision. To make the funnel smarter.

**ASHA:** Relay passes workflow adjacency and product capability. It fails entry value on normalized earnings, customer concentration without protection, rights transfer, and integration capacity. Those are separate failure codes. If every declined target is simply marked "price," the machine learns nothing.

**COLE:** The team also changes screening. Customer concentration and assignability move ahead of management meetings. Integration capacity becomes a live portfolio inventory, not a diligence appendix. Any synergy already present in a company plan is tagged so it cannot reappear in acquisition value.

**ASHA:** And they add a counterfactual to every future return bridge: what would the same equity, debt capacity, and executive attention earn in the best internal initiative? A target does not compete against cash earning zero. It competes against the portfolio opportunity queue.

**COLE:** That is the roll-up machine Northstar actually builds this quarter. No acquired company. Better gates, better pacing, cleaner denominators, and a capital decision the fund can explain.

### THE OPERATOR ARTIFACT

**ASHA:** Your artifact is a six-part roll-up operating pack.

**COLE:** Part one: the target funnel. For every candidate, record the primary strategic job, source, stage, verified financial baseline, gating risks, decision date, and why it advanced or failed. Do not reduce every rejection to price.

**ASHA:** Part two: the acquisition cohort scorecard. Group completed deals by thesis and track entry multiple on verified earnings, retained revenue, customer concentration, gross margin, integration cash, leadership retention, time to control, time to first customer value, realized synergy, leverage, and actual return against the original memo.

**COLE:** Part three: the quality-of-earnings and rights memo. Put seller-reported, diligence-adjusted, and underwritten results in separate columns. Map customer contracts, revenue recurrence, working capital, data purpose, assignability, consent, and change of control. Every adjustment has a source and recurrence rationale.

**ASHA:** Part four: the integration-factory capacity model. List the required work by leadership, customer, finance, identity, security, product, data, and people. Show named capacity, current load, surge reserve, next available start date, and the executive with authority. A template is not capacity.

**COLE:** Part five: sources and uses plus a downside. Enterprise purchase price, assumed debt, sponsor equity, fees, integration cash, company debt, interest, covenant headroom, and downside liquidity. Use normalized earnings before synergy at close. Show exactly when any synergy enters EBITDA and who validates it.

**ASHA:** Part six: the return and opportunity-cost bridge. Entry value, operating improvement, retained revenue, verified synergy, debt change, exit value, time, and required follow-on capital. Beside it, show the best internal use of the same capital and leadership.

**COLE:** Then run the drill. Take your most attractive live target. Remove every revenue synergy. Replace reported EBITDA with normalized EBITDA. Assume the largest customer leaves. Delay integration by two quarters. Name the person who would lead it and remove their current work. If you still want the deal, you may have an underwrite. If the return disappears, you had a presentation.
