**NORA:** And the leadership?

**JAMES:** Three chapters. Founder-owned from 1999 until 2018. Then in April 2018, NexPhase Capital, a private equity firm, acquired it. And in January 2025, NexPhase announced the sale to Levine Leichtman, investing from its lower middle market fund, alongside management. Terms weren't disclosed.

**NORA:** So Levine Leichtman has owned it for less than two years.

**JAMES:** About twenty-two months as we record. That's early in a private equity hold. Which means this is still the growth and investment phase, not the "get ready to sell" phase. That's worth keeping in mind when we talk about AI, because there's time to do things properly.

**NORA:** And under NexPhase, what happened?

**JAMES:** The CEO change in 2020, and a big expansion in territories. The footprint grew from three hundred and thirty-six territories at the end of 2019 to six hundred and twenty-six at the end of 2025, which spans the NexPhase years and the first year under the new owner.

**NORA:** Why do private equity firms like franchisors in particular?

**JAMES:** General background again. A franchisor is what people call asset-light. The franchise owners put up the capital for each location, hire the local staff and carry the local risk. The franchisor collects royalties and fees on the whole system's sales. So if the network grows, the franchisor's revenue grows without it having to fund every new office. That tends to mean high margins and steady cash flow, which private equity investors like.

**NORA:** And the catch?

**JAMES:** The franchisor doesn't control the front line directly. It can set standards, train and persuade, but the owners run their own businesses. That's exactly why the bandwidth comment matters so much for this company.

**NORA:** Any acquisitions under the current owner?

**JAMES:** None found. Growth has been organic, through selling new territories. One hundred and one in 2025, a record, and thirty-three more in the first half of 2026. The CEO said in April 2026 that about a hundred were planned for the year.

### The problem it solves

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer problem. And there are really two customers here, aren't there?

**JAMES:** There are layers, in fact. There's the family and the person receiving care. There's the franchise owner, who is SYNERGY's direct customer as a franchisor. And there's the caregiver, who isn't a customer but without whom nothing works.

**NORA:** Start with the family.

**JAMES:** The family's problem is that an older relative, or someone with a disability or recovering from surgery, needs help with daily life to stay safely at home. They don't need a nurse at the bedside, but they need someone. Bathing, dressing, meals, medication reminders, light housekeeping, transport, companionship. And memory care for people living with dementia. SYNERGY also runs specialty programs, for example around cancer care and Parkinson's disease, according to a 2023 profile.

**NORA:** And who pays?

**JAMES:** Mostly families, privately. That's the core of non-medical home care. But SYNERGY's CEO has also talked about programs for long-term care insurance, which is private insurance some people buy to cover this kind of care, and for veterans through the Department of Veterans Affairs, which can fund home care for eligible veterans.

**NORA:** And why is the demand growing?

**JAMES:** The obvious driver is an ageing population. The baby boomers are moving into their late seventies and eighties. That's general background rather than a number from our sources. And most people, given the choice, would rather age at home than move into a facility.

**NORA:** Can you draw the line between non-medical home care and home health care? People mix them up.

**JAMES:** Sure, as general background. Home health care is clinical. Nurses, physical therapists, wound care, often ordered by a doctor and paid by Medicare or health insurance. Non-medical home care is help with daily living: bathing, dressing, meals, companionship, reminders. It usually isn't covered by Medicare, which is why private pay dominates. SYNERGY sits in the non-medical part, although its caregivers often work alongside home health nurses in the same household.

**NORA:** And where do clients come from?

**JAMES:** Referrals, mostly. Hospital discharge planners, doctors' offices, senior living communities, social workers, elder law attorneys, and of course families searching online. Much of a franchise owner's marketing effort goes into building those local referral relationships. That's exactly what a customer relationship management system is meant to track.

**NORA:** Now the franchise owner.

**JAMES:** The franchise owner's problem is how to build a successful local care business without starting from zero. They get a brand, training, systems, marketing support, and a playbook. In return they pay an initial investment, ongoing royalties and fees. Many franchise owners in home care come from other careers, and some have a personal story, a parent they cared for themselves.

**NORA:** And the caregiver?

**JAMES:** The caregiver is the heart of it, and the scarcest resource. SYNERGY's CEO said in April 2026 that caregiver supply is the operating constraint. You can have families calling every day asking for help, but if you can't recruit, train and keep caregivers, you can't serve them.

**NORA:** So the binding constraint isn't demand. It's labour.

**JAMES:** That's the core of this whole episode. Home care is a labour business. Every hour of care is an hour of a caregiver's time. Revenue grows when you can staff more hours, and it stalls when you can't.

### How the franchise model works

**NORA:** Let's go into the economics. How much does it cost to buy in, and what does a location make?

**JAMES:** The best figures we have come from the 2024 Franchise Disclosure Document, as reported by Franchise Times in January 2025. A franchise disclosure document, by the way, is the legally required document a franchisor gives to prospective buyers, and it often includes financial performance figures.

**NORA:** And what does it say?

**JAMES:** For a single-unit owner, the initial investment ranged from about seventy-three thousand dollars to about one hundred and fifty-three thousand dollars. Average sales for single-unit owners open one to five years were about four hundred and eighty-eight thousand dollars. For single-unit owners open six to nine years, about one point one six million dollars. And for multi-unit owners, averages from about nine hundred and fifty-six thousand dollars up to about one point eight seven million.

**NORA:** So the established locations do more than twice what the newer ones do.

**JAMES:** Right, and that's an important pattern. It's a business that takes years to ramp. You need time to build referral relationships with hospitals, doctors and senior living communities. You need time to build a reliable bench of caregivers. And word of mouth matters enormously. A new owner should expect several years before reaching those mature numbers.

**NORA:** And that's sales, not profit.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Those are revenue averages. Caregiver wages are by far the largest cost, and margins depend on how well the owner manages scheduling, overtime and turnover. We don't have profit figures, so I won't guess.

**NORA:** And what does the franchisor actually provide?

**JAMES:** The brand, the training, the systems, national marketing, and support through that Performance Success Team. In April 2026, the CEO described a set of recent investments: a new customer relationship management system, which is the software for managing referrals, leads and client relationships; a new recruiting system for caregivers; and programs around long-term care insurance and veterans' care.

**NORA:** And the CEO's comment about bandwidth?

**JAMES:** Yes, this is the bit I find really interesting. In an April 2026 conversation with Home Health Care News, he said the company would "hold rollouts" when franchisees couldn't absorb them. In other words, there's a limit to how many new systems, programs and processes a small business owner can take on at once. If you push too many changes too fast, adoption fails.

**NORA:** That's refreshingly honest.

**JAMES:** It is, and in my view it's the single most important constraint on SYNERGY's AI strategy. The franchisor can sign the best AI partnership in the world. But it only creates value when two hundred and fifty or more independent owners actually use it, consistently, in their daily work.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** How big is the market?

**JAMES:** Two useful figures. IBISWorld, a market research firm, estimates the US home care providers industry at about one hundred and ninety-two billion dollars in revenue in 2026, growing about four percent this year and about six point seven percent a year over the five years from 2021. That's a broad category that includes medical home health, not just non-medical care.

**NORA:** And the franchised slice?

**JAMES:** Franchise Times, in its Top 400 ranking, put franchised home care system sales at about ten point four billion dollars across eighteen brands in 2025, up seven point three percent on 2024. So franchised home care is a growing but still relatively small part of the overall market. There are lots of independent agencies too.

**NORA:** And Sensi has its own market size number?

**JAMES:** It has a claim of a three hundred and ninety billion dollar global market, but that's company-sourced and we're not going to lean on it.

**NORA:** Is it a recession-proof business?

**JAMES:** SYNERGY picked up a recession-resistance ranking from Franchise Business Review, and the general logic is that people's need for care doesn't disappear in a downturn. But I'd be careful with "proof." Private-pay care is expensive for families. When budgets tighten, some families cut hours or lean more on unpaid family help. So I'd call it resilient rather than immune, in my view.

**NORA:** Who are the competitors?

**JAMES:** By system sales in the 2026 Franchise Times Top 400, Home Instead is the biggest we have numbers for, at about two point four six billion dollars in global sales. From general industry knowledge, Home Instead has been owned by Honor, a home care technology company, since 2021. Then Visiting Angels at about one point two nine billion. Right at Home at about one point one two billion, with more than seven hundred and fifty locations. BrightStar Care at about eight hundred and one million, which Franchise Times calls the largest brand in the category, presumably on a different measure. And Comfort Keepers at about seven hundred and fifty-three million, with more than six hundred locations.

**NORA:** So SYNERGY, at two hundred and twenty-three million in 2022, is well behind those on sales.

**JAMES:** Well behind, at least on the last disclosed figure, which is old. And that's why I'd be careful with the "second-largest" claim. On territories it may be near the top. On system sales, the bigger brands are several times larger. That gap also tells you something hopeful: many SYNERGY territories are young and still ramping.

**NORA:** Which competitor is the most interesting on AI?

**JAMES:** Comfort Keepers, by a distance. In July 2026, Home Health Care News described its AI toolkit: an enterprise AI platform built on OpenAI technology, a scheduling agent called Rosie, a shift-fill tool in beta, and Sensi in some of its franchises. The same article said franchisees found the scheduling tool hard to adopt at first.

**NORA:** So Sensi works with a rival too.

**JAMES:** Yes, and that matters. SYNERGY's Sensi partnership isn't exclusive. Any AI advantage that comes from Sensi's product can, in principle, be matched by competitors buying the same product.

**NORA:** Anyone else?

**JAMES:** Right at Home rolled out a new Zoho customer relationship system and a mobile app in 2025, and says it's exploring AI. It's also targeting states like Ohio, Pennsylvania, Minnesota, Michigan, Wisconsin, Massachusetts and Iowa in 2026. Always Best Care has around two hundred and ninety-eight territories in thirty-one states and two Canadian provinces, is overhauling its technology, and, interestingly, NexPhase, the firm that sold SYNERGY, became its majority investor. And there's a company called Avid that has done an eight-acquisition roll-up, which is a different growth playbook.

**NORA:** So the industry pattern is new systems, private equity owners and early AI.

**JAMES:** That's the pattern. Everyone is upgrading their technology stack, and AI is the next layer.

### The Sensi partnership

**NORA:** Let's dig into Sensi. What is it?

**JAMES:** Sensi is a venture-backed AI company that does audio-based monitoring for seniors at home. A device in the home listens for sounds and patterns that might indicate a problem, things like distress, a fall, or changes in behaviour, and alerts the care agency. It also has what it calls an AI operating platform for agencies.

**NORA:** And it's well funded.

**JAMES:** It raised a thirty-one million dollar Series B in June 2024, led by Insight Partners, and, according to a secondary source, a forty-five million dollar Series C in October 2025. So more than seventy-six million dollars across those two rounds.

**NORA:** And what did SYNERGY sign?

**JAMES:** A preferred partnership, announced on the sixteenth of July 2026, though one trade outlet dates it the twenty-first, to bring Sensi's monitoring and platform to its network of more than two hundred and fifty franchisees. Preferred partnership usually means the franchisor endorses and negotiates terms, and franchisees opt in. It doesn't necessarily mean every franchisee is using it.

**NORA:** What does Sensi claim it does?

**JAMES:** Sensi has claimed outcomes like twenty-two percent fewer hospitalisations, and that it works with more than eighty percent of the largest home care networks. Those are vendor claims, and we haven't seen independent verification. The partnership announcement also refers to HIPAA compliance, the US health privacy law, but that's Sensi's claim about its own product.

**NORA:** If I were the daughter in Chicago, what should I ask before agreeing to monitoring in my mother's home?

**JAMES:** As general guidance, not legal advice, I'd ask a few things. Is audio recorded and kept, or only analysed and discarded? Who can listen, and when? How long is anything stored? Can we switch it off, for example in the bathroom or during private conversations? What happens when it raises an alarm at three in the morning, and who responds? And how does my mother consent, and what if her memory makes consent difficult? A good agency should have clear answers to all of those.

**NORA:** So the honest status is "signed, not yet proven."

**JAMES:** That's exactly how I'd put it. We have the partnership. We don't have adoption figures, the number of homes using it, or any SYNERGY-specific outcome data. All of that would be the next thing to watch.

**NORA:** Let me ask the uncomfortable question. A device listening in someone's home. How do people feel about that?

**JAMES:** It's the right question. On one hand, for a family worried about a parent living alone, knowing that someone will be alerted if something goes wrong can be a huge relief. On the other hand, audio monitoring in a private home raises serious questions about consent, especially for someone with dementia who may not fully understand it, and about what's recorded, stored and who hears it. We didn't find SYNERGY's own privacy controls published, so we can't say how it handles this.

**NORA:** And caregivers? They're in that home too.

**JAMES:** Right. A caregiver working in a home with audio monitoring may feel watched. How the technology is explained to caregivers, what it's used for and what it isn't used for, matters a lot. If it's framed as safety for the client, that's one thing. If it starts to feel like performance surveillance, it could make an already hard job harder to fill.

### AI at SYNERGY today

**NORA:** Beyond Sensi, what's SYNERGY doing with AI?

**JAMES:** In that same April 2026 conversation, the CEO talked about using AI for business intelligence and described an AI-driven analytical report card for franchisees. We don't have much detail. My understanding is a report card that pulls together each franchise's performance measures and highlights where it's ahead or behind.

**NORA:** Why would a report card matter to a franchise owner?

**JAMES:** Because most small business owners don't have an analyst. A good report card tells Denise, or any owner, how she compares with similar territories on things like how quickly she responds to new enquiries, how many enquiries become clients, how many hours she fills and how long her caregivers stay. If AI can then point out the one or two things most worth fixing this month, that's coaching at scale. The franchisor's support team can't sit with two hundred and fifty owners every week, but a report card can.

**NORA:** And the new CRM and recruiting system?

**JAMES:** Those were described as upgrades, not as AI. But they're the plumbing. A modern customer relationship system and a modern recruiting system create the data that AI needs. Who called, from which referral source, how fast they were contacted, whether they became a client. Which caregiver applicants applied, how far they got, who was hired and how long they stayed.

**NORA:** So who leads AI inside the company?

**JAMES:** The executive sponsor named is Chad Ainsworth, the chief administrative and financial officer. We didn't find dedicated AI roles, an AI team or a published AI governance policy. That's typical for a franchisor of this size, in my view, but it means the AI expertise mostly lives with vendors.

**NORA:** So, overall read?

**JAMES:** SYNERGY is ahead of many companies in this series on AI intent and on getting a real tool into the field. It's behind on proof. The AI is bought, not built. Adoption figures aren't public. And the outcome claims belong to the vendor. That's not a criticism. Buying good AI from specialists is often the right call for a franchisor. But the value comes from the next step: measuring whether it works, franchise by franchise.

**NORA:** You keep saying franchise by franchise.

**JAMES:** Because a franchise network isn't one company. It's two hundred and fifty or more small companies, each with its own owner, staff, clients and habits. An AI tool can work brilliantly in one and gather dust in another. The franchisor's job is to find out why, and spread what works.

### A week with a franchise owner

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through a week for a franchise owner. Invented, not real.

**JAMES:** Let's call her Denise. She owns two territories in a suburban area. She used to work in hospital administration, and she bought her first SYNERGY territory four years ago after caring for her own father. She has an office manager, a care coordinator who does scheduling, and around sixty caregivers on her books, most part-time.

**NORA:** Monday morning.

**JAMES:** Monday morning starts with gaps. Two caregivers called in sick over the weekend. One client's daughter wants to increase care from four hours a day to eight because her mum had a fall. And a hospital discharge planner has called about a new client who's going home on Wednesday and needs help from day one.

**NORA:** So the coordinator is juggling.

**JAMES:** The coordinator is on the phone for most of the morning, calling caregivers to see who can cover which shifts. Who lives close enough, who has the right experience, who's already near overtime, who gets on well with that client. It's a puzzle that changes every hour.

**NORA:** And recruiting?

**JAMES:** Denise is always recruiting. This week twenty people applied online. Some never answer the phone. Some don't turn up to meet her. Some are great but need two weeks of onboarding. By Friday, maybe two or three are actually ready to start. And meanwhile, two caregivers have told her they're leaving.

**NORA:** So she's running to stand still.

**JAMES:** That's how a lot of home care owners describe it, in general. And this is where the CEO's comment really lands. On top of all that, the franchisor is asking her to adopt a new CRM, a new recruiting system, a new insurance program and now Sensi. Each one might be good. But each one takes time she doesn't have.

**NORA:** Where does Sensi fit into her week?

**JAMES:** Let's say she's using it with a few clients. On Thursday, at three in the morning, a device in an eighty-seven-year-old client's home picks up sounds that suggest a fall. The alert goes to the agency's on-call line. The on-call staff member calls the client, gets no clear answer, calls the daughter and calls emergency services. The client had fallen in the bathroom. She's taken to hospital, bruised but okay.

**NORA:** That's the best case.

**JAMES:** That's the best case, and it's a compelling one. Without monitoring, she might have lain on the floor until the caregiver arrived at nine. But Denise also had six alerts that week that turned out to be nothing: a television, a dropped pan, a loud phone call. Each one had to be checked. If false alarms are too frequent, the on-call staff start to tune them out, and that's dangerous.

**NORA:** So the value depends on the details.

**JAMES:** Entirely. How accurate the alerts are, how the agency responds, whether families value it enough to pay for it or prefer it bundled into care, and whether it actually reduces hospital visits. Those are measurable, but only if someone measures them.

**NORA:** Let's talk about the people side of all this. Who has to be won over for any AI tool to work in a network like this?

**JAMES:** I'd think about four groups. The franchise owners, who decide whether to adopt and pay. The office staff, especially care coordinators, who would use scheduling and recruiting tools every day. The caregivers, whose work is being scheduled and, with monitoring, whose clients' homes are being listened to. And the families and clients themselves.

**NORA:** What does each care about?

**JAMES:** Owners care about revenue, margin and their own time. If the tool doesn't clearly fill more hours or save staff time, they won't bother. Coordinators care about whether it makes their day calmer or adds another screen. Caregivers care about fair schedules, enough hours, not too much driving, and being treated with respect. And families care about safety, dignity and cost.

**NORA:** So the pitch to each is different.

**JAMES:** Very different. And the risk is that a franchisor pitches only to owners, and forgets the coordinator who actually has to use the thing at seven in the morning. In our view, from how software rollouts tend to go generally, the daily user decides whether the tool survives.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about the challenges.

**JAMES:** I'd name five. One, franchisee bandwidth. The CEO's own words. Every new tool competes for the attention of busy small business owners. That caps how fast any AI tool reaches the field.

**NORA:** Two?

**JAMES:** Two, proof. The AI outcome claims, like fewer hospitalisations, are Sensi's. SYNERGY hasn't published its own data. Until it does, the AI story is a vendor's story.

**NORA:** Three?

**JAMES:** Three, commoditisation. Sensi serves rivals. Comfort Keepers has its own OpenAI-based platform and scheduling agent. Features can be matched quickly. So any advantage has to come from adoption and execution, not from the tool itself.

**NORA:** Four?

**JAMES:** Four, the ramp. New owners average about four hundred and eighty-eight thousand dollars in sales in their first five years against about one point one six million for more mature single units. That's a long climb. With about a hundred new territories a year, a big chunk of the network is always in the early, harder phase. That's our inference from the disclosure figures.

**NORA:** And five?

**JAMES:** Five, disclosure and data discipline. Different franchisee and state counts in different releases, no recent revenue, no royalty rate. Some of that is normal for a private company. But for a network that wants to use AI-driven report cards, consistent, clean data across every franchise is the foundation.

**NORA:** And caregiver pay? That must squeeze everything.

**JAMES:** It does, as general background. Caregivers compete with retail, hospitality and warehouses for workers, and those employers can often offer more predictable hours. When wages rise, owners have to raise prices to families, which pushes against what families can afford. That's why the efficiency of scheduling matters so much. Wasted travel time and unfilled gaps are costs nobody gets paid for.

**NORA:** You haven't mentioned privacy as a challenge.

**JAMES:** I'd make it a sixth. Audio monitoring in private homes, with vulnerable people, raises consent and regulatory questions. Getting it wrong would hurt the brand far more than any efficiency gain would help it.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's do opportunities.

**JAMES:** The biggest, in my view, is AI-driven caregiver recruiting, screening and scheduling across the network. That's where the constraint is. If you can fill more client hours by recruiting faster, keeping caregivers longer and matching shifts more intelligently, revenue grows. The new recruiting system gives a data foothold.

**NORA:** What would that look like in practice?

**JAMES:** On recruiting: an AI assistant that responds to applicants within minutes, at any hour, answers their questions, screens for basics like availability and location, and books a first meeting. Speed matters, because good applicants get snapped up by other employers. On scheduling: a tool that proposes who should cover a gap based on distance, skills, client preferences, hours and overtime, with the coordinator approving.

**NORA:** Is there a way to put a number on it?

**JAMES:** Only illustratively. If we take the last disclosed system sales of two hundred and twenty-three million dollars in 2022, every one percent increase in hours filled would be about two point two million dollars a year of extra franchisee revenue. That's our arithmetic on an old number, and the franchisor's share would depend on the royalty rate, which we don't know. But it shows the scale.

**NORA:** Second opportunity?

**JAMES:** Turning Sensi outcome data into something payers value. If SYNERGY can show, independently, that monitored clients have fewer hospital visits, then long-term care insurers and the veterans' care programs might value that. That's our inference, and it requires independent measurement first.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Cross-selling specialty programs. Memory care, cancer care, Parkinson's, veterans and long-term care insurance, all through existing territories. More revenue per client without needing new territories.

**NORA:** And geography?

**JAMES:** SYNERGY is in forty-four or forty-five states. There are a few gaps. Right at Home is pushing into a set of Midwestern and Northeastern states in 2026. It's worth checking where those overlap with SYNERGY's gaps. Also our inference.

**NORA:** Acquisitions?

**JAMES:** None disclosed under the current owner. But buying independent agencies, or taking over struggling territories, is a recognised playbook in home care, as Avid's eight acquisitions show. It's an option.

**NORA:** And franchise sales?

**JAMES:** AI could help qualify the leads for those hundred or so territory sales a year. Faster responses, better matching of candidates to territories. A modest but practical use.

**NORA:** Let's go back to Denise and replay her week with a recruiting and scheduling assistant. Still invented.

**JAMES:** Monday morning, the same two sick calls. But overnight, the scheduling assistant has already looked at every available caregiver and proposed cover for both shifts, ranked by distance, experience with those clients and overtime. The coordinator approves one, tweaks the other, and sends the shift offers by text. Both are accepted by eight thirty.

**NORA:** And the client who wants eight hours instead of four?

**JAMES:** The assistant shows the coordinator which caregivers have spare hours this week and who has worked with that client before. The coordinator calls the family back before ten with a plan.

**NORA:** And recruiting?

**JAMES:** The twenty online applicants got a reply within five minutes, many of them in the evening when they applied. The assistant answered basic questions about pay range, location and hours, using answers Denise approved, asked a few screening questions, and booked first meetings straight into her calendar. Instead of two or three ready to start by Friday, maybe she has four or five. And because response was quick, fewer good applicants were lost to the supermarket down the road.

**NORA:** And the caregivers who were leaving?

**JAMES:** That's where it gets interesting. If the scheduling data shows that caregivers who drive long distances between clients leave sooner, Denise can try to build tighter routes. Turnover is the silent killer in home care, and the data to understand it is sitting in the scheduling system.

**NORA:** What does she not hand over to the AI?

**JAMES:** The hiring decision, the conversation with a worried family, and the judgement about which caregiver is right for a client with dementia who's frightened of strangers. Those stay human. The AI handles the speed and the puzzle-solving.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** Caregiver recruiting, screening and scheduling. It's the binding constraint, it's measurable, and it helps every franchise owner, not just those whose clients want monitoring. And I'd make sure Sensi has a proper measurement program running alongside.

**NORA:** How would you run the first hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: choose about fifteen to twenty franchise owners who volunteer, mixing new and mature territories, so you learn whether the tool helps the ramp. Agree the measures with them: time from application to first contact, application-to-hire rate, time to fill an open shift, caregiver turnover at ninety days, and client hours delivered. Get a baseline from the new recruiting system before anything changes.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Switch on AI screening and response for those owners. Keep humans making hiring decisions. Hold a weekly call with the pilot owners. And here's the bandwidth point: the franchisor's team does the setup, not the franchisee. If it takes the owner more than an hour to get started, you've already lost half of them.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Compare pilot owners to similar non-pilot owners. If hiring is faster and turnover lower, write it up in plain language with the pilot owners' own words, and present it at the franchise conference. Franchisees believe other franchisees far more than they believe head office. Then decide the rollout, in waves, with a support plan for each wave.

**NORA:** And Sensi in that same period?

**JAMES:** Pick the franchisees already using it and set up honest measurement. Number of monitored clients, alerts per week, how many were real, response times, hospital visits compared with similar clients without monitoring, and family satisfaction. Publish the results internally, good or bad. If the hospitalisation effect is real, it's a powerful story. If it isn't, better to know before scaling.

**NORA:** Who owns all this?

**JAMES:** One accountable person at the franchisor, with the authority to say "we're holding this rollout," which, as we know, the CEO is already willing to do. That willingness is actually an asset.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** What can other businesses learn from SYNERGY?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, the CEO's insight: adoption capacity is a real constraint, and it's worth naming out loud. Any business that rolls tools out to franchisees, dealers, branches or partners should ask, what's their bandwidth, and what are we taking off their plate before we add something?

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, put AI where the constraint is. In home care, the constraint is caregivers. A brilliant AI tool that addresses a non-constraint doesn't change the outcome much. Find the bottleneck, then apply AI there.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, buying AI is fine, but measuring it is your job. A vendor's case study isn't your result. Franchisors and multi-site businesses have a natural experiment built in: some locations adopt, others don't. Use that to measure honestly.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, trust is the product. In home care you're in people's homes, with vulnerable people. Any technology that touches privacy must be introduced with clear consent and clear limits. Get that right, and AI can strengthen trust. Get it wrong, and it can destroy it quickly.

**NORA:** Any lesson from the competition?

**JAMES:** Yes, a fifth one. Comfort Keepers built a big AI toolkit and found adoption hard. SYNERGY bought a focused tool and is pacing rollouts. Neither approach is automatically right. But the lesson is that the ambition of the AI matters less than the fit with how busy people actually work. A smaller tool that's used every day beats a bigger platform that isn't.

**NORA:** I'd add one about franchising generally. The franchisor's real job is to make independent owners better at their jobs.

**JAMES:** That's well put. AI at the franchisor level is only as valuable as its effect on the owner's Monday morning.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI disrupting the revenue model. If monitoring and smarter scheduling cut the hours of care clients need, or the number of coordinators, then franchisee revenue and royalties could be pressured. That's speculative, but it's a real tension in any hours-based business.

**NORA:** Two?

**JAMES:** Two, commoditisation, which we've covered. Three, the scale gap with Home Instead, Visiting Angels and others on system sales. Four, privacy and consent, especially with audio in the home. And five, adoption risk. Comfort Keepers found its scheduling tool hard for franchisees to adopt. SYNERGY could hit the same wall.

**NORA:** Let me push on that first risk. Isn't it odd for a business paid by the hour to adopt technology that might reduce hours?

**JAMES:** It's a real tension, and not unique to home care. Lawyers face it, consultants face it. But I'd argue that in home care today, demand outstrips the caregivers available. So if monitoring means a client can safely have six hours of care instead of eight, those two hours can go to another family who's on a waiting list. In a labour-constrained market, efficiency is growth, not shrinkage. That could change if caregiver supply ever catches up with demand, but there's no sign of that in our sources.

**NORA:** And there's a pricing question too.

**JAMES:** Yes. If monitoring adds value, it could become a paid service in its own right, a monthly fee for peace of mind. Then the hours lost could be offset by a new revenue line. That's our speculation, but it's the kind of model franchisors will be testing.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any SYNERGY-specific data on Sensi: how many franchisees and clients are using it, and what it's doing to hospital visits. Second, whether SYNERGY hits roughly a hundred new territories in 2026. Third, any AI in recruiting and scheduling. And fourth, any published privacy and consent policy for in-home monitoring.
