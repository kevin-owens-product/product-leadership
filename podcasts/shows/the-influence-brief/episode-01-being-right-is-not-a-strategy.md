### SEGMENT 1: THE INFLUENCE MAP (6 minutes)

**SLOANE:** Start with the tool almost everyone thinks they're too senior for: actually mapping the room. Victor, make the case, because when I first heard "stakeholder mapping" I filed it under HR-workshop nonsense.

**VICTOR:** <laugh> Everyone does, and then everyone gets burned the same way. Here's the case. Think about the last big proposal you watched die. I'd bet money it didn't die in the meeting. It died *before* the meeting — in a hallway conversation you weren't in, in a one-on-one where someone said "I have concerns," in a Slack thread where a skeptic framed your idea before you got to. Decisions of consequence are almost never made in the room where they're announced. So walking into the announcement room well-prepared is preparing for the wrong event.

**VICTOR:** The map is just a disciplined answer to four questions, written down, for every person who can affect the outcome. One: what do they *want* — not their title's official objective, but what they're actually optimizing for this quarter. Two: what do they *fear* — because fear predicts behavior far better than ambition does. Three: what's their actual *power* over this decision — formal authority, budget control, veto by expertise, or the invisible one, social permission: the person others glance at before agreeing. Four: where do they currently stand on your issue — champion, ally, neutral, skeptic, opponent.

**SLOANE:** The written-down part matters more than it sounds. The map in your head is a mood. The map on paper is a plan, and it confronts you with the cells you can't fill in. If you can't answer "what does the CFO fear this quarter," that's not a gap in the map — that's the next conversation you need to have, and notice it's a conversation where you *ask things*, not pitch things.

**VICTOR:** Two failure modes to flag, because sophisticated people fail this in sophisticated ways. First: mapping only the org chart. The org chart shows formal power. It doesn't show that the CEO trusts the founder-era staff engineer more than half the exec team, or that the CFO's chief of staff decides what reaches the CFO in what framing. Ask yourself: when this decision gets discussed without me, who's in that conversation? *That's* your map.

**SLOANE:** Second failure mode: treating the map as static. People move. In fact, the entire point of the map is to plan movement — the unit of work in influence is moving one person one column. You don't need to convert your opponent into a champion. You usually just need the opponent moved to neutral, two neutrals moved to allies, and your champion armed well enough to spend their credibility for you when you're not in the room.

**VICTOR:** Which raises the question the map can't answer: *how* do you move someone a column? You move them with something they value. Which brings us to currencies.

### SEGMENT 2: CURRENCIES OF EXCHANGE (6 minutes)

**VICTOR:** The core idea comes from Cohen and Bradford's *Influence Without Authority*, and it's the single most useful reframe I know: influence is trade. If you need something from someone who doesn't report to you, you are asking them to spend something — time, budget, political capital, focus. Nobody spends for free, no matter how good your slide deck is. The question is never "how do I convince them." It's "what do they value that I can pay in."

**SLOANE:** And the reframe works because the word "currencies" is plural. People fixate on the obvious ones — headcount, budget, quid pro quo roadmap trades — which executives mostly can't offer each other anyway. The currencies that actually move people day to day are softer and cheaper for you to mint. Recognition: their team's work named in your QBR, credit passed upward with their name attached. Information: early sight of your plans so they're never surprised in front of their boss — you'd be amazed what "never let them get blindsided" alone buys you. Access: an introduction, a seat in a forum they're not usually in. Advancement of *their* narrative: framing your initiative so it visibly serves the story they're telling about their own org. And relief: quietly taking a problem off their plate that they're embarrassed to still have.

**VICTOR:** Notice the asymmetry — that's the beautiful part. The things on that list cost you almost nothing and are worth a lot to the right person. Trade only works because the same item has different values to different people. Your CFO doesn't want your roadmap slots. Your CFO wants to walk into the board meeting with a cost story that holds up. If your platform work *is* a cost story, you have something to trade — you just haven't denominated it in their currency yet.

**SLOANE:** The discipline that makes this compound instead of feeling transactional: pay *before* you need anything. Cohen and Bradford talk about the law of reciprocity, and every functional executive relationship runs on it — but reciprocity has a memory. Deposits made only in the week before your big ask are recognized instantly for what they are. <laugh> Everyone has met that person. The influence-rich executives you know made their deposits eighteen months ago, in small denominations, consistently, with no invoice attached.

**VICTOR:** A caution so this stays on the right side of the line: currencies get you *engagement* — they get your ask taken seriously by someone with no reason to care. They don't get you *belief*. If the idea is bad, good trading just gets you a politer no. Currencies are the doorway, not the room. What determines what happens inside the room is whether they trust what you say. Which is the third tool.

### SEGMENT 3: THE TRUST EQUATION (5 minutes)

**SLOANE:** The trust equation comes from David Maister's *The Trusted Advisor*, and it's worth writing on a whiteboard once a quarter. Trust equals credibility, plus reliability, plus intimacy — all divided by self-orientation. Four variables. Three you add. One that divides everything.

**SLOANE:** Quickly through the numerator. Credibility: do your claims hold up — do you know what you're talking about, and crucially, do you *say so* when you don't. Reliability: do your commitments hold up — the boring compounding of doing what you said, on the timeline you said, fifty times in a row. Intimacy: can people be honest *with* you — will they tell you the real number, the real fear, the thing they'd never put in the deck. Most executives over-invest in credibility, coast on reliability, and treat intimacy as a personality accident rather than a skill.

**VICTOR:** But the denominator is where careers are decided, and it's why this belongs in episode one. Self-orientation: how much of what you do reads as being *for you*. And because it's the divisor, it doesn't matter how brilliant and reliable you are — if every argument you make happens to conclude "and therefore my org should get the budget," people discount all of it. Not because they think you're lying. Because they can't *separate* your judgment from your interest, so they down-weight the whole signal. That's the tragedy of the brilliant, frustrated, technically-right executive we opened with: enormous numerator, catastrophic denominator.

**SLOANE:** And the repair is almost embarrassingly concrete: argue against your own interest, occasionally, in public, when it's true. Kill one of your own projects before someone makes you. Concede the strongest version of the other side's point before you answer it. Recommend the option that shrinks your scope when it's genuinely right. You only need a handful of these a year — they're expensive, that's *why* they work. Each one is you visibly paying to keep your judgment and your interest separable, and it re-rates everything else you say.

**VICTOR:** One sentence on how the three tools stack, because they're a system, not a menu: the map tells you *who* and *in what order*. Currencies get each conversation to start warm instead of cold. Trust determines whether anything said in that conversation actually lands. Map, currency, trust. Now let's run the whole stack against a real scenario.

### SEGMENT 4: THE SCENARIO -- FUNDING THE PLATFORM REBUILD (5 minutes)

**VICTOR:** The setup, and tell me this isn't someone you know. You're the CPO of a two-hundred-person B2B software company. The checkout-and-billing platform is eight years old and creaking — incidents are climbing, every new feature costs triple what it should, and two of your best engineers have quit citing the codebase. You want three squads for two quarters to rebuild it. The CRO wants those same squads building the feature list attached to the four biggest deals in the pipeline. The CEO is deal-oriented and conflict-averse. The CFO is new, hunting for credibility, and has said the word "efficiency" in every meeting since arriving. Last exec off-site, you made the technical case with a beautiful deck — architecture diagrams, incident graphs — and it died in real time. The CRO said "so we're pausing revenue for six months to refactor code," and that was that.

**SLOANE:** Classic. And the instinct — we've all had it — is to come back with a *better deck*. More data, scarier incident graphs, an even more irrefutable case. Which fails identically, because the deck was never the problem. Watch what the three tools say instead. <breath> First, the map. Who actually decides? Formally, the CEO. Practically — conflict-averse CEO — the CEO ratifies whatever consensus walks into the room. So the real decision-makers are the CRO and CFO, and the real event is the pre-meeting alignment, not the meeting. Where does everyone stand? CRO: opponent, and here's the important part — not because he loves features, but look at his fear column: he's terrified of missing the year with four whale deals in play. Your rebuild isn't his enemy; *risk to his quarter* is his enemy. CFO: unmapped. Genuinely neutral, hunting for a first win. That empty cell is the whole game.

**VICTOR:** Because run the currency analysis on the CFO. What does a new, credibility-hunting, efficiency-obsessed CFO value? A defensible cost-reduction story to bring to the board — one with an owner who isn't her, and numbers that hold up. Now look at what a platform rebuild actually *is*, economically: incident costs, triple-cost feature delivery, attrition of expensive engineers. You've been denominating this project in architecture diagrams. Denominate it in *her* currency and the rebuild is a cost-efficiency program with an engineering implementation detail. You're not asking the CFO to support you. You're *offering* her the flagship of her efficiency narrative. That's a deposit, not a request.

**SLOANE:** And the CRO — you don't fight him, because the map says you can't win a fight and don't need one. You need him moved from opponent to neutral. His currency is deal risk, so you pay in that currency: you carve the plan so the two platform capabilities his whale deals actually depend on — say, usage-based billing and the enterprise SSO piece — ship *first*, visibly sequenced for his pipeline, with his team consulted on the order. Now opposing the rebuild means opposing the thing de-risking his own deals. He won't champion it. He doesn't have to. Silence from the CRO plus enthusiasm from the CFO *is* consensus, for a ratifying CEO.

**VICTOR:** And the trust equation is why any of this is even available to you. The CFO only picks up the cost story if your numbers have a history of holding up — that's credibility you either banked over the past year or didn't. The CRO only believes the sequencing promise if you've *delivered* what you promised before — reliability. And notice the self-orientation move hiding in the design: the plan you're now proposing has the CFO's name on the board slide and the CRO's deals in the first milestone. You've made your initiative structurally *about them*. The version that died at the off-site was about your architecture. <laugh> Same rebuild. Same three squads. The engineering plan didn't change — the denomination did.

**SLOANE:** Trace what we just did, because it's the template for every episode ahead: map told us the decision was two people and a pre-meeting, not a deck and a boardroom. Currencies told us what each of those people would actually accept payment in. Trust determined whether our payment was good. Being right was necessary the entire time — and sufficient at no point.
