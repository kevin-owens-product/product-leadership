### The federal stance

**THEO:** Let's start in Washington. What's the federal government's position on AI regulation in late 2026?

**HANA:** In a sentence: promote AI development, avoid new federal regulation, and push back against state regulation it sees as burdensome. In January 2025, the President revoked the previous administration's executive order on AI safety and issued a new order focused on removing barriers to American AI leadership. In July 2025, the White House released an AI Action Plan built around accelerating innovation, building AI infrastructure, and international leadership, with a theme of reducing regulatory obstacles.

**THEO:** And the states?

**HANA:** That's the live battle. In 2025, Congress considered a moratorium that would have blocked states from enforcing AI laws for ten years. It was removed from the final legislation after strong opposition, including from many states and members of both parties. Then in December 2025, the President signed an executive order aimed at a national AI policy framework, which directed the Justice Department to set up a task force to challenge state AI laws in court, asked the Commerce Department to identify state laws it considered onerous, and tied some federal funding to states' AI policies.

**THEO:** Has that changed what states do?

**HANA:** It's had some effect. Colorado's story, which we'll get to, is partly a response to that pressure. But most state laws remain in force, new ones continue to pass, and an executive order can't by itself override state law. Preemption normally needs Congress, or a court finding that a state law conflicts with the Constitution or federal law. So the practical position for a company is: comply with the state laws that apply to you, and watch the litigation and Congress.

**THEO:** And federal agencies' own use of AI?

**HANA:** The Office of Management and Budget issued memos in 2025 on how federal agencies use and buy AI, which matter if you sell AI to the government. They set expectations for agencies around high-impact AI uses, risk management and procurement. For government contractors, those requirements flow into contracts.

---

### Selling AI to the government

**THEO:** A quick detour for companies that sell to government. What's different?

**HANA:** Federal agencies buy under rules set by the Office of Management and Budget, which issued memos in April 2025 on agencies' use and acquisition of AI. They ask agencies to identify high-impact AI uses, apply minimum risk management practices to them, like testing, impact assessments and human oversight, and to build those expectations into contracts. They also emphasize buying American AI and avoiding vendor lock-in, including through data portability and rights to models and data developed under contract.

**THEO:** So the contract terms change.

**HANA:** They do. Expect government contracts for AI to include requirements about documentation, testing, data rights, notification of changes and incidents, and sometimes restrictions on using government data to train commercial models. State and local governments have their own policies too, and many states have passed laws on government use of AI, with inventories, assessments and procurement rules. For a company selling into the public sector, those requirements arrive through procurement rather than regulation, but they're just as binding.

---

### What federal agencies enforce

**THEO:** Even without a federal AI law, you said federal agencies still enforce existing laws. Let's go through them.

**HANA:** Start with the Federal Trade Commission, the broadest. It enforces the law against unfair and deceptive practices across most of the economy. On AI, it has acted on three main themes. Deceptive claims about AI, like the cases in its Operation AI Comply sweep in September 2024, including against a company that marketed itself as a robot lawyer. Unfair use of AI that harms consumers, like the case against the pharmacy chain Rite Aid, which in December 2023 agreed to a five-year ban on using facial recognition for surveillance after the FTC alleged its system falsely flagged customers, disproportionately women and people of color, as shoplifters.

**THEO:** And the third?

**HANA:** Data. The FTC has required companies to delete algorithms and models built with data obtained improperly, a remedy sometimes called algorithmic disgorgement. That's a powerful threat for any company whose AI depends on data it may not have had the right to use. The emphasis of the FTC's AI work has shifted under the current leadership, with more focus on fraud and deception and less on broader theories, but the core law hasn't changed.

**THEO:** Securities.

**HANA:** The Securities and Exchange Commission has brought AI-washing cases against investment advisers and, in January 2025, against Presto Automation, a restaurant technology company, over claims about its AI voice ordering. For any company raising money or reporting to investors, AI claims need the same accuracy as any other material statement. Public companies also increasingly disclose AI as a risk factor, and those disclosures need to be specific and honest.

**THEO:** Employment.

**HANA:** The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforces federal anti-discrimination law, which applies to AI hiring tools. In 2025, the agency withdrew some of its earlier technical guidance on AI, and its enforcement priorities shifted. But the laws themselves, like Title VII and the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, still apply, and private lawsuits like the Workday case continue. Employers remain responsible for discriminatory outcomes from tools they use.

**THEO:** Finance.

**HANA:** Federal banking regulators expect banks to manage model risk under long-standing guidance, which covers AI models. Fair lending laws, like the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, require lenders to give specific reasons for adverse credit decisions. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau said in 2023 that creditors can't use the complexity of an AI model as an excuse for vague reasons. The Bureau's role has been significantly reduced under the current administration, but the statute, and private and state enforcement, remain.

**THEO:** Health.

**HANA:** The Food and Drug Administration regulates AI that qualifies as a medical device, and has authorized over a thousand AI-enabled devices. It finalized guidance in December 2024 on predetermined change control plans, which let device makers update AI models within pre-agreed limits without a new submission each time, and issued draft guidance in early 2025 on AI-enabled device software. The Department of Health and Human Services enforces HIPAA on patient data, and its nondiscrimination rule requires covered organizations to manage discrimination risks from patient care decision support tools, including AI.

**THEO:** And communications.

**HANA:** The Federal Communications Commission ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices are artificial voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. So AI voice calls to consumers generally need prior express consent, like any robocall. Given the statutory damages per call, that's one of the most financially dangerous AI rules for consumer businesses.

### The states: why they matter so much

**THEO:** Now the states. Why have they become the center of American AI regulation?

**HANA:** Three reasons. Congress hasn't passed a broad AI law, so states are filling the gap, as they did with privacy. States have traditionally regulated employment, insurance, consumer protection and professional licensing, which are exactly the areas where AI decisions affect people. And state attorneys general are active enforcers, with broad consumer protection powers they can apply to AI without any new law.

**THEO:** How many laws are we talking about?

**HANA:** Hundreds of AI bills are introduced in state legislatures every year, and dozens pass, though most are narrow: government use of AI, deepfakes in elections, task forces and studies. The ones that matter for businesses are fewer, and they fall into the categories we sketched in episode one. Let's go through them properly.

---

### Category one: consequential decisions

**THEO:** First, the broad laws on consequential decisions. Tell us the Colorado story properly.

**HANA:** Colorado passed the first comprehensive state AI law in May 2024. It was modeled partly on the EU approach. Developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems, meaning systems that are a substantial factor in consequential decisions about things like jobs, housing, credit, insurance, education and healthcare, had a duty to use reasonable care to protect consumers from algorithmic discrimination. Deployers needed risk management programs and annual impact assessments. And following a recognized framework like NIST's gave a defense.

**THEO:** But it never took effect.

**HANA:** It never took effect. It was originally due in February 2026. The governor signed it with reservations and asked for changes. In a special session in August 2025, the legislature delayed it to the end of June 2026. Then, under pressure from businesses and with the federal government calling out state AI laws, the legislature repealed and replaced it this year. The governor signed the replacement in May 2026, and it takes effect in January 2027.

**THEO:** What does the new law require?

**HANA:** It's much narrower. It covers automated decision-making technology that materially influences a consequential decision in seven areas: education, employment, residential real estate, financial and lending services, insurance, healthcare and essential government services. Developers must give deployers documentation on intended uses, known limitations, the categories of training data, and instructions for appropriate use and human review, and keep records for three years.

**THEO:** And deployers?

**HANA:** Deployers must tell consumers, before use, that such technology will be used and how to get more information. Within thirty days of an adverse decision, they must explain the decision, the technology's role, how to request information about it and its inputs, and the consumer's rights. Consumers can access and correct their personal data, and, where commercially reasonable, get a meaningful human review and reconsideration. Deployers keep compliance records for three years.

**THEO:** And what was dropped?

**HANA:** The duty of care against algorithmic discrimination, the mandatory risk management programs, the impact assessments and annual reviews, and reporting to the attorney general. Discrimination claims are left to Colorado's existing anti-discrimination law, with liability split by relative fault between developers and deployers. Only the attorney general enforces it, with a sixty-day cure period for violations that can be fixed, at least until 2030. There's no private right of action.

**THEO:** So the shift is from preventing discrimination through process to transparency and recourse.

**HANA:** That's a fair summary. And it's interesting as a signal. The most ambitious EU-style state law in the country was replaced with a disclosure-and-recourse model. Other states considering broad laws may follow that template. But the practical obligations, notice, explanation, human review on request, documentation, are still real, and they look a lot like what California's privacy regulator now requires.

---

### Category two: employment

**THEO:** Second category, employment. What are the key laws?

**HANA:** New York City's Local Law 144, enforced since July 2023, is the best known. Employers and employment agencies using automated employment decision tools to screen candidates or employees for hiring or promotion in the city must have an independent bias audit of the tool done each year, publish a summary of the results, and notify candidates at least ten business days before using the tool, including what it assesses.

**THEO:** How has it worked?

**HANA:** It's been criticized from both sides. Advocates say it's too narrow, because employers can argue their tools don't substantially assist decisions and fall outside it. A New York State comptroller audit in late 2025 found weak enforcement by the city agency. Employers say it's burdensome. But it established the bias audit as a concept, and many vendors now provide audit results as a matter of course.

**THEO:** Illinois.

**HANA:** Illinois has two laws. Its Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act, from 2020, requires notice, explanation and consent when employers use AI to analyze video interviews, plus some demographic reporting. And an amendment to the Illinois Human Rights Act, effective January 2026, makes it a civil rights violation for employers to use AI in a way that discriminates on protected characteristics in recruitment, hiring, promotion, discipline and other decisions, or to use zip codes as a proxy for protected classes. It also requires notice to employees when AI is used for those purposes.

**THEO:** And elsewhere?

**HANA:** Maryland requires consent for facial recognition in job interviews. California's civil rights regulators adopted regulations, effective October 2025, clarifying that the state's employment discrimination law applies to automated decision systems, with record-keeping requirements and a note that anti-bias testing is relevant evidence. And Colorado's new law covers employment decisions from 2027. Plus federal law everywhere.

**THEO:** What's the practical approach for employers?

**HANA:** Treat any AI tool that screens, ranks, scores or evaluates candidates or employees as high risk, wherever you are. Ask the vendor for bias audit results and documentation. Do your own check of outcomes by demographic group where you can. Notify candidates and employees clearly. Keep a human making the actual decisions. And keep records. That approach satisfies most of the specific laws and reduces your exposure under the general ones.

---

### Category three: privacy and automated decisions

**THEO:** Third, privacy laws. How do they affect AI?

**HANA:** About twenty states now have comprehensive consumer privacy laws, following California's lead. Most give consumers rights to access, correct and delete their personal data, and to opt out of profiling in furtherance of decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects. Many require data protection assessments for high-risk processing, which often includes profiling. Those rights and assessments apply to AI that uses personal data.

**THEO:** And California specifically?

**HANA:** California's privacy regulator, the California Privacy Protection Agency, finalized regulations in 2025 covering automated decision-making technology, risk assessments and cybersecurity audits. For businesses that use automated decision-making technology to make significant decisions about consumers, like decisions about financial services, housing, education, employment or healthcare, the rules require a pre-use notice, a right to opt out in many cases, and a right to access information about how the technology was used. Those requirements phase in, with the decision-making rules applying from January 2027.

**THEO:** So January 2027 is a busy month.

**HANA:** It's a big date. Colorado's replacement law and California's automated decision rules both start then, and they overlap considerably. A company making consequential decisions about consumers in both states should design one notice, explanation and review process that meets both.

---

### Category four: disclosure, chatbots and content

**THEO:** Fourth, disclosure and chatbots.

**HANA:** California's bot disclosure law has, since 2019, made it unlawful to use a bot to communicate with someone in California with the intent to mislead them about its artificial identity in order to sell goods or influence a vote. Utah's AI Policy Act, from 2024 and since amended, requires disclosure of generative AI in certain consumer interactions, especially if the consumer asks, and for regulated occupations. California requires healthcare providers to disclose generative AI use in patient communications about clinical information unless a licensed provider reviews them.

**THEO:** And companion chatbots?

**HANA:** California passed a law in October 2025 on companion chatbots, meaning AI designed to provide social or emotional companionship. It requires disclosure that the user is talking to AI, protocols for responding to users expressing suicidal thoughts, and extra safeguards for minors. Other states, including New York, have similar rules. Most business chatbots aren't companion chatbots, but customer service bots should still disclose they're AI, and anything consumer-facing that might be used by minors needs care.

**THEO:** And content?

**HANA:** Many states have laws on deepfakes in elections, and on non-consensual intimate deepfakes. Tennessee's ELVIS Act protects voices against unauthorized AI imitation, and several states have expanded rights of publicity to digital replicas. California passed laws in 2024 requiring large generative AI providers to offer detection tools and include provenance information, with later amendments adjusting the timeline. For a typical business, the rule is: label realistic synthetic media, get consent for any real person's likeness or voice, and don't use AI-generated content in ways that could mislead.

---

### Category five: frontier models and the rest

**THEO:** And the frontier model laws?

**HANA:** California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, signed in September 2025 after the governor vetoed a broader bill the year before, requires large developers of the most powerful models to publish frameworks for managing catastrophic risks, publish transparency reports, report critical safety incidents to the state, and protect whistleblowers. New York passed its own frontier model law, the RAISE Act, signed in December 2025. Those mostly affect the big AI labs.

**THEO:** Why should operators care?

**HANA:** Indirectly. They increase transparency from the companies whose models you build on, which helps your own risk management. And they're a sign of where state legislators' attention goes. And then there's a long tail: laws on AI in insurance, based on the insurance commissioners' model bulletin, laws on AI in utilization review for health insurance, laws on AI in political ads, laws on government use of AI, and Texas's 2025 law, which took effect in January 2026 and focuses mostly on government use and a list of prohibited practices, like using AI to manipulate people into self-harm or to produce certain illegal content.

---

### Health insurance and utilization review

**THEO:** You mentioned utilization review laws. Explain those.

**HANA:** Utilization review is how health insurers decide whether a requested treatment is medically necessary and will be covered, including through prior authorization. After lawsuits and press reports alleging that some insurers used algorithms to deny claims inappropriately, states moved. California's law, effective January 2025, requires that decisions to deny, delay or modify care based on medical necessity be made by a licensed physician or qualified health professional, not by an algorithm alone, and that AI tools used in the process be applied fairly and be open to inspection by regulators.

**THEO:** And other states?

**HANA:** Several states have passed or proposed similar laws, requiring human clinical review of AI-assisted denials, disclosure of AI use in utilization review, or both. At the federal level, Medicare Advantage rules issued in 2023 already required plans to base coverage decisions on the individual patient's circumstances, not solely on an algorithm. And the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services began a model in 2026 testing AI-assisted prior authorization in traditional Medicare in six states, with clinicians making final denial decisions.

**THEO:** So the principle is consistent.

**HANA:** Very consistent: AI can help, a qualified human decides any denial. For providers, the flip side is that their documentation is increasingly read by AI on the payer side, so it needs to be complete and accurate. Our Real Economy show's healthcare episode covers that from the provider's angle.

---

### Children and teens

**THEO:** Children's privacy and safety have been a big theme. What applies?

**HANA:** At the federal level, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children under thirteen online. The FTC updated its COPPA rule in 2025, including tighter limits on using children's data and a requirement for separate parental consent before disclosing it to third parties for things like targeted advertising, with most changes requiring compliance by 2026. Using children's data to train AI raises real questions under those rules.

**THEO:** And the states?

**HANA:** Several states have age-appropriate design codes or laws on minors' use of social media, some of which have been challenged in court. And as we said, companion chatbot laws in California and New York add protections for minors. State attorneys general have been especially active on AI and children, including investigations of AI chatbots that interacted inappropriately with minors.

**THEO:** And for a business that isn't aimed at children?

**HANA:** Consider whether children might use your AI anyway. A general consumer chatbot, an education product, a game, or a family-oriented service probably will be used by minors. Age-appropriate safeguards, content filters and escalation for sensitive topics are increasingly expected, and in some states required.

---

### State attorneys general

**THEO:** You mentioned attorneys general. How active are they?

**HANA:** Increasingly. State attorneys general can use their consumer protection and civil rights laws against AI practices without waiting for AI-specific laws. Texas's attorney general reached a settlement in 2024 with a healthcare AI company over claims about the accuracy of its products. Massachusetts's attorney general issued an advisory in 2024 explaining how existing consumer protection, civil rights and data security laws apply to AI. Several attorneys general have investigated AI chatbots over harms to children. And groups of attorneys general have written to AI companies about safety.

**THEO:** And in the fight with the federal government?

**HANA:** Attorneys general are on the front line, defending their states' laws against federal challenges. And they've been clear that their existing powers don't depend on new AI laws. So even if federal preemption succeeded for some AI-specific state laws, state consumer protection enforcement against deceptive or unfair AI practices would very likely continue.

**THEO:** So for companies, attorney general risk is real regardless of the preemption fight.

**HANA:** Very real. And it's often triggered by consumer complaints or press stories, which is another reason to get customer-facing AI right.

---

### Professional rules

**THEO:** There's another layer we haven't mentioned: professional rules for lawyers, doctors, accountants and others. Do they cover AI?

**HANA:** Increasingly. The American Bar Association issued a formal ethics opinion in July 2024 on lawyers' use of generative AI, saying the existing duties of competence, confidentiality, communication with clients, supervision and reasonable fees all apply. Lawyers must understand the tools well enough to use them competently, protect client information, check outputs, and in some cases tell clients. Many state bars have issued their own guidance. And courts have sanctioned lawyers for filing briefs with AI-invented case citations, repeatedly.

**THEO:** And other professions?

**HANA:** Medical boards, accounting bodies and engineering boards have issued guidance along similar lines: the professional remains responsible for the work, whatever tools they use. For businesses that employ licensed professionals, like law firms, medical groups, accounting firms and engineering firms, this means AI policies need to align with professional rules, and the professionals need training. A firm whose policy allows something the professional rules forbid puts its people in a difficult position.

**THEO:** And the billing question?

**HANA:** The ABA opinion addressed it: lawyers billing by the hour can't bill for time they didn't spend because AI did the work, and charges for AI tools need to be reasonable and explained. That's the business services pricing question again, now with an ethics rule attached.

---

### The preemption fight, practically

**THEO:** Let's spend a minute on what the preemption fight means for compliance planning.

**HANA:** Three scenarios. In the first, the status quo continues for years: federal executive pressure, some litigation, but state laws mostly stand, and new ones pass. That's the most likely near-term path. Companies comply state by state. In the second, Congress passes a federal AI law that preempts some or all state AI-specific laws. That would simplify things, but the federal law could have its own requirements, and preemption usually doesn't reach general consumer protection or civil rights law.

**THEO:** And the third?

**HANA:** Courts strike down some state laws, perhaps on grounds like the First Amendment for laws regulating AI outputs, or interstate commerce. That's possible for particular laws, especially those regulating speech or content, but unlikely to sweep away employment, insurance and consumer protection rules.

**THEO:** So the planning advice?

**HANA:** Build for the common denominator. The specific obligations that keep reappearing across laws, notice, explanation, human review, bias testing for high-stakes decisions, documentation, disclosure of AI interactions, are good practice that customers and buyers will expect anyway. A program built on those will survive any of the three scenarios. What you don't want is a program designed around one state's specific wording, which could change next year.

---

### One program for fifty states

**THEO:** So let's design that program. A company operating in many states. What does one compliance program look like?

**HANA:** Five building blocks. First, the inventory, tagged by decision type and by state. For each AI use, what it does, whether it makes or materially influences consequential decisions about people, in which domains, and in which states those people are. That tells you which laws to care about for which uses.

**THEO:** Second.

**HANA:** A tiering based on the strictest common requirements. Uses that make or substantially influence consequential decisions about individuals, in employment, credit, housing, insurance, education, healthcare or essential services, are high risk everywhere. Customer-facing AI interactions are medium risk. Internal productivity uses are low risk. You apply the same controls to a tier regardless of state, and add state-specific steps only where needed.

**THEO:** Third.

**HANA:** A standard set of controls for the high-risk tier. Vendor documentation and bias testing results. Your own outcome monitoring. A pre-use notice template that meets the strictest state's requirements. An adverse decision explanation process, within thirty days, with a path to human review. Data access and correction processes, which your privacy program probably already has. And records kept for at least three years.

**THEO:** Fourth.

**HANA:** Disclosure by default. Every chatbot and voice agent says it's AI. Realistic synthetic media is labeled. Outbound AI calls only with documented consent. Those are cheap and remove a lot of risk.

**THEO:** And fifth?

**HANA:** A monitoring routine. Someone, internal or external, tracks laws in your key categories and states, and reviews the inventory against them each quarter. Your privacy team is often the natural owner, because state privacy laws and AI laws overlap so much.

**THEO:** That's not a huge program.

**HANA:** It isn't, for most mid-sized companies. The heavy lifting is concentrated on the few high-risk uses, and for many companies, that's mainly hiring. Get hiring right, get customer-facing disclosure right, keep the inventory current, and you've covered most of the exposure.

---

### Building the evidence file

**THEO:** You keep stressing documentation. What should the evidence file contain, in practice?

**HANA:** Think of it as the folder you'd hand to an attorney general, a regulator, a plaintiff's lawyer in discovery, or a buyer's diligence team. For each high-risk AI use, it should contain: what the system does and why you use it. The vendor's documentation, including intended uses, limitations and testing. Your own assessment of the risks and the controls you chose. Bias or outcome testing results, with dates. The notices you give people, and when.

**THEO:** And the ongoing records?

**HANA:** The monitoring results over time. Records of adverse decisions, explanations given and human reviews requested and completed. Training records for the people who oversee the system. Incidents, complaints and what you did about them. And approvals, showing who signed off on using the system and on major changes.

**THEO:** That sounds like a lot of paper.

**HANA:** For one or two high-risk uses, it's manageable, and much of it is generated by doing the work properly anyway. The key is to keep it as you go. Recreating it after a complaint arrives is expensive and much less convincing. And for low-risk uses, the evidence file is just the inventory entry and the approval.

**THEO:** And who keeps it?

**HANA:** The owner of each AI use keeps its file, and the central governance function, often the privacy or compliance team, keeps the index and checks quarterly that files are current. That's also what makes audits, internal or external, straightforward.

---

### Vendors and contracts in the US

**THEO:** How should US contracts with AI vendors reflect all this?

**HANA:** For high-risk uses, ask for the documentation the laws now expect developers to provide: intended uses, known limitations, training data categories, instructions for appropriate use and human review, and bias testing results. Colorado's new law requires that from developers anyway. Ask for cooperation with your notice, explanation and review obligations, including access to information about how a decision was made. And ask for notice of material changes to the system.

**THEO:** And liability?

**HANA:** That's evolving. Colorado's new law voids contract clauses that indemnify a party for its own violations, and allocates discrimination liability by relative fault. The Workday litigation raises the possibility of vendors being liable directly. Vendors will push to limit their liability, and deployers will push the other way. At minimum, make sure the vendor stands behind its own documentation and testing, and has insurance that covers its AI products.

**THEO:** And data?

**HANA:** The standard AI data terms: whether your data trains the vendor's models, where it's processed and stored, retention, security, and what happens at termination. For consumer and employee data, those terms need to match your privacy notices and state privacy obligations.

### Sector example: an insurer

**THEO:** Let's do an example. A made-up regional property and casualty insurer, operating in ten states, using AI for claims triage, fraud detection, and a customer service chatbot.

**HANA:** Start with insurance regulation. Most of those ten states have adopted the insurance commissioners' model bulletin on AI, so the insurer needs a written AI program: governance, risk management, internal controls, oversight of third-party AI vendors, and documentation, with a focus on avoiding unfair discrimination and complying with insurance laws. Regulators can ask for it during examinations.

**THEO:** The claims triage?

**HANA:** If it materially influences decisions about paying or denying claims, it's a consequential decision under Colorado's new law from 2027, if the insurer operates there, and potentially under California's automated decision rules. So notices, explanations after adverse decisions, and human review on request. And the claims adjusters must remain the decision-makers, with the AI as an input, documented as such.

**THEO:** Fraud detection?

**HANA:** Generally less regulated as a category, but if it leads to denying claims or canceling policies, the same decision rules can apply, and unfair discrimination laws certainly do. Test it for disparate impact, and make sure a person reviews before adverse action.

**THEO:** The chatbot?

**HANA:** Disclose it's AI. Tie its answers to approved policy information. Never let it make coverage decisions. Monitor its conversations. And remember the Air Canada lesson: what the bot says, the company said.

---

### Sector example: a home services company

**THEO:** And a less regulated example. A home services company in fifteen states, using an AI voice agent for inbound calls, AI for outbound appointment reminders, AI to set financing offers, and an AI resume screener.

**HANA:** The inbound voice agent: disclose it's AI, give recording notices that satisfy the all-party consent states, and keep payment card numbers out of recordings. The outbound reminders: if they use an AI voice, the TCPA applies, so make sure consent records support each call, separate informational from marketing calls, and honor opt-outs. That's the biggest legal exposure in this company's AI.

**THEO:** The financing offers?

**HANA:** Credit decisions bring in fair lending law, including adverse action reasons, and state automated decision rules. If a lending partner makes the credit decision, much of the obligation sits with them, but the company should understand what's happening at its point of sale. Keep a human in the loop for anything that looks like an individual pricing or credit decision.

**THEO:** And the resume screener?

**HANA:** The highest regulatory risk per use. If any applicants are in New York City, the bias audit and notice rules apply. In Illinois, notice and the anti-discrimination rule. In California, the civil rights regulations. In Colorado from 2027, notice, explanation and review. And federal discrimination law everywhere. The company should get the vendor's bias audit, notify applicants, have recruiters make the decisions, and monitor outcomes. If it can't do that properly, it should consider whether the tool is worth the risk.

---

### Sector example: a staffing firm

**THEO:** One more example. A made-up staffing firm placing light industrial and administrative workers across twelve states, using AI to match candidates to job orders, to run chat-based screening interviews, and to schedule shifts.

**HANA:** The matching and screening are the high-risk uses. As an employment agency, the firm is directly covered by New York City's rules if it places people there, and by anti-discrimination law everywhere. The chat-based screening is an automated employment decision tool if it scores or filters candidates. So: bias audits, candidate notices, human recruiters making decisions, and outcome monitoring by group. And Illinois's notice and anti-discrimination rules for Illinois candidates, California's civil rights regulations for California candidates, and Colorado's new law from 2027.

**THEO:** And the clients?

**HANA:** The firm's clients, the employers, may also be liable for discrimination in the placements, so they'll increasingly ask the firm about its AI. A staffing firm that can show a clean, documented process has a sales advantage. One that can't may find clients moving work elsewhere.

**THEO:** And shift scheduling?

**HANA:** Scheduling is usually lower risk, unless it allocates work based on individual traits or performance in ways that disadvantage protected groups, or conflicts with predictive scheduling laws in some cities that require advance notice of shifts. It's worth a check, but it's not in the same tier as screening.

**THEO:** What's the biggest risk for this firm?

**HANA:** Scale. A staffing firm screens huge numbers of candidates. A small bias in a screening tool, multiplied by hundreds of thousands of candidates, becomes a large class of potential plaintiffs. That's why the testing and monitoring matter more here than almost anywhere.

### Questions we keep hearing

**THEO:** Quick questions. If the federal government wins against the states, can we drop all this?

**HANA:** Unlikely. Federal anti-discrimination, consumer protection, fair lending and communications laws would still apply, and state general laws would too. The specific obligations might change shape, but the core practices stay.

**THEO:** Do small businesses get exemptions?

**HANA:** Some laws have thresholds. Many state privacy laws only apply above a certain number of consumers or revenue. Colorado's new law has some small deployer provisions. But employment discrimination and consumer protection laws apply broadly, and the TCPA applies to anyone making calls. Small doesn't mean exempt.

**THEO:** Is following NIST's framework enough?

**HANA:** It's a strong foundation, and it shows reasonable care. But it doesn't automatically satisfy specific requirements like New York City's bias audit, notice timelines, or explanation duties. Use NIST or ISO as the structure, and map the specific legal requirements onto it.

**THEO:** And the most common US mistake?

**HANA:** Outbound AI calling without proper consent. It's the one with automatic statutory damages and active plaintiffs' lawyers. If you do one thing after this episode, check that.

### What to watch next year

**THEO:** Looking ahead, what should listeners watch over the next twelve months in the US?

**HANA:** Five things. First, January 2027, when Colorado's replacement law and California's automated decision-making rules both start. Expect guidance from both states, and the first questions from customers and employees. Second, the Justice Department's task force and any court rulings on state AI laws. A ruling on whether a state can regulate AI outputs, for example, would ripple widely.

**THEO:** Third.

**HANA:** Congress. There's bipartisan interest in some federal AI legislation, including a national framework that would preempt parts of state law, and narrower bills on deepfakes, children's safety and AI in specific sectors. Whether anything passes is uncertain, but a federal framework would change the map. Fourth, the Workday case and other litigation over AI hiring tools, which will shape vendor and employer liability more than any statute.

**THEO:** And fifth?

**HANA:** The next legislative sessions. States like California, New York, Illinois, Texas and Virginia are where most AI bills that affect businesses originate. Watch employment, insurance, healthcare and chatbot bills especially. And remember that a bill that fails one year often returns the next, narrower and more likely to pass.

### Takeaways

**THEO:** Sum it up.

**HANA:** The federal government is promoting AI and pushing against state laws, but federal agencies still enforce existing laws on deception, discrimination, lending, health, securities and calls. The FCC's ruling that AI voices are artificial under the TCPA is one of the most expensive rules to get wrong.

**THEO:** The states?

**HANA:** Colorado replaced its EU-style law this year with a narrower notice, explanation and review law starting January 2027, when California's automated decision rules also apply. Employment is the most regulated area, with New York City, Illinois and California rules plus federal law. Privacy laws give opt-out and assessment duties. Disclosure laws cover bots, companion chatbots, healthcare messages and synthetic media. And attorneys general enforce consumer protection against AI regardless.
