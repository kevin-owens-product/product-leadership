**COLE:** Then he removes six point eight million of unsupported or misclassified value. Two point one million is reassigned labor. People report time saved, but payroll, headcount, service capacity, and output have not changed. Time is an input observation, not automatically a benefit.

**ASHA:** One point four million is a model-vendor rebate negotiated through the common procurement contract. The rebate is real. It should reduce AI workload cost. It is not caused by the local workflows, and all three companies cannot claim the same portfolio rebate again.

**COLE:** One point two million is forecast cost avoidance. Managers say they will not hire roles next year. There is no approved demand plan, volume threshold, hiring authorization, or evidence that the hires would otherwise occur. A forecast against an unapproved forecast is not value.

**ASHA:** One point one million is duplicated benefit. Vela’s operations team claims faster resolution. Its customer-success team claims the same hours as lower support cost. The central program claims both again as platform leverage. One operational change has appeared in three stories.

**COLE:** One million is work that never left the cost base. Aegis assembles audit packets faster, then analysts use the released time on unpriced internal review. That may improve quality. Until the ledger identifies the output, customer effect, or cost action, it is unassigned capacity.

**ASHA:** Add the exclusions. Two point one, one point four, one point two, one point one, and one point zero. Six point eight million. Subtract six point eight from eleven. Four point two remains.

**COLE:** The four point two has four finance labels. One point five million is realized cost reduction visible in invoices or payroll-related spend. One point two is approved run-rate from actions already executed but not fully represented in the trailing period.

**ASHA:** Seven hundred thousand is avoided cost supported by an approved volume plan and a hiring decision that would otherwise have triggered. Eight hundred thousand is measured capacity applied to a named backlog or contracted volume. It is value evidence, not booked savings.

**COLE:** One point five plus one point two plus point seven plus point eight equals four point two. Julian does not add them to EBITDA. He preserves the categories so the company ledger can receive each benefit when, and only when, its accounting event occurs.

**ASHA:** This is the first rule of benefit accounting: a strong causal claim and a correct finance classification are separate tests. A workflow can cause time savings that never become cash. A cost reduction can be real but caused by procurement rather than AI. Both distinctions matter.

### THE FIVE PILOTS

**COLE:** The eleven-million slide combines five pilots. Three survive. Two are killed. Start with Keystone’s claim-evidence assembly workflow. It retrieves authorized documents, organizes a packet, drafts a triage rationale, and routes missing evidence to an adjuster.

**ASHA:** Its owner is the head of claims operations. The pre-period baseline is complete-packet handling time, material omission rate, reopen rate, contractor expense, and customer service level. The target is faster complete packets without higher omission or reopen rates.

**COLE:** Keystone cannot randomize insurers casually because each customer has its own implementation and claims mix. It stages rollout across comparable adjuster teams within two participating insurers and retains not-yet-activated teams as comparisons.

**ASHA:** The matched design controls for claim type, document count, severity band, adjuster tenure, and week. The workflow lowers adjusted handling time and external indexing work. Material omissions remain inside the agreed non-inferiority guardrail. The effect persists after the initial coaching period.

**COLE:** One million dollars survives. Four hundred thousand is realized contractor spend removed. Three hundred thousand is approved run-rate from ended temporary contracts. Three hundred thousand is capacity applied to an existing claims backlog, labeled capacity, not savings.

**ASHA:** Confidence is moderate, not high. Only two insurers participate. Teams know they are observed. Seasonal catastrophe volume could alter the mix. The decision is to scale evidence assembly to the next matched cohort, not to generalize to automated coverage or payment.

**COLE:** Keystone’s second pilot is a renewal-risk copilot. It scores customers and drafts intervention plans. The team selected accounts already receiving executive attention, compared their renewal outcomes with the full base, and credited every retained account to the copilot.

**ASHA:** That is selection bias. High-risk accounts with senior attention differ from ordinary accounts. Renewal is also a lagging event; most contracts have not reached decision. Leading activity—plans generated and meetings held—does not prove incremental retention.

**COLE:** The pilot is killed as a scale candidate. Keystone may keep it in observe mode to gather evidence. No capital release, no retained-ARR claim, and no automated customer action.

**ASHA:** Vela’s surviving pilot handles supplier exceptions. The agent gathers order state, approved supplier policy, inventory, price, delivery commitments, and credit status. At Q1 it drafts resolutions for humans; selected low-risk cases remain under explicit approval.

**COLE:** The owner is Vela’s vice president of operations. Baseline measures median exception age, expedite fees, rework, cancellation, and analyst minutes by exception class. The target is faster resolution with no increase in unauthorized substitution, margin leakage, or late delivery.

**ASHA:** Vela can randomize within eligible low-risk exception queues by alternating assignment at the case level. High-value, regulated, new-supplier, and safety-related exceptions are excluded. That protects customers and keeps treatment comparable.

**COLE:** There is contamination. Analysts learn from agent drafts and apply the pattern to comparison cases. That makes the comparison group improve. Instead of hiding it, the team records cross-use, analyzes an early uncontaminated window, and treats the later estimate as conservative but less clean.

**ASHA:** One point six million survives. Five hundred thousand is realized reduction in causally attributable expedite and rework charges. Four hundred thousand is approved run-rate from staffing and schedule actions already taken. Seven hundred thousand is avoided cost after processed volume crossed the pre-agreed hiring threshold.

**COLE:** Confidence is moderate. The randomized eligible queue is strong evidence for those classes, but the exclusion criteria limit generalization. Vela earns scale for the three proven low-risk classes, not for every supplier decision.

**ASHA:** Vela’s killed pilot is a demand-forecast copilot. It launched first with the strongest planners in the most stable categories. Forecast error improved against the prior quarter. So did forecast error in categories without the tool because a major supply shock ended.

**COLE:** The design has selection bias and a time trend. No matched comparison, no stable baseline, and no decision rule connecting forecast changes to inventory cash. The pilot team also reports gross working-capital movement without separating price and customer mix.

**ASHA:** Kill the value claim and the scale release. Research may continue inside its current budget if Lina values the learning, but no fund benefit and no common platform justification enter the ledger.

**COLE:** Aegis’s surviving workflow assembles audit evidence. It retrieves only authorized records, maps them to a customer’s control framework, identifies missing evidence, and drafts a package for analyst review. It does not change an industrial system or certify compliance.

**ASHA:** The owner is Aegis’s delivery leader. Baseline measures analyst hours, external audit-prep expense, missing-evidence reopen rate, delivery time, and customer acceptance. Rollout is staggered by audit team and customer renewal window.

**COLE:** Randomization is impractical because large customers schedule audits months ahead and contractual staffing differs. Aegis uses matched historical and concurrent cohorts, then estimates the change in treatment relative to the change in comparison. That is a difference-in-differences logic.

**ASHA:** In plain language: treatment improved after launch. Comparison also improved. The attributed effect is the treatment improvement minus the comparison improvement, subject to the assumption that their trends would otherwise have remained comparable.

**COLE:** That assumption is inspected, not asserted. The pre-period trends are plotted. Customer size, control-framework complexity, evidence-source count, analyst tenure, and renewal timing enter the match. One atypical industrial shutdown is excluded under a rule set before outcome review.

**ASHA:** One point six million survives. Six hundred thousand is realized external audit-prep spend removed. Five hundred thousand is approved run-rate from completed contractor changes. Five hundred thousand is capacity assigned to contracted audit volume, labeled capacity.

**COLE:** Confidence is moderate because matching cannot remove every unobserved difference. The workflow scales to similar evidence-assembly cases. It does not earn authority to propose or execute industrial-control changes.

### COUNTERFACTUALS WITHOUT THEATER

**ASHA:** Operators often hear, “We cannot run a perfect experiment.” True. The alternative is not no design. Choose the strongest ethical design the operating system permits and make its assumptions visible.

**COLE:** Randomization is powerful because, with enough units and correct execution, it balances observed and unobserved differences on average. The eligible Vela queue supports it. But randomization does not rescue a bad outcome definition, contamination, low adherence, or a sample too small for the decision.

**ASHA:** A staggered rollout randomizes or deliberately sequences timing. Everyone may eventually receive the workflow, while early and later groups provide comparison. It works well when implementation capacity already limits simultaneous launch.

**COLE:** Matching constructs a comparison from units with similar observed characteristics. It is useful when randomization is impractical. It cannot balance factors you did not measure. Calling a cohort “similar” without showing the match is not causal evidence.

**ASHA:** A pre-period baseline shows the starting level and trend. One week is rarely a trend. Seasonality, backlog age, customer mix, learning curves, pricing, and concurrent policy changes can all make a simple before-and-after difference lie.

**COLE:** Difference-in-differences subtracts the comparison group’s change from the treatment group’s change. If treatment handling time falls twelve minutes and comparison falls five, the estimated incremental reduction is seven. The design relies on credible parallel trends absent treatment.

**ASHA:** Interrupted time series can help when one system changes at a clear date and a long stable history exists. It asks whether the level or slope changes beyond prior variation. A concurrent event at launch can still confound the result.

**COLE:** Synthetic controls combine untreated units to approximate the treated unit’s pre-period behavior. They can be useful at company or market level, but a sophisticated model cannot compensate for weak input history or a moving definition.

**ASHA:** The method should match the decision. A reversible fifty-thousand-dollar extension may need directional evidence and a tight loss limit. A forty-five-million platform or a workforce action needs stronger attribution, longer observation, and finance confirmation.

### FROM AN ESTIMATE TO A CAPITAL RELEASE

**COLE:** A causal estimate is not yet a capital decision. Kevin needs magnitude, uncertainty, durability, cost, and downside. “Statistically different from zero” can still be economically trivial. A large point estimate with a wide range may be too fragile for an irreversible action.

**ASHA:** Start with the minimum decision effect. Before the analysis, state how much improvement would justify the next stage after full cost. That prevents a team from celebrating a tiny detectable effect simply because the sample is large.

**COLE:** Then choose the observation window. Too short and novelty, training support, or backlog selection dominates. Too long and capital waits after the decision is already obvious. The window follows the workflow’s operating cycle, not a generic thirty-day pilot calendar.

**ASHA:** Keystone needs enough completed packets to observe material omissions and reopens, not just handling time. Vela needs supplier outcomes after the exception closes. Aegis needs customer acceptance and missing-evidence returns. Each lag defines when the gate can honestly close.

**COLE:** Sample size is not “more is better.” It connects baseline variation, minimum decision effect, acceptable false-positive and false-negative risk, clustering, and available units. If there are only two insurer customers, the team should not disguise customer concentration with thousands of claim rows.

**ASHA:** The unit of assignment and unit of analysis must align. Randomizing individual cases while analysts share queues can create interference. Randomizing teams reduces contamination but produces fewer independent units. The design memo states the compromise.

**COLE:** Missing data gets a reason code. System outage, customer withdrawal, ineligible case, analyst override, and unresolved outcome mean different things. Dropping every difficult row after launch manufactures a clean result.

**ASHA:** Adherence also matters. An intention-to-treat view asks what happened to units assigned the workflow, including non-use. A per-protocol view asks what happened when it was used as designed. The first reflects deployment reality; the second can illuminate mechanism but invites selection bias.

**COLE:** Report both when useful, and do not substitute the larger number. If only the strongest users adopt, the per-user gain may be genuine while the company value remains small.

**ASHA:** Full cost follows the same cohort and period. Product labor spent before launch can be shown as investment. Production model, retrieval, orchestration, human review, exception support, implementation, and ongoing reliability belong in workload economics.

**COLE:** A benefit can pass the causal threshold and fail the economic threshold. Suppose the workflow causes one million of gross operating improvement but requires one point two million of recurring support. Keep the learning; reject the scale economics.

**ASHA:** Durability needs a decay test. Does performance persist when executive attention falls, volumes change, new employees enter, and easy cases are exhausted? A stage can release with a scheduled revalidation instead of pretending permanence.

**COLE:** Heterogeneity matters because averages can allocate badly. If novice adjusters improve and experts slow down, the correct rollout may target novices. If only simple supplier exceptions benefit, retain the eligibility boundary instead of marketing one portfolio-wide effect.

**ASHA:** Look for tail harm alongside average gain. A two-minute average improvement cannot compensate for a rare material omission if the loss is severe. Quality non-inferiority and exception-loss thresholds sit beside the value target.

**COLE:** Finally, connect the evidence to a release ladder. Learn releases no new capital. Bounded scale releases a defined cohort and budget. Broad scale requires repeatability across a second cohort, stable full-cost economics, and operating capacity. Structural cost action requires realized demand and workforce design.

**ASHA:** That ladder protects both directions. Finance cannot demand cash removal before the workflow has had time to change capacity. Product cannot demand permanent funding because an early cohort liked the tool. Evidence earns the next decision, not eternal approval.

### BIAS, CONTAMINATION, AND SURVIVORS

**COLE:** Selection bias enters when early users differ from later users. Enthusiastic teams volunteer. Strong managers adopt. Clean customers implement first. Their success may reflect readiness rather than the tool.

**ASHA:** Survivorship bias enters when failed activations disappear from the denominator. If ten customers start, three abandon implementation, and seven report success, the success rate is not seven out of seven. The three failures are product and operating evidence.

**COLE:** Regression to the mean enters when teams are chosen after an unusually bad month. Some improvement would occur even without intervention. A longer baseline and contemporaneous comparison help.

**ASHA:** Hawthorne effects enter when observation changes behavior. Pilot teams receive training, executive attention, and extra support. The scaled state may receive none. Record the support package as part of treatment cost.

**COLE:** Contamination enters when comparison users gain access, copy agent outputs, or share new process knowledge. Sometimes that understates the direct effect. Sometimes it reveals that the real intervention is training rather than software.

**ASHA:** Spillovers can be value, harm, or both. Faster Vela exceptions may shift work to supplier management. Aegis evidence assembly may create more remediation tickets. Keystone may reduce packet handling but increase adjuster review. Measure the next queue, not just the automated step.

**COLE:** Never eliminate spillovers by drawing the workflow boundary conveniently. The economic unit is the complete customer or operating outcome. Local time saved that produces downstream rework is not productivity.

### LEADING, LAGGING, AND FINANCE EVIDENCE

**ASHA:** A value contract carries an evidence ladder. Technical eligibility and quality come first. Then user adoption. Then workflow performance. Then customer or operating outcome. Then financial realization. Each answers a different question.

**COLE:** Leading metrics are useful for early stopping. Eligible cases, activation, draft acceptance, override, latency, error severity, exception routing, and support demand can reveal whether the service is viable before a renewal or cost action arrives.

**ASHA:** Lagging metrics show the outcome the investment was supposed to change: retention, paid volume, external spend, headcount cost, gross margin, cash conversion, or debt. They arrive later and are exposed to more confounders.

**COLE:** Finance sign-off is not ceremonial. Julian verifies the baseline, classification, source system, timing, and whether the benefit already appears elsewhere. The operating owner remains accountable for producing it. Finance does not own product adoption.

**ASHA:** Realized cost means the cost has left the ledger in the measured period. A canceled contractor invoice qualifies when causally linked and not replaced elsewhere. Run-rate means a completed action changes the forward annual cost but only part has reached LTM results.

**COLE:** Avoided cost means an approved future cost did not occur because a measured demand threshold was served differently. It is not EBITDA uplift against last year. Capacity means time or throughput was released and assigned to named demand. Revenue contribution needs contract and margin evidence.

**ASHA:** Confidence should change the decision. High confidence does not mean certainty. It means the design, execution, sample, and sensitivity support the stated use. Moderate confidence may justify bounded scale. Low confidence may justify learning but not a value claim.

**COLE:** Put a range beside point estimates. Show what happens when matches change, contaminated periods are removed, or outliers return. If the decision flips under a reasonable specification, the evidence is fragile.

### WHAT EXTERNAL STUDIES CAN AND CANNOT DO

**ASHA:** Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond’s “Generative AI at Work,” published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics on February 4, 2025, studied a particular customer-support setting and reported an average productivity gain around fifteen percent, with larger gains among less-experienced workers.

**COLE:** That is credible evidence that one deployed tool affected one workforce under one design. It is not a benchmark for claims, supplier orchestration, or industrial audit work. Kevin cannot multiply portfolio labor by fifteen percent and call the result a benefit.

**ASHA:** METR’s July 10, 2025 randomized study found experienced open-source developers working on familiar repositories took about nineteen percent longer with the tested early-2025 AI tools, despite expecting speed gains. That study also has a bounded setting and tool vintage.

**COLE:** Put the studies together and the lesson is not “AI works” or “AI fails.” It is that effects vary by task, worker, workflow, tool, and measurement design. External research informs priors and methods. Local capital still requires local evidence.

**ASHA:** OpenAI’s July 14, 2026 investment guidance recommends staged measurement of AI capabilities. It is a provider’s operating guidance, not independent causal proof. The portfolio uses the staging logic and still requires company evidence and Julian’s sign-off.

### ETHICAL LIMITS

**COLE:** Not every counterfactual deserves an experiment. Do not withhold a safety control known to be necessary. Do not randomize legal compliance. Do not expose customers to undisclosed material risk for statistical cleanliness.

**ASHA:** The design can randomize timing among equally eligible low-risk units, compare draft assistance while keeping human accountability, or use historical and matched cohorts. Ethics and causality are constraints to solve together, not excuses to abandon rigor.

**COLE:** Customer consent, contractual commitments, employee monitoring, and data-use rights remain local legal and governance questions. This fictional case is not legal advice. The operator documents the purpose, minimum data, review path, and stop rule.

**ASHA:** There is also an ethical cost to weak measurement. False savings can trigger cuts that overload the remaining team. A misleading quality claim can expand risk. A biased rollout can allocate service to already advantaged customers. Measurement design is part of operating accountability.

### THREE CREDIBLE CHOICES

**COLE:** Choice one: approve the forty-five-million common build. The eleven-million claim suggests a short payback and the companies want momentum. The risk is scaling categories that do not reconcile and centralizing before transfer proof.

**ASHA:** Choice two: reject common measurement and let each CEO decide. Local teams understand their workflows and can move quickly. The risk is incompatible definitions, duplicated benefits, and capital decisions that cannot be compared.

**COLE:** Choice three: common causal proof rules, company-local value ownership, and milestone funding. Use randomization or staggered rollout where practical, matching and pre-period trends where not, explicit contamination checks, and finance-owned realization definitions.

**ASHA:** This option adds friction. Two pilots will lose their success label. Founders will argue that finance is slowing product. The benefit is that capital follows evidence rather than optimism.

**COLE:** Kevin, choose now. Write which claims you reject, which workflows you scale, what evidence design each receives, and whether the choice requires another fund call.

### THE COUNTERFACTUAL BENEFIT LEDGER

**COLE:** The artifact begins with a causal value contract. Name the decision, unit of analysis, eligible population, treatment, comparison, pre-period, outcome, leading indicators, guardrails, owner, timing, cost, and confidence standard.

**ASHA:** Add a counterfactual sentence: “Without this workflow, we expect this outcome for this population over this period because…” If the because clause is only intuition, mark the confidence low and improve the design.

**COLE:** The rollout sheet records assignment, activation, adherence, crossover, exclusion, and missing data. Analyze assigned treatment as well as actual use when selection into use could bias the result.

**ASHA:** The benefit ledger then bridges operational effect to finance. Baseline volume times incremental effect produces gross operational change. Subtract workload cost, implementation, support, downstream rework, and overlapping initiatives. Classify what remains.

**COLE:** Every line carries a source system and evidence link. Every manual adjustment has a reason and approver. Every benefit has a start date and expiry or revalidation date. Benefits do not become immortal because one cohort succeeded.

**ASHA:** Decision thresholds sit at the bottom. Learn, scale bounded, scale broad, hold, or kill. The threshold should state both value and risk. A positive mean with a severe tail loss may fail.

### THE OPERATOR DRILL

**COLE:** Kevin, take one AI benefit claim from your own world. Write the reported number at the top. Then build a waterfall through reassigned labor, external price effects, forecast avoidance, duplicates, work still in the cost base, and full workload cost.
