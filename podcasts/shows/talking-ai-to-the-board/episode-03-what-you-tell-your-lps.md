### What LPs want to know

**MARCUS:** Start with the audience. What do limited partners actually want to know about AI?

**CLAIRE:** In my experience, five things, roughly in this order. First, risk to the portfolio: is AI going to hurt any of the companies they're invested in, and does the firm have a view? That's become the most urgent question since the software repricing earlier this year. Second, value creation: is the firm using AI to make portfolio companies better, and is there evidence it's working?

**MARCUS:** Third.

**CLAIRE:** Governance: does the firm manage AI risks in the portfolio responsibly, given regulation, reputational risk and the firm's own responsible investment commitments? Fourth, the firm's own use of AI: how the firm uses AI in sourcing, diligence, monitoring and reporting, and how it protects confidential information. And fifth, the strategic question: is the firm's investment strategy positioned well for an AI-changed economy, or exposed?

**MARCUS:** And who's asking?

**CLAIRE:** Different LPs emphasize different things. Large pension funds and sovereign funds often focus on governance and risk, alongside their responsible investment frameworks. Endowments and foundations often ask about strategy and disruption. Consultants who advise LPs on manager selection ask about all of it, and they compare answers across firms. So the firm needs a consistent core answer, with depth available on each topic.

**MARCUS:** And what do they not want?

**CLAIRE:** Hype. LPs have heard a lot of AI marketing in the last three years, from managers in every asset class. A firm that leads with AI buzzwords gets discounted. A firm that leads with evidence, including the problems, gets credibility.

### The rules on claims

**MARCUS:** Let's do the rules early, because they shape everything else. What governs what a US private equity firm can say about AI?

**CLAIRE:** For registered investment advisers, which includes most mid-sized and larger private equity firms in the US, the main rule is the SEC's marketing rule, which took effect in 2022. It applies to advertisements, which include most communications offering advisory services to investors, including fundraising materials for private funds. It prohibits untrue statements of material fact, and material statements the adviser doesn't have a reasonable basis to believe it can substantiate if the SEC asks.

**MARCUS:** Substantiation is the key word.

**CLAIRE:** It's central. If a fundraising deck says "our AI value creation program delivered thirty million dollars of EBITDA across the portfolio," the firm needs to be able to show, on request, how that number was calculated and that it's supportable. The evidence grades we discussed last episode are exactly what substantiation requires.

**MARCUS:** What else does the rule cover?

**CLAIRE:** It prohibits including or excluding performance results in a way that's unfair or unbalanced, so you can't show the three AI successes and leave out the five that didn't work if the overall impression is misleading. It requires fair and balanced treatment of material risks and limitations. And it has strict rules on hypothetical performance, meaning performance results that weren't actually achieved by any portfolio.

**MARCUS:** How does hypothetical performance relate to AI?

**CLAIRE:** Directly. If a firm presents "projected AI value" across its portfolio, or models how its returns would have looked with AI initiatives in place, that may be hypothetical performance, which can only be shown to investors under specific conditions, with policies and procedures, and with information that lets the audience understand the assumptions and risks. Projections of AI value presented casually in a fundraising deck are a compliance risk.

**MARCUS:** And the SEC is watching.

**CLAIRE:** The SEC's examination division has named AI as a focus area in its annual priorities, including whether advisers' representations about their AI capabilities are accurate. And beyond the marketing rule, the general anti-fraud provisions apply to all communications with investors, including quarterly letters and annual meeting presentations. The emphasis of SEC enforcement changes with leadership, but the rules themselves are stable, and limited partners can also bring their own claims if they're misled.

**MARCUS:** And Europe?

**CLAIRE:** European fund marketing rules also require that marketing communications be fair, clear and not misleading, and that risks be presented alongside benefits. National regulators enforce them. The same practical standard applies: say what you can prove, and present risks honestly.

### Two kinds of AI claim

**MARCUS:** Let's be precise about what firms claim. What kinds of AI claims show up in LP communications?

**CLAIRE:** Two kinds. Claims about the portfolio: AI value created in portfolio companies, AI risk managed, AI governance in place. And claims about the firm: that the firm itself uses AI to source deals, run diligence or monitor the portfolio better than competitors. Both need substantiation, but the second kind is where AI-washing risk is highest.

**MARCUS:** Why?

**CLAIRE:** Because firm-level AI claims are often aspirational. "We use proprietary AI to identify opportunities before our competitors" might mean the firm has a sophisticated data platform that has demonstrably sourced deals. Or it might mean a few associates use a general AI assistant to summarize information memoranda. If it's the latter, the claim isn't true in the way an investor would understand it.

**MARCUS:** So how should a firm talk about its own AI use?

**CLAIRE:** Concretely and modestly. "Our deal team uses enterprise AI tools to accelerate the review of data room documents, which has reduced first-pass review time on a typical deal. Investment decisions remain with the investment committee." That's true, useful and defensible. "AI-driven investment strategy" is a phrase that should make your compliance officer nervous unless it's literally true and provable.

**MARCUS:** And "proprietary"?

**CLAIRE:** Same caution as in The AI Diligence Room. If the firm calls its AI proprietary, it should be able to say what's proprietary: its own data, its own models, its own methods. Using a commercial AI tool with good prompts isn't proprietary AI.

### The quarterly letter

**MARCUS:** Let's go through the channels. Start with the quarterly report or letter to limited partners. Where does AI belong?

**CLAIRE:** In two places. In the portfolio company updates, where AI initiatives should be mentioned like any other value creation work, when they're material: "The company launched AI-assisted after-hours call handling across all branches this quarter, contributing to a three percent increase in booked jobs versus the prior year." Specific, measured, and in context.

**MARCUS:** And the second place?

**CLAIRE:** In the risk discussion. If the firm has portfolio companies with significant AI disruption exposure, the quarterly report should reflect it, especially if it affected the valuation. "The company's valuation reflects lower multiples for comparable public companies following the repricing of AI-exposed software businesses earlier this year. The company has begun transitioning its pricing model and launched an AI agent product." That's honest, and it explains the mark.

**MARCUS:** Should there be a separate AI section?

**CLAIRE:** Once a year, perhaps, a short portfolio AI summary, drawn from the investment committee's review: realized value by evidence grade, governance status, the disruption watch list in general terms, and the main actions. In the quarterly letters, integrated mentions are better. A separate AI section every quarter tends to become a marketing piece.

**MARCUS:** And consistency?

**CLAIRE:** Critical. The numbers in the letter should match the fund's register and the companies' board reports. If an LP later sees a different number in a fundraising deck, or hears a different story from a portfolio company CEO at the annual meeting, credibility suffers.

### The annual meeting

**MARCUS:** The annual meeting. Many firms now put AI on the agenda. How do you do it well?

**CLAIRE:** Show, don't announce. The most effective AI sessions I've seen at annual meetings feature a portfolio company CEO or COO, not the firm's partners, talking for fifteen minutes about one AI initiative: the problem, what they did, what went wrong, the measured result, and what's next. LPs remember a candid operator far longer than a slide of logos.

**MARCUS:** And the firm's part?

**CLAIRE:** A short framing: how the firm approaches AI value creation and risk across the portfolio, the common language and evidence grades, a few portfolio-level numbers with their grades, and the disruption view. Then questions. And be ready for hard ones, like "which of your companies are most exposed to AI, and what are you doing about it?"

**MARCUS:** How should you answer that one?

**CLAIRE:** Directly, at the appropriate level of detail. "Two companies are on our high exposure list. Both are software or services businesses where AI is changing what customers pay for. Both have boards actively working on repositioning plans, which include new pricing and AI-based products, and we've factored the exposure into our valuations and exit planning." LPs want to know that the firm sees the risk clearly. Dodging the question is far more worrying to them than the exposure itself.

**MARCUS:** And demos?

**CLAIRE:** Live demos can be powerful but risky. An AI phone agent demo that works well is memorable. One that fails in front of two hundred investors is memorable too, for the wrong reasons. If you demo, rehearse it, and have a recording as backup. And never demo something that isn't in production at a portfolio company.

### The due diligence questionnaire

**MARCUS:** Now the due diligence questionnaire, which LPs send when considering a commitment to a new fund. What AI questions are showing up?

**CLAIRE:** They're growing every year. Typical ones: does the firm have an AI policy for its own use, and what does it cover? How does the firm protect confidential information when using AI tools? How does the firm assess AI opportunities and risks in diligence? How does it oversee AI governance in portfolio companies? Has the firm or any portfolio company had a material AI-related incident? How does the firm consider AI disruption in its investment strategy and valuations? And sometimes, does the firm use AI in investment decisions, and with what human oversight?

**MARCUS:** How should a firm answer?

**CLAIRE:** With a prepared, compliance-reviewed set of answers that are specific, consistent and evidenced. For each question: what the firm does, since when, how it's applied in practice, and an example or a number. "Yes, we have an AI policy" is a weak answer. "We adopted a firm AI policy in early 2025, reviewed annually. All staff use an enterprise AI assistant with contractual data protections and complete annual training. Confidential deal and portfolio data may only be used in approved tools. Our portfolio framework requires each company to maintain an AI inventory and tiered controls, and fifteen of eighteen companies have completed theirs, with the remaining three in progress." That's a strong answer.

**MARCUS:** And the incident question?

**CLAIRE:** Answer it honestly, with the firm's definition of material. If there was a significant incident, describe it briefly, with the response and the lessons. Many LPs view a well-handled incident as evidence of a working system. An answer of "none" that later turns out to be untrue is far worse than a candid answer.

**MARCUS:** And keeping the answers current?

**CLAIRE:** Review the AI section of the questionnaire library every six months. AI governance at firms is changing quickly, and an answer that was accurate a year ago may now understate or overstate what the firm does. And make sure the people answering ad hoc LP questions use the same library.

### Writing an AI case study

**MARCUS:** Case studies are central to fundraising. How do you write an AI case study that survives scrutiny?

**CLAIRE:** I'd follow a structure with seven parts. The company and the problem, in plain terms. The baseline, with the period and the source. What was done: the workflow, the tool, the integration, the people involved, and the time it took. The result, with the measurement method, the attribution approach and the evidence grade. The costs, one-time and ongoing, and the net value. What went wrong and what was learned. And the current status: whether it's at scale, sustained, and spreading to other companies.

**MARCUS:** That's more honest than most case studies.

**CLAIRE:** It's more credible, which is what matters in fundraising. LPs and their consultants read hundreds of case studies. The ones that include a baseline, a method, the costs and a candid "what went wrong" stand out, because they read like evidence rather than marketing. And they're far easier to substantiate if the SEC or an LP asks.

**MARCUS:** And selection?

**CLAIRE:** That's the marketing rule's cherry-picking point. If the firm presents three AI case studies, it should be able to explain how they were chosen and whether they're representative. A good practice is to present the case studies alongside the portfolio-level numbers, by evidence grade, so the audience can see that the case studies are examples, not the whole picture.

**MARCUS:** And attribution between the firm and management?

**CLAIRE:** Be careful about claiming credit. If a portfolio company's management team did most of the work, say so. LPs know that value creation is a partnership, and firms that claim all the credit for management's work lose credibility, including with the management teams they'll want to back again.

### Talking about disruption and the marks

**MARCUS:** Let's spend more time on disruption, because it's the topic LPs are most anxious about. How should a firm talk about it?

**CLAIRE:** Proactively. Don't wait for LPs to ask. Once a year, give a portfolio-level view: the share of the portfolio's value in sectors or business models with significant AI exposure, how the firm assessed it, which companies are on the watch list in general terms, and what's being done. And explain how the exposure is reflected in valuations, especially where comparable public companies have been repriced.

**MARCUS:** What if the marks haven't moved?

**CLAIRE:** Then explain why, with evidence. "Although public comparables in this sector fell, this company's revenue is in exclusive data and regulated workflows, with net revenue retention stable at one hundred and eight percent and no AI-related seat reductions at renewal. We therefore weight comparables with similar characteristics." That might be a perfectly good reason. But it needs to be stated, because LPs and their auditors will compare the firm's marks with public market movements.

**MARCUS:** And when the news is bad?

**CLAIRE:** Say it early. If a company's prospects have materially worsened because of AI, the mark should reflect it, and the letter should explain it. LPs forgive bad news. They don't forgive surprises at the end of a fund's life, when a company that was carried at a high mark is sold for much less.

**MARCUS:** And continuation vehicles?

**CLAIRE:** That's an important case. When a firm sells a company from an older fund to a new vehicle it also manages, called a continuation vehicle, the existing LPs choose whether to sell or roll over, and the firm is on both sides. AI exposure and AI value claims in that process need extra care, because the firm's conflict of interest is obvious. An independent valuation and full disclosure of the AI risks and opportunities protect everyone.

### Fundraising: the firm's AI story

**MARCUS:** Let's talk about fundraising itself. How should AI feature in a firm's pitch for its next fund?

**CLAIRE:** As part of the value creation capability, with evidence, and as part of the strategy, with a clear view of risk. In the value creation section: the firm's approach, the portfolio-level realized value by evidence grade, two or three representative case studies, and the capability behind it: the operating team, the playbooks, the vendor bench. In the strategy section: how AI changes the opportunity in the firm's target sectors, both as a lever and as a disruption risk, and how the firm selects and prices deals accordingly.

**MARCUS:** And the firm's own AI use?

**CLAIRE:** Briefly, and concretely. How the deal team and operating team use AI tools, what that has changed in practice, and how the firm protects confidential information. Avoid claiming that AI makes the firm's investment decisions better unless you can prove it.

**MARCUS:** What about sector strategies built around AI, like "we invest in businesses AI will make more valuable"?

**CLAIRE:** That can be a good thesis, if it's specific and supported. "We buy fragmented services businesses with high labor intensity, put them on a common platform, and use AI to centralize the back office" is a clear strategy LPs can evaluate, with the roll-up risks we've discussed. "We invest in the AI economy" is too vague to evaluate, and LPs will ask what it means.

**MARCUS:** And track record?

**CLAIRE:** Most firms don't yet have a full fund cycle of AI-driven returns to point to, because AI value creation at scale is recent. Be honest about that. Show realized operational results and the trajectory, and don't extrapolate them into fund returns that haven't happened. That extrapolation is where hypothetical performance risk sits.

### Responsible investment and AI

**MARCUS:** Many LPs have responsible investment or ESG frameworks. Where does AI fit?

**CLAIRE:** Increasingly as its own topic, alongside data privacy and cybersecurity. LPs with responsible investment commitments want to know that the firm considers AI risks to people: workers, customers and communities. That includes fair treatment in AI-driven hiring and customer decisions, transparency, privacy, safety, and the workforce effects of AI-driven changes.

**MARCUS:** The workforce question.

**CLAIRE:** It's sensitive and important. Some LPs, especially public pension funds whose members are workers, care about how AI affects jobs in the portfolio. A firm should be ready to explain its approach: in most real-economy companies, AI absorbing growth rather than cutting roles, redeployment and training where roles change, and compliance with notice and consultation requirements where reductions happen. Honest answers about specific situations are better than general reassurance.

**MARCUS:** And reporting?

**CLAIRE:** Fold AI into the firm's existing responsible investment reporting, with a short section on AI governance across the portfolio: the framework, completion rates, high-risk uses and how they're controlled, material incidents, and the firm's own policy. Use the same numbers as everywhere else.

---

### Co-investors and LP advisory committees

**MARCUS:** What about co-investors and the LP advisory committee?

**CLAIRE:** Co-investors, who invest alongside the fund in specific deals, increasingly want the AI diligence findings for those deals: the claim test, the stack review, the disruption assessment and the hundred-day plan. Sharing a clear AI diligence summary, appropriately, builds confidence and saves them duplicating the work.

**MARCUS:** And the advisory committee?

**CLAIRE:** The LP advisory committee typically reviews conflicts and certain valuation questions. If AI disruption has significantly affected valuations, or if a continuation vehicle involves a company with AI exposure or AI value claims, the committee should get a full briefing. Treat the advisory committee as the most informed and skeptical audience you have, and prepare accordingly.

### The hard questions, rehearsed

**MARCUS:** Let's make this practical. Annual meeting, the floor opens for questions, and an LP from a large pension plan stands up. What are the questions you should have rehearsed answers for?

**CLAIRE:** I'd prepare at least six. The first is the blunt one: "Which of your companies are most at risk from AI, and what are you doing about it?" The second: "How much of the value creation you're claiming from AI is realized, and how much is projected?" The third: "What are you spending on AI across the portfolio, and what's the return?" The fourth: "How is the firm itself using AI, and what are you doing with our data?" The fifth: "How do you govern AI risk in the portfolio, who owns it?" And the sixth, which is coming up more and more: "Has AI changed how you underwrite new deals?"

**MARCUS:** Take the first one. Most at risk. The temptation is to say "we've looked at it carefully and we're comfortable."

**CLAIRE:** And that answer lands badly, because every LP in the room has read the same headlines you have. They watched public software and information services stocks fall sharply when a new AI product launched. They know some business models are exposed. "We're comfortable" sounds like you haven't looked.

**MARCUS:** So what does a good answer sound like?

**CLAIRE:** Something like this: "We ran a disruption review on every company last year, using the same framework we use in diligence. We grouped them into three buckets. Most of the portfolio is in the lowest-risk bucket, because the work is physical, regulated or relationship heavy. Two companies are in the middle bucket, where AI changes how the product is delivered, and both have plans to build AI into the product that their boards approved this year. One company is in the highest-risk bucket. We've reflected that in its valuation, we've changed its plan, and we'll report on it specifically every quarter." That answer names a method, gives a distribution, admits exposure, and describes action.

**MARCUS:** Without naming the company in a room full of people.

**CLAIRE:** You may or may not name it, depending on your confidentiality obligations and what's already in the quarterly reports. But you don't hide that it exists. LPs are far more forgiving of a disclosed problem than a discovered one.

**MARCUS:** Second question. Realized versus projected.

**CLAIRE:** This is where all the work from episode one pays off. If your portfolio companies report AI value using the evidence grades we talked about, you can answer with a number in each bucket. "Across the portfolio, we can point to a certain amount of annual EBITDA impact that's realized and measured against a baseline. There's another amount in pilots with early evidence. And there's a larger amount in plans that we haven't counted." If you can't split it that way, the honest answer is "we don't yet measure it consistently, and here's what we're doing about that," which is better than a number you can't defend.

**MARCUS:** Third question. Spend and return.

**CLAIRE:** Have the spend number. It should include software, model and compute costs, outside advisors and consultants, and the internal team time you can estimate. Then the return, again split by evidence grade. And be ready for the follow-up, which is "is that spend going up?" For most firms the honest answer is yes, and the reason should be specific: more companies moving from pilot to production, which costs more to run but also produces the realized value.

**MARCUS:** Fourth. The firm's own use and their data.

**CLAIRE:** This one has teeth, because LPs hand you confidential information, and their own investment committees are asking whether that information is being put into AI tools. The answer should cover which tools the firm uses, whether they're enterprise versions with contractual commitments not to train on your data, what data is allowed into them, who approved that, and how it's monitored. If you use AI to read LP side letters or questionnaires, say so and explain the controls.

**MARCUS:** Fifth, governance.

**CLAIRE:** Name the owner. Usually there's an operating partner or a portfolio AI lead, a policy that applies at the fund and recommended practices for portfolio companies, and a regular review at the firm's risk or operating committee. And be specific about what the policy requires of companies, because "we encourage good practice" is not governance.

**MARCUS:** And sixth. Underwriting.

**CLAIRE:** LPs want to know whether you've changed what you buy and how you price it. A good answer describes the disruption test in diligence, how AI opportunities show up in the value creation plan before you close, and, critically, how you avoid paying for AI upside that hasn't been proven. If you can say "we walked away from a deal because of AI risk," that's actually a powerful signal of discipline.

**MARCUS:** One more thing about rehearsal. Who answers?

**CLAIRE:** Decide in advance. The managing partner should own the strategy answer. The operating partner or AI lead should own the evidence. And compliance should have reviewed every number anyone is likely to say out loud, because the annual meeting is marketing, and an off-the-cuff claim is still a claim.

### When the news is bad

**MARCUS:** Let's spend some time on the hardest letter to write. A portfolio company has been hurt by AI. Customers are leaving for cheaper AI-native alternatives, the growth plan is broken, and you're writing the mark down. How do you tell LPs?

**CLAIRE:** Early, plainly, and with a plan. The worst version is the one where the write-down shows up in the numbers before it shows up in the narrative. LPs see a big drop in the mark with two vague sentences about "market headwinds," and they immediately wonder what else you're not telling them.

**MARCUS:** What does early look like?

**CLAIRE:** It means that when your quarterly review first flags the company as at risk, the letter says so, even before the mark moves. "We've identified increased competitive pressure from AI-based alternatives in this company's core market. We're watching three indicators: new customer win rates, renewal rates in the affected segment, and pricing. Here's where they stand." Then if the indicators deteriorate and the mark falls, nobody is surprised.

**MARCUS:** And plainly?

**CLAIRE:** Use the word. Don't say "evolving technology landscape." Say "AI tools now let customers do a large part of what this product does at much lower cost." LPs respect clarity. It also helps them, because they have their own boards and they need to explain the mark too.

**MARCUS:** And the plan.

**CLAIRE:** The plan has to be real, and it should cover a few options honestly. Can the company build AI into its own product fast enough to defend its customers? Should it narrow its focus to the segments where its data, integrations or relationships still matter? Does it need new leadership with different skills? Is a sale to a strategic buyer the right move, even at a lower price? You don't have to have decided, but you need to show that you're weighing real options with a timetable.

**MARCUS:** What about the valuation methodology itself? LPs will ask how you arrived at the new mark.

**CLAIRE:** Be ready to explain which inputs changed. In practice it's usually some combination of lower forecast growth, lower terminal assumptions, and comparable public company multiples that have fallen. If public peers in a category have been rerated because of AI, LPs will expect your marks to reflect that, and they'll ask why if they don't. Consistency matters here. If you marked down one company because its public comparables fell, but you didn't apply the same logic to another company with similar exposure, someone will notice.

**MARCUS:** Is there a version of this where the GP comes out looking good?

**CLAIRE:** Yes, honestly. A GP that spotted the risk early, told LPs, acted decisively and limited the damage often earns more trust than one whose portfolio was never tested. LPs know that no manager avoids every problem. What they're judging is how you handle them.

### Reporting templates and data requests

**MARCUS:** Let's talk about the more routine side, which still takes a lot of time. LPs increasingly send structured data requests about AI. How should a GP handle those?

**CLAIRE:** Build the data once and use it everywhere. The worst pattern is a team scrambling to answer each LP's request separately, producing slightly different numbers each time. That's exactly the inconsistency we warned about.

**MARCUS:** What should the data set include?

**CLAIRE:** For each portfolio company: whether it has an AI policy, whether it has a named owner, what AI systems are in production and what they do, whether any are high risk under rules like the EU AI Act, the disruption risk rating from your review, AI spend, and AI value by evidence grade. At the fund level: the firm's own policy, the tools it uses, training, and any incidents.

**MARCUS:** Incidents. That's sensitive.

**CLAIRE:** It is, and you need a clear definition so that you're consistent. An AI incident might be a data leak through an AI tool, a customer harmed by an AI decision, a regulatory inquiry, or a material system failure. Decide what you'll report, at what threshold, and stick to it. LPs would rather see a small number of well-described incidents than a suspicious zero.

**MARCUS:** And how does this fit with the standard reporting templates LPs already use?

**CLAIRE:** The industry association for LPs, ILPA, updated its reporting and performance templates, and many LPs are pushing GPs to adopt them. Those templates are mostly about fees, expenses and performance, not AI specifically. But the same discipline applies: standard definitions, consistent periods, and data that reconciles. If your AI spend is passed through to the fund or to portfolio companies, it may also show up in the expense reporting, and the two should match.

**MARCUS:** What about side letters? Some LPs negotiate specific reporting rights.

**CLAIRE:** Increasingly, yes, especially large public pensions and sovereign funds with their own responsible investment policies. Some ask for specific disclosures about AI governance or for notification of material AI incidents. Track those obligations carefully, because missing a side letter commitment is a contractual problem, not just a relationship one. And remember that under the most-favored-nation clauses common in fund documents, what you offer one LP may need to be offered to others.

**MARCUS:** Any advice for the team that actually fills these in?

**CLAIRE:** Two things. Give them a single source of truth, ideally a simple database that portfolio companies update each quarter, with the AI lead reviewing it. And give them a library of approved answers to the narrative questions, reviewed by compliance, with a date on each. Ironically, this is a good place to use AI yourself. Drafting a questionnaire answer from an approved library is a sensible use, as long as a person checks every answer before it goes out and the tool meets your data rules.

### The firm's own AI use, in more depth

**MARCUS:** We touched on the firm's own use of AI. Let's go a bit deeper, because I think it's becoming a fundraising question in its own right.

**CLAIRE:** It is. LPs want to know two things. Is the firm getting more productive and making better decisions with AI? And is it doing so safely?

**MARCUS:** Start with productive.

**CLAIRE:** Concrete examples work best. Using AI to screen and summarize a much larger volume of potential deals. Using it in diligence to read data rooms, contracts and customer reviews faster. Using it to monitor portfolio company data and flag problems sooner. Using it in investor relations to draft questionnaire answers from an approved library. For each one, say what it changed. "Our deal team now reviews twice as many companies at the first screen" is a claim you can substantiate if you track it.

**MARCUS:** And where it hasn't worked?

**CLAIRE:** Say that too, briefly. "We tried AI-generated first drafts of investment committee memos and stopped, because the time saved writing was lost checking." That kind of honesty makes the successes more believable.

**MARCUS:** And safely?

**CLAIRE:** Here the LP is thinking about three risks. Confidential information leaking, including their own. Decisions being made on AI outputs that nobody checked. And regulatory exposure, because the SEC has paid attention to how advisers use AI and data analytics in dealing with clients, and to whether advisers' statements about their AI use are accurate. The answer covers approved tools, data rules, human review of anything material, training, and monitoring.

**MARCUS:** Should a firm claim to be "AI-driven" in its investment process?

**CLAIRE:** Only if it's true and provable, and even then I'd be careful with the phrase. The SEC's AI-washing cases were about advisers who said they used AI in their investment process when they didn't, or not in the way they described. If AI helps your team find and analyze deals, say exactly that. If your investment decisions are made by people at an investment committee, which they almost certainly are, don't imply otherwise.

### Writing for the LP's own investment committee

**MARCUS:** One perspective we haven't covered: the person you're writing to at the LP has a boss too.

**CLAIRE:** Right, and it's easy to forget. The investment officer at a pension plan or endowment who reads your letter usually has to summarize it for their own investment committee or board, often alongside dozens of other managers. If your AI message can't survive being condensed into two sentences in their memo, it probably won't get through.

**MARCUS:** So write the two sentences for them.

**CLAIRE:** Exactly. Somewhere near the top, say clearly: here's our portfolio's AI exposure, here's the value we've measured, here's the main risk and what we're doing about it. If the LP's investment officer can lift that almost unchanged into their own report, you've done them a favor and made sure your message is the one their committee hears.

**MARCUS:** And their committee may have its own AI questions.

**CLAIRE:** Increasingly, yes. Many institutional investors are developing their own views on AI risk across their whole portfolio, public and private. They want to know whether their private equity managers are on top of it. Anything that helps the LP show their committee that the private markets portfolio is being managed thoughtfully on AI makes you an easier manager to keep, and an easier one to re-up with.

**MARCUS:** Any format advice?

**CLAIRE:** Keep the AI section in the same place each quarter, with the same structure, so it's easy to compare. Use the same definitions and evidence grades every time. And when something changes, say what changed, rather than making the reader work it out.

---

### Common mistakes with LPs

**MARCUS:** Let's do the mistakes list.

**CLAIRE:** Six. Leading with buzzwords instead of evidence. Claiming firm-level AI capabilities that are really just staff using general tools. Showing projected AI value as if it were realized. Cherry-picking case studies without showing the full picture. Ignoring disruption risk, or mentioning it only when asked. And inconsistency between channels: different numbers in the letter, the deck and the CEO's annual meeting talk.

**MARCUS:** And the single most important habit?

**CLAIRE:** Run every AI claim to LPs through the same question your compliance officer would ask: if the SEC or an LP asked us to prove this tomorrow, could we? If the answer is yes, with documents, say it. If the answer is "sort of," rewrite it until it's yes.

### Takeaways

**MARCUS:** Sum it up.

**CLAIRE:** LPs want to know about AI risk to the portfolio, evidence of value creation, governance, the firm's own use, and strategic positioning, roughly in that order. Lead with evidence, not hype.

**MARCUS:** The rules?

**CLAIRE:** For US registered advisers, the marketing rule requires substantiation of material claims, fair and balanced presentation, and strict handling of hypothetical performance, which includes projected AI value. The SEC has charged AI-washing and named AI as an examination priority. European rules require marketing to be fair, clear and not misleading. Say what you can prove.

**MARCUS:** And the channels?

**CLAIRE:** In quarterly letters, integrate AI into company updates and risk discussions, and explain AI's effect on marks. At annual meetings, let operators tell candid stories. Keep a compliance-reviewed questionnaire library, specific and current. Write case studies with baselines, methods, costs, failures and evidence grades. Talk about disruption proactively. In fundraising, present AI as an evidenced capability and a clear-eyed strategy. And keep every number consistent across every channel.
