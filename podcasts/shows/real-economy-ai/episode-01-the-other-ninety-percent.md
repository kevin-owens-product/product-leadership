# Episode 1: The Other Ninety Percent
## "Why AI Value in Private Equity Lives Outside Software"

**Duration:** ~7 minutes
**Hosts:** Maya & Grant
**Podcast:** AI in the Real Economy

---

### Cold open

[INTRO MUSIC]

**MAYA:** Picture two portfolio companies owned by the same fund. One is a software business with two hundred engineers. The other runs four hundred HVAC technicians across three states. Which one has more to gain from AI this year?

**GRANT:** Most people would say the software company. And most people, I think, are wrong. The software company has already been told for three years that AI is its future. The HVAC business is still losing a quarter of its inbound calls to voicemail.

**MAYA:** Welcome to AI in the Real Economy, the show about AI value creation in the businesses that make, fix, deliver and care for things. I'm Maya.

**GRANT:** And I'm Grant. Over four episodes, we're going to look at services, healthcare, industrial and consumer businesses, the kinds of companies that make up most private equity portfolios, and ask where AI actually moves EBITDA. Everything we cite comes from public sources, and when a number comes from a vendor or a fund's own reporting, we'll say so.

---

### Why the real economy

**MAYA:** Let's start with the obvious objection. Isn't AI a technology story? Why would a fund look for it in a plumbing roll-up or a contract manufacturer?

**GRANT:** Because the value of AI shows up wherever there's a lot of repetitive judgment work done by people, and wherever revenue leaks because nobody had time to catch it. Non-software companies are full of both. Call centers. Dispatch desks. Estimating. Claims and billing. Quality inspection. Scheduling. Collections.

**MAYA:** And the labor market makes it urgent.

**GRANT:** It does. A Foley and Lardner piece from May this year on AI, automation and robotics in private equity singled out labor-constrained industries: logistics yards, food service, construction, field services and healthcare delivery. Places with labor shortages, wage inflation or big seasonal swings. In those businesses, AI isn't mainly about cutting people. It's about doing the work you can't hire for.

**MAYA:** There's also an exit argument.

**GRANT:** The same piece made it. With a tight exit environment and longer hold periods, value creation during the hold matters more. And they drew a useful distinction: one-time cost cuts tend to get competed away, while building AI into how the business operates gives you more durable margin. That's the story a buyer will pay for.

---

### What the leaders are reporting

**MAYA:** Give me evidence that this is real and not just conference talk.

**GRANT:** Permira's a good example of a large fund that has talked publicly about it. In an interview with Private Equity International in September 2025, their team described AI value creation beyond the tech sector: in services, healthcare and consumer businesses, where AI is applied inside sector-specific workflows. Two of their portfolio examples stood out to me.

**MAYA:** Go on.

**GRANT:** In pharma services, they said AI in drug formulation cut a cycle from about ten weeks to four or five days. In clinical services, they said AI in adverse event case processing cut intake time by a quarter and improved auditability. Those are the fund's own numbers, so treat them as claims. But notice what they have in common. Neither is a chatbot bolted onto a website. Both sit inside the core work the customer pays for.

**MAYA:** And on the industrial side?

**GRANT:** Parsec Automation published a survey in July of twelve hundred manufacturing leaders. Seventy two percent said they had adopted AI in some form, up from fifty three percent in 2024. But only ten percent had deployed it across their operations. The top use cases were quality control, IT operations and supply chain management. The top barriers were implementation cost, data privacy and security, and integration with existing systems.

**MAYA:** So almost everyone has tried it, and almost nobody has scaled it.

**GRANT:** Which is exactly the gap a private equity owner is built to close. Funds are good at forcing a decision, funding it, and making management accountable for it.

---

### Four levers

**MAYA:** Let's give people a framework. When you walk into a non-software portfolio company, where do you look first?

**GRANT:** I use four levers. The first is revenue capture. Money the business has earned but doesn't collect. Missed calls, unquoted leads, under-billed jobs, denied claims, expired service agreements nobody renewed. AI is very good at catching leakage because it doesn't get tired at four on a Friday.

**MAYA:** Second.

**GRANT:** Labor leverage. Getting more output from the same people, especially scarce experts. A technician who spends forty minutes a day on paperwork. A nurse who spends an hour on notes after every shift. An estimator who builds every quote from scratch. You give that time back, and you've effectively hired without hiring.

**MAYA:** Third.

**GRANT:** Working capital. Faster invoicing, cleaner billing, fewer disputes, better inventory forecasts. This one's underrated, because it shows up in cash rather than EBITDA, and cash is what funds care about at exit.

**MAYA:** And fourth.

**GRANT:** Quality and risk. Fewer defects, fewer safety incidents, fewer compliance misses, better documentation. It's harder to put a number on, but it protects the multiple. A buyer's diligence team will find the defects and the compliance gaps either way.

**MAYA:** Which lever usually pays first?

**GRANT:** Revenue capture, almost every time. It's measurable within weeks, it doesn't require anyone to change their job, and it funds the harder work.

---

### What goes wrong

**MAYA:** Now the failure modes. Because I've seen a lot of real-economy AI programs stall.

**GRANT:** Three big ones. The first is pilot sprawl. Every site manager tries a different tool, nothing connects to the system of record, and after a year you have twelve pilots and no EBITDA. The fix is to pick a small number of workflows and run them across the whole business.

**MAYA:** Second.

**GRANT:** Treating AI as an IT project. Permira's team said every portfolio company should have a chief AI officer or equivalent on the management team, not tucked away in IT. I'd put it more simply. The person who owns the workflow has to own the AI in that workflow. If dispatch is the use case, the head of operations owns it.

**MAYA:** And third.

**GRANT:** Ignoring the people doing the work. In a services business, the technicians and the call center staff decide whether AI works. If they see it as a threat, they'll route around it. If they see it as taking the worst parts of their day away, they'll make it better. Change management isn't a soft add-on here. It's the plan.

### The rest of the season

**MAYA:** So where are we going over the next three episodes?

**GRANT:** Episode two is services: field services, home services and business services, and the AI-enabled roll-up thesis that a lot of funds are chasing. Episode three is healthcare services and other regulated work, where documentation and compliance shape what's possible. And episode four is factories and franchises, plus a hundred-day plan that a fund could actually run.

**MAYA:** And the thread through all of it?

**GRANT:** Start where the money leaks, put AI inside the core workflow, make the workflow owner accountable, and measure it in dollars, not demos.
