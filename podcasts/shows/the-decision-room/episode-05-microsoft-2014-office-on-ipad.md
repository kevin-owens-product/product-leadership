## "Ship Office on the iPad Before Windows Touch"

### THE ROOM: REDMOND, MARCH 2014 (4 minutes)

**DANA:** It's March 2014, Redmond, Washington. Satya Nadella has been CEO of Microsoft for about six weeks — announced February 4th, the third CEO in the company's history, an insider from the cloud and enterprise business. He inherits a company that is enormously profitable and widely described as strategically lost. The stock has been flat for a decade. The phone bet is failing — Windows Phone is scraping three percent share while the company digests the Nokia acquisition Ballmer signed on his way out. And in July 2013, Microsoft took a nine-hundred-million-dollar write-down on unsold Surface RT tablets. That number matters for this room, so hold onto it.

**ELI:** Now the asset at the center of the decision. Microsoft Office — Word, Excel, PowerPoint — is arguably the most successful software franchise ever built. The Office division books more revenue than Windows itself, roughly twenty-four billion dollars a year. And for Office's entire existence, it has served a second job beyond its own P&L: it sells Windows. Office runs best on Windows, so businesses buy Windows, so developers target Windows, so consumers buy Windows. The two moats are interlocked — each one's strength is the other's defense. Inside Microsoft this has a name people say half-jokingly and obey completely: Windows first.

**DANA:** Meanwhile, out in the world, Apple has sold roughly two hundred million iPads. The iPad is four years old and its single biggest gap — the reason it's a "consumption device" in every enterprise conversation — is that it doesn't run real Office. And here's the fact that makes this room tense: a full touch version of Office for iPad *exists inside Microsoft*. It's been in development for years. Steve Ballmer saw it, and held it — the sanctioned plan was that touch Office ships on Windows first, so Surface keeps its one unanswerable differentiator: the tablet that runs Office. The iPad version waits its turn.

**ELI:** One more piece of context. Office 365 — Office as a subscription rather than a boxed license — exists but is young, a small slice of the business. Google Docs is free and eating the low end, especially schools and startups. Apple made its iWork suite free with every new device in September 2013. So every year that Office stays off the world's two hundred million tablets, a growing share of humanity learns — in school, at home, at work — that documents get made without Microsoft in the loop.

**DANA:** The decision on the table, then, for a CEO six weeks into the job: March 27th is your first public product event. Do you walk on stage and ship Office on the iPad — before it ships on Microsoft's own touch platform, over the objections of the Windows organization, validating your rival's device in the middle of your own struggling hardware push? Or do you honor the sequence, ship Windows touch first, and protect the coupling that built the company? <breath> It's March 2014. Nothing after it exists. I'm against. Eli's for.

[PAUSE]

### THE CASE AGAINST: THE MOATS DEFEND EACH OTHER (5 minutes)

**DANA:** Let me start by naming what's actually being proposed, without the press-release perfume: we would take our crown jewel and hand it to the competitor that is currently beating our hardware in the market, at the precise moment our own tablet strategy is fighting for its life. Nine hundred million dollars of unsold Surface RT inventory was written off *eight months ago*. The one sentence every Surface salesperson has — the only differentiator that survives contact with an Apple Store — is "it runs real Office." Ship Office on the iPad and that sentence dies on March 27th. We wouldn't just be failing to defend Surface; we'd be personally deleting its reason to exist, on stage, at our own event.

**DANA:** Second — the coupling is not sentiment, it's the business model. Office sells Windows and Windows sells Office; that loop is why enterprises buy site licenses for both, why four decades of corporate IT is built on our stack. Uncouple it and you're betting that Office alone, as a naked app in someone else's store — paying Apple's thirty percent toll, living under Apple's review process, discoverable at Apple's pleasure — retains its pricing power. Maybe! But you're trading a *system* you control end-to-end for a *tenant relationship* on a rival's land. Companies that dismantle their own lock-in to chase users usually discover the users were never the thing being monetized — the lock-in was.

**ELI:** And the two hundred million iPads that run no Office at all today? What are they doing to the lock-in?

**DANA:** They're waiting, Eli — and that's my third argument: this is a sequencing question, not a whether question, and sequencing is free. The touch-first Windows version is in the pipeline. Ship *that* first, even a quarter earlier than planned, then follow on iPad. Same endgame, no message that Windows comes second, no gift to Cupertino during Surface's most fragile year. The room isn't debating "should Office go multi-platform someday" — everyone sane says yes. The room is debating whether the *new CEO's first public act* should be a ritual humiliation of the Windows division. Because that's how Redmond will read it, that's how the OEM partners who sell ninety-something percent of Windows machines will read it, and partners price disloyalty into every future decision they make about you.

**DANA:** And the boring argument, as always, is the strongest. What does shipping first on iPad actually *earn*, in dollars? The app will be free to view; editing requires an Office 365 subscription — a young product at a hundred dollars a year against Google's free and Apple's free. We'd be trading the strongest software franchise on earth — twenty-four billion in high-margin license revenue — for a *hope* that tablet users subscribe. If the subscription thesis is right, it's just as right in September after Windows touch ships. If it's wrong, we'll have spent our crown jewel to find out. <sigh> Six weeks into the job is when you learn the machine, not when you bet it.

### THE CASE FOR: OFFICE IS THE MOAT — WINDOWS IS THE TAX (5 minutes)

**ELI:** Everything Dana said was true in 2004. That's the problem — the whole case rests on a loop that has already been broken, just not by us. <breath> Walk through it honestly. The coupling says: Office sells Windows, Windows sells Office. But mobile happened, and Windows *lost*. Not "is losing" — lost. The devices people actually carry are iPhones, iPads, and Androids; Windows Phone is at three percent and the write-down on our own tablet is nearly a billion dollars. So the real question in this room is not "should we protect the coupling?" The coupling is *gone* on every growth platform that exists. The question is whether Office dies with Windows mobile, out of loyalty, or lives where the users are.

**ELI:** Because here's what the Windows-first sequence actually purchased these past four years, while the iPad version sat finished on a shelf: two hundred million tablets' worth of people learning to live without us. Every school that standardizes on Google Docs, every startup that never buys a license, every executive who discovers the iPad plus Keynote gets them through the week — that's not a delayed sale, it's a *broken habit*. Office's true moat was never the Windows loop, Dana. It's that every working human's muscle memory and every company's file archive speaks Office. Habits and formats. And habits decay with every year of absence, compounding, in the one direction we can't reverse. Waiting isn't neutral. Waiting is the aggressive option — against ourselves.

**DANA:** And Surface? You're on stage in three weeks killing its only sales pitch.

**ELI:** If Surface's entire value proposition is "we withhold our own software from better-selling devices," then Surface isn't a product, it's a hostage-taking — and the hostage is a twenty-four-billion-dollar franchise being held to protect a business that just wrote off nine hundred million. That's the pattern I want this room to see, because it's everywhere once you name it: the *strategy tax*. Every hour of Office roadmap bent toward propping up Windows mobile is the strongest product in the company paying tax to the weakest. Real companies die of this — not of competition, but of their best businesses being slowly bled to subsidize their most sentimental ones.

**ELI:** Now the subscription point, because Dana's "trading licenses for hope" line deserves a real answer. Office 365 is the entire future of this franchise — recurring revenue instead of a boxed purchase every five years — and a subscription is only worth paying for if it follows you across every device you own. That's the *definition* of its value. An Office subscription that works on your work PC but not the iPad in your bag is a worse product than the box it replaced. Shipping on iPad isn't a distraction from the 365 transition — it's the *proof of concept*. Free to read, subscribe to edit: the iPad becomes the largest free-trial funnel Office has ever had, on hardware we didn't have to build.

**ELI:** And last — the part that's about the six weeks, not the software. A hundred and twenty thousand employees are watching to find out if the new CEO actually means any of it. Culture doesn't change by memo; it changes when the organization watches leadership pay a real price for a stated principle. "Mobile first, cloud first" costs nothing to say. Shipping Office on iPad, first, at his first event, over the Windows division's objections — that's the version with a receipt attached. He gets exactly one first move. <laugh> Spend it on the sacred cow, or spend the next five years being told no by the same people who told Ballmer no.

### THE REVEAL: WHAT MICROSOFT DID (4 minutes)

[MUSIC STING]

**DANA:** Here's what actually happened. On March 27th, 2014, in San Francisco, at his first press event as CEO, Satya Nadella announced Word, Excel, and PowerPoint for iPad — full touch-first versions, free to read and present, with editing unlocked by an Office 365 subscription. He framed the whole event around a phrase that became the company's banner: mobile first, cloud first. The Windows touch version — the one that was supposed to ship before it — came later. On Apple's tablet, Microsoft shipped first.

**ELI:** The market's answer was immediate: twelve million downloads in the first week. Office titles went straight to the top of the App Store charts. And the thing Dana's case predicted would be a hope turned into the engine — Office 365 subscriptions became the growth story of the whole franchise, consumer subscriptions climbing quarter after quarter, and the commercial cloud — 365 plus Azure — became the center of gravity of the entire company. Over the following decade, Microsoft's stock rose roughly tenfold, from a company the market had written off as a legacy toll booth to, at various points, the most valuable company on earth.

**DANA:** And my hostage? Surface didn't die of the betrayal. It repositioned as a premium niche — the reference hardware for Windows — and settled into a modest, respectable business. The OEM revolt never came. It turns out the differentiator I was defending mattered less to Surface's fate than Surface's own product quality did. The coupling's protective power was, as Eli said, mostly already spent.

**ELI:** Now the honest counterweight, because this episode gets remembered as "Nadella walked in and turned the ship in six weeks," and that's not quite what happened. The iPad product was *built* under Ballmer — he green-lit the development, and he later said versions of "it would have shipped eventually." The strategic logic was understood by the previous regime. What the previous regime couldn't do was pay the *symbolic* price: shipping it first, publicly, framed as a new company priority, with the Windows division watching. The technology was inherited. The decision — the willingness to reprice the sacred cow on stage — was new. That's worth being precise about, because it means the lesson here isn't vision. Everyone had the vision. It's permission.

**DANA:** Which is a genuinely strange kind of decision, when you think about it. The product existed. The analysis existed. The *only* thing that changed between "held on a shelf" and "twelve million downloads" was one person deciding the org chart's feelings were no longer a blocking dependency. <breath> Entire strategies are sometimes just waiting for someone willing to absorb the anger.

### WHAT TRANSFERS (5 minutes)

**ELI:** Alright, what you carry out of this room. Four things.

**ELI:** One: find your load-bearing moat — and be willing to discover it's not the one the org chart worships. Microsoft's identity said Windows was the castle and Office the treasure inside it. The truth had quietly inverted: Office's habits and file formats were the moat; Windows on mobile was a drawbridge to nowhere. Ask of your own portfolio: if we could only defend one asset, which one actually keeps customers here? Then check whether your roadmap is defending that one — or taxing it.

**DANA:** Two: name your strategy tax. Anywhere a strong product's roadmap is bent to protect a weak one — features withheld, launches sequenced for internal politics, integrations forbidden — that's a tax, and it compounds invisibly because the cost is measured in things that never happened. The two hundred million iPads without Office never showed up on any Microsoft P&L. Nobody books "habits we didn't form" as a loss. Audit for it deliberately, because the accounting never will.

**ELI:** Three: new leaders get one free heresy — spend it on the most symbolic sacred cow, not the biggest line item. Nadella's first move wasn't the largest decision available to him; Nokia was bigger money. It was the most *legible* one: a single image — Office, on an iPad, on stage — that told every employee and partner the old rule was dead. Organizations don't parse strategy documents; they parse what leadership is visibly willing to pay for. If you've just taken a role with a mandate for change, your first irreversible act is the only memo anyone will remember.

**DANA:** Four: distribution follows users, not org charts — and it can't wait for your internal sequencing to be comfortable. The Windows-first sequence felt free because it was measured in quarters; the habit decay it purchased was measured in a generation. When your users have visibly moved and your product hasn't followed because of *whose feelings it would hurt internally*, every quarter of delay is a gift to whoever's teaching your users to live without you.
