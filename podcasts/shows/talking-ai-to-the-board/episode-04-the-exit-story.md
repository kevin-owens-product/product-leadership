# Episode 4: The Exit Story
## "Presenting AI to Buyers So It Adds Value Instead of Inviting a Discount"

**Duration:** ~49 minutes
**Hosts:** Claire & Marcus
**Podcast:** Talking AI to the Board and LPs

### Cold open

[INTRO MUSIC]

All of that builds toward this moment, because at exit, every claim you've made gets tested by someone whose job is to find reasons to pay less.

### Why the exit audience is different

**MARCUS:** Let's start with what makes buyers different from boards and LPs.

**CLAIRE:** Three things. First, incentives. Your board and your LPs want the AI story to be true, because they're on your side. A buyer wants to pay a fair price, which in practice means they're looking for every weakness. Second, access. A buyer's diligence team gets deep access to data, contracts and people in a compressed period, and they bring specialists. Third, consequences. What you say in a sale process ends up in a purchase agreement, with representations, warranties and, potentially, claims after closing.

**MARCUS:** And the specialists have gotten better at this.

**CLAIRE:** Much better. Two or three years ago, a buyer's technology diligence might have spent an afternoon on AI. Now there are dedicated AI diligence workstreams, at the big advisory firms and at specialist boutiques. They know what to ask, and they know where companies tend to overstate. If you want to hear the buyer's side in depth, our sister show, AI Diligence Room, spends a whole season on it.

**MARCUS:** So the starting assumption has flipped.

**CLAIRE:** Completely. A few years ago, calling a company AI-powered could add to the multiple. Today, sophisticated buyers often treat an AI claim as something to verify, and an unverified claim as a risk. The question isn't "does this company have AI?" It's "does AI make this business more valuable or more fragile, and can you prove it?"

---

### Three AI stories a seller can tell

**MARCUS:** Let's get to the equity story. What kinds of AI story can a seller tell?

**CLAIRE:** I think there are three, and it helps to be clear about which one you're telling, because each needs different evidence.

**MARCUS:** Go through them.

**CLAIRE:** The first is AI as efficiency. "We've used AI to lower our cost to serve, and here's the effect on margins." That's the most common, and in many ways the easiest to prove, because it shows up in the financials. The second is AI as product. "We've built AI into what we sell, and it's driving growth, pricing or retention." That's more valuable if true, but harder to prove, because you have to separate the AI effect from everything else. The third is AI as defense. "AI could disrupt businesses like ours, and here's why we're resilient, or why we're the ones doing the disrupting." That's the story nobody wants to tell but every buyer wants to hear.

**MARCUS:** Can you tell all three?

**CLAIRE:** You can, and the strongest equity stories often do. But be careful not to oversell any one of them. A buyer who catches you exaggerating the efficiency story will discount your product story too.

**MARCUS:** Take efficiency first. What makes it credible?

**CLAIRE:** It needs to be in the actual numbers, with a baseline and a method. "Our support cost per ticket fell by a third over eighteen months, here's the monthly trend, here's when the AI assistant was introduced, and here's how we separated it from other changes like the new ticketing system." It needs to include the costs: software, model usage, the team that maintains it. And it needs to show that quality held up, with customer satisfaction or error rates over the same period.

**MARCUS:** And the sustainability question?

**CLAIRE:** Critical. A buyer will ask whether the savings are durable. Will they hold if model prices rise? If the vendor changes its terms? If the key engineer who built it leaves? If regulation changes how you can use it? You want to answer those before they're asked.

**MARCUS:** AI as product.

**CLAIRE:** Here the evidence is commercial. Adoption of the AI features by customers. Whether customers who use them retain better or expand more. Whether you can charge for them, either as an add-on or through higher prices. And ideally, wins against competitors where the AI capability was cited as a reason. The trap is correlation: your best customers might use the AI features because they're your most engaged customers, not the other way round. Buyers know that trap, so address it, perhaps by comparing similar customers before and after adoption.

**MARCUS:** And the unit economics.

**CLAIRE:** Yes. If your AI features run on a third-party model, every use costs you money. The buyer will want to see gross margin on the AI features specifically, and what happens to that margin as usage grows. Some companies discover that their most popular AI feature is their least profitable one.

**MARCUS:** And AI as defense.

**CLAIRE:** This is where honesty earns the most. The buyer has already done the disruption test themselves, or they will. If you present your own analysis first, with the same rigor, you control the framing. "Here are the parts of our business that AI could commoditize. Here's what we've done about each one. Here's why our position in these other parts is durable: proprietary data, deep integration into customer workflows, regulatory approvals, physical operations, relationships." That's much stronger than letting the buyer discover the exposure and wonder why you didn't mention it.

### AI savings and the quality of earnings

**MARCUS:** Let's go into the numbers. In a sale, the earnings that matter are usually adjusted EBITDA. How do AI savings fit into that?

**CLAIRE:** Carefully. This is one of the places where sellers most often overreach. The temptation is to add back projected AI savings as a pro forma adjustment. "We're rolling out an AI assistant that will save this much in labor next year, so let's adjust EBITDA as if it already had."

**MARCUS:** And buyers push back.

**CLAIRE:** Hard. The buyer will typically commission a quality of earnings review, and those reviewers are skeptical of forward-looking adjustments in general. A saving that has already been achieved and is visible in the run rate is one thing. A saving that's planned but not achieved is a projection, and most buyers will treat it as part of the value creation plan they're paying for, not as earnings they're buying.

**MARCUS:** So what's acceptable?

**CLAIRE:** There's a spectrum. At the strong end, savings that are fully in the run rate, with a few months of data showing they're stable. Slightly weaker, savings from actions that are complete but haven't flowed through a full period yet, like roles that were reduced last month. Those may be accepted as run-rate adjustments if the evidence is clear. Weaker still, savings from a rollout that's under way but not finished. And at the weak end, savings from a plan. The further down that spectrum you go, the more likely the buyer is to exclude it, and the more your credibility suffers if you included it anyway.

**MARCUS:** Is there a way to get credit for the planned savings?

**CLAIRE:** Through the narrative and the forecast rather than through adjusted earnings. Show the plan, show the progress on early phases as evidence that the plan works, and let it support the growth and margin forecast. Some buyers will pay for a credible plan, especially if you can show the early phases delivered. You might also structure part of the price as an earnout tied to results, though earnouts bring their own complexity and disputes.

**MARCUS:** What about the costs side? Do buyers adjust for those?

**CLAIRE:** Yes, and this catches people out. If AI costs have been capitalized, or sit in a different part of the business, or were paid by the private equity owner rather than the company, the buyer will want to see the full, ongoing cost. And if the company is on a promotional or early-stage price from a model provider, they'll ask what the price will be at renewal. An efficiency story that ignores rising costs is not going to survive a quality of earnings review.

**MARCUS:** Any pattern you'd warn sellers about?

**CLAIRE:** Relabeling. A company had a cost reduction program for other reasons, outsourcing or a reorganization, and now describes it as AI-driven because that sounds better. A good diligence team will ask to see the AI system, the usage data and the timeline, and if the savings came before the tool was deployed, the story collapses. And that's not just embarrassing. If those claims end up in the documents, they can become a legal problem.

### Vendor due diligence

**MARCUS:** Many sellers now commission their own diligence before the sale. Does that include AI?

**CLAIRE:** Increasingly, yes. Vendor due diligence, where the seller commissions reports that buyers can rely on, has long covered financials, tax and commercial matters. Technology vendor diligence has become more common, and AI is now a regular part of it.

**MARCUS:** What's the benefit?

**CLAIRE:** Three things. You find your problems before the buyer does, which gives you time to fix them or at least explain them. You set the frame, because the buyer starts from your report rather than a blank page. And you speed up the process, because buyers can focus their own work on confirming rather than discovering.

**MARCUS:** What should an AI section of a vendor diligence report cover?

**CLAIRE:** An inventory of AI systems in use, both in operations and in the product. For each important one, what it does, which models and vendors it depends on, what data it uses, how it's monitored, and what the business impact is, with evidence. The contracts with AI vendors, including terms on data use, pricing, and what happens if the vendor changes its model. Intellectual property: who owns the training data, any fine-tuned models, the outputs. Regulatory exposure, such as whether anything is high-risk under the EU AI Act or touches state rules on automated decisions. Security, including the newer AI-specific risks like prompt injection. Governance: policy, ownership, incidents. And the disruption analysis.

**MARCUS:** Does the buyer trust it?

**CLAIRE:** Partly. Sophisticated buyers know the seller paid for it, so they'll verify the key points. But a well-done report, from a reputable firm, that discloses problems honestly, is usually trusted more than one that reads like a marketing document. Which brings us back to the theme of the whole season: credibility comes from showing what didn't work as well as what did.

**MARCUS:** When should it start?

**CLAIRE:** Earlier than people think. If the vendor diligence finds that your AI inventory is incomplete, your vendor contracts don't cover data use, or your savings aren't measured properly, you need months to fix those things. Six to twelve months before launching a process is not too early to do an AI readiness review.

---

### Getting the data room ready

**MARCUS:** Let's talk about the data room. What AI documents should be in it?

**CLAIRE:** If you've followed the practices from this season, most of it already exists, and that's the point. You want the AI policy and governance documents, including who owns AI and how decisions are made. The AI inventory, with a description of each material system. Board materials about AI, which show the buyer that the board has been overseeing it properly. The AI value reporting, with evidence grades and methods. Vendor contracts and their key terms. Data rights documentation: where training data came from, consents, licenses. Evaluation results showing how well key systems perform and how that's monitored. Incident logs and how incidents were handled. Any regulatory correspondence. And the disruption analysis.

**MARCUS:** That's a lot of documents.

**CLAIRE:** It is, but think about the alternative. If the buyer asks for these things and they don't exist, the buyer concludes that AI isn't really governed, and they either discount the price or ask for stronger protections in the purchase agreement. If they exist and they're coherent, the buyer's confidence goes up. Missing documentation is one of the most common reasons AI turns from an asset into a discount.

**MARCUS:** Anything that should be handled carefully?

**CLAIRE:** A few. Customer data should not go into the data room in raw form; summaries and samples with appropriate protections. Source code and model details for a product company may need to go into a clean room with restricted access, especially if the buyer is a competitor. And anything about pending legal or regulatory matters should be reviewed by counsel before it's shared.

**MARCUS:** And the problems.

**CLAIRE:** Disclose them. If an AI system had an incident, if a vendor dispute is ongoing, if a customer complained about an AI decision, put it in with an explanation of what happened and what you did. In a purchase agreement, the seller will typically make representations about the business, and items disclosed against those representations are usually treated very differently from items the buyer discovers after closing.

---

### Representations, warranties and insurance

**MARCUS:** You mentioned the purchase agreement. Are AI-specific representations becoming common?

**CLAIRE:** They're showing up more often, particularly in deals where AI is central to the business. Buyers ask the seller to state that its use of AI complies with applicable laws, that it has the rights to the data it used to train or fine-tune models, that it maintains certain governance practices, that its descriptions of its AI capabilities are accurate, and that it hasn't had undisclosed material AI incidents.

**MARCUS:** And sellers push back.

**CLAIRE:** Naturally. Sellers try to qualify them with knowledge and materiality, and to limit them to specific laws rather than "all applicable laws," given how fast AI regulation is changing. That negotiation is normal. The important thing for the seller is that the facts are known before the negotiation starts, so the company can make representations it's confident about and disclose exceptions properly.

**MARCUS:** And warranty and indemnity insurance? Many private equity deals now use it.

**CLAIRE:** Yes, representation and warranty insurance is common in private equity exits, so the seller can walk away with fewer claims hanging over it. Insurers underwrite based on the diligence the buyer did. If AI diligence was thin, insurers may exclude AI-related matters from cover, which pushes the risk back to the negotiation between buyer and seller. So a seller who wants a clean exit has a strong interest in the buyer being able to do good AI diligence. Good preparation helps the seller here, not just the buyer.

**MARCUS:** What about the intellectual property questions? Those feel especially live.

**CLAIRE:** They are. There's been a wave of copyright litigation against AI developers over training data, including a large settlement between Anthropic and book authors, and there are ongoing cases against others. Most portfolio companies aren't training foundation models, but if a company has fine-tuned models on data it scraped, or on customer data without clear rights, a buyer will want to know. And there are questions about who owns AI-generated outputs, like code written with AI assistance. Buyers will ask what the company's policy is and whether any open-source license or third-party rights issues have arisen.

### The management presentation

**MARCUS:** Let's go into the room. The management presentation. How should AI be presented?

**CLAIRE:** First, by whom. The CEO should own the AI strategy in a sentence or two, because buyers want to see that leadership understands it. But the details should come from the person who actually runs it, whether that's the chief technology officer, the chief operating officer, or a head of AI. A buyer can tell very quickly whether someone has done the work or is reciting slides.

**MARCUS:** And what should they show?

**CLAIRE:** Less than they think. One or two AI stories, told properly, with evidence, are much better than a list of fifteen initiatives. For each story: the problem, what was built, what it costs, what it delivered against a baseline, what went wrong and how it was fixed, and what's next. If possible, a live demonstration of something real, not a prototype.

**MARCUS:** Live demos are risky.

**CLAIRE:** They are, which is why buyers like them. A recorded demo or a polished prototype tells the buyer what the company wants them to see. A live demo of a production system, with real data, suitably anonymized, tells them it works. If you're not confident demoing it live, that's useful information about how ready it really is.

**MARCUS:** And the questions?

**CLAIRE:** Rehearse them as hard as you'd rehearse for an LP meeting, harder actually. The questions you should be ready for include: How do you know the savings are caused by AI? What happens to margins if model prices double? What if your main model vendor changes terms or is disrupted? How much of this could a competitor replicate with off-the-shelf tools, and how fast? Who are the key people, and what happens if they leave? What would you do with more investment? And what are you most worried about?

**MARCUS:** That last one is a good question.

**CLAIRE:** It's a great question, and the answer reveals a lot. A management team that says "nothing really" loses credibility. One that names a real risk, explains it clearly, and describes what they're doing about it gains credibility.

**MARCUS:** Is there anything the private equity owner should do differently in the presentation?

**CLAIRE:** Stay in the background on AI. Buyers are buying the management team's ability to keep delivering. If the AI story sounds like it belongs to the sponsor's operating team rather than the company, buyers will worry about what happens when the sponsor leaves. The best evidence that AI capability will survive the sale is that the company's own people clearly own it.

### Handling the disruption question

**MARCUS:** Let's come back to the question from the cold open. "If a competitor can build this with off-the-shelf tools in six months, why should we pay for it?" How do you answer?

**CLAIRE:** First, don't be defensive. It's a reasonable question, and the buyer is right to ask it. Then answer on the specifics. Often the honest answer is that the AI itself isn't the moat, and you shouldn't claim it is. The moat is what surrounds it.

**MARCUS:** Such as?

**CLAIRE:** Proprietary data that a competitor can't easily get, like years of operational data or labelled outcomes. Integration into customer workflows that would be costly for customers to replace. Distribution and relationships. Regulatory approvals or certifications. Physical operations. Speed of execution, shown by a track record of shipping. And the learning loop: the fact that every customer interaction makes the system better. If you can show that, with evidence, the answer becomes "yes, someone could build a similar tool, but they couldn't replicate what it runs on and where it sits."

**MARCUS:** And if the honest answer is that a competitor really could replicate it?

**CLAIRE:** Then don't price it as a moat. Present it as a capability that keeps you competitive and protects margins, which is still valuable. Buyers will discount a defensibility claim they don't believe, but they'll credit a well-run operation that uses AI sensibly.

**MARCUS:** Does the type of buyer change the answer?

**CLAIRE:** Quite a lot. A strategic buyer, a larger company in the same industry, may already have AI capabilities and may care more about your customers, data and market position than your AI tools. For them, the question is how easily your AI will integrate, or whether they'll replace it. A financial buyer, another private equity firm, will care more about whether the AI value is durable and whether there's more to come, because that's the next value creation plan. And a buyer that is itself an AI-native company will look very hard at whether your business is something they could replicate rather than buy.

**MARCUS:** And for a company going public instead?

**CLAIRE:** Then the audience is public investors, and the rules on disclosure get stricter. The SEC expects AI claims in a registration statement to be accurate and specific, and has said it's watching for AI-washing in public company disclosures too. Risk factors about AI need to be tailored to the company, not boilerplate. And whatever you say about AI in the offering documents becomes the baseline you'll be measured against in every quarterly report afterward.

### Timing and the eighteen-month plan

**MARCUS:** Let's talk about timing. When should a private equity owner start preparing the AI exit story?

**CLAIRE:** I'd say eighteen to twenty-four months before a planned exit. That sounds early, but think about what you need. Savings need to be in the run rate for several quarters to be credible. AI product features need adoption data and enough time to show an effect on retention or pricing. Governance needs to be in place and to have operated for a while, so that board minutes and incident logs show a track record. And any gaps from an AI readiness review need time to be fixed.

**MARCUS:** What does that plan look like?

**CLAIRE:** Roughly in three phases. About two years out, do an honest AI readiness review: inventory, evidence quality, contracts, data rights, governance, disruption exposure. Pick the two or three AI initiatives that matter most for the exit story and make sure they're measured properly. Around twelve months out, the measurement should be producing reliable data, governance gaps should be closed, and vendor contracts should be renegotiated if needed. Six months out, commission vendor diligence, build the data room, and start drafting the equity story. And in the last few months, rehearse the management presentation, including the hard questions.

**MARCUS:** What if the exit comes sooner than expected? An unsolicited offer, say.

**CLAIRE:** Then you'll find out how well you've been reporting all along. If you've been following the practices from earlier in the season, with evidence-graded value reporting to the board and a current AI inventory, much of the work is already done. If not, the honest approach is to present what's proven and describe the rest as opportunity, rather than rushing to dress up an AI story that won't hold up.

**MARCUS:** It's interesting how the season ties together there.

**CLAIRE:** It's the whole argument of the season. The way you report to the board in year one determines what you can prove to a buyer in year five. Every shortcut you take in the board pack shows up as a discount in the sale.

### A worked example

**MARCUS:** Let's make this concrete with a made-up company. Call it Harbor Field Services. A commercial maintenance business, about three hundred million in revenue, owned by a private equity fund for four years, heading to a sale next year.

**CLAIRE:** Good. And let's say Harbor has done three things with AI. It uses an AI scheduling and routing system for its technicians. It uses an AI assistant in its call center to handle booking and status questions. And it's built an AI tool that reads technician notes and photos to draft quotes for follow-up repair work.

**MARCUS:** What's the first draft of the equity story look like?

**CLAIRE:** The first draft, the one the banker writes, probably says something like: "Harbor is an AI-enabled leader in commercial maintenance, with proprietary AI driving superior technician productivity, customer experience and revenue capture." And it adds a margin bridge with a few points of improvement labelled "AI initiatives."

**MARCUS:** And what does the buyer's diligence team do to that?

**CLAIRE:** They take it apart one claim at a time. On scheduling, they ask whether the system is proprietary or a vendor product. Let's say it's a vendor product, configured well. So "proprietary AI" is wrong, and that word alone costs credibility. Then they ask for technician utilization before and after. Let's say it improved meaningfully, and the data is clean. That's a real result.

**MARCUS:** Call center?

**CLAIRE:** They ask for contact volumes, the share handled without a person, customer satisfaction, and staffing. Let's say the AI handles a good share of status calls, but booking calls still mostly go to people, and staffing fell a little but partly because of turnover the company chose not to backfill. That's a modest, real result, but smaller than the banker's draft implied.

**MARCUS:** And the quoting tool, which is the most interesting one.

**CLAIRE:** It is, because it's the one that's genuinely built in-house, using the company's own technician notes and photos, years of them, along with the outcomes of past quotes. That's proprietary data in a meaningful sense. The buyer asks how many quotes it drafts, how many technicians accept them, and whether follow-up revenue went up. Let's say follow-up revenue per job rose in the regions where it's deployed, compared with regions where it isn't yet. That's a strong result, with a reasonable comparison.

**MARCUS:** So how should the equity story have been written?

**CLAIRE:** Something like this: "Harbor has used AI to improve three parts of the business. A vendor routing system, configured by Harbor's operations team, has increased technician utilization by a measured amount since rollout. An AI assistant now handles most status calls, with stable customer satisfaction and a modest staffing benefit. And Harbor's own quoting tool, trained on years of proprietary job data, has increased follow-up revenue per job in the regions where it's deployed, with a rollout plan for the remaining regions." Each claim is specific, each can be proven, and the most valuable one is clearly identified.

**MARCUS:** And the margin bridge?

**CLAIRE:** Utilization gains and call center savings that are already in the run rate go into the historical numbers, where they belong. The quoting tool's effect in deployed regions is also historical. The rollout to remaining regions is forward-looking, so it goes into the forecast, supported by the results in deployed regions, not into adjusted EBITDA. And the full costs, including the vendor subscription, model usage and the small internal team, are shown clearly.

**MARCUS:** Does Harbor get paid for the quoting tool?

**CLAIRE:** It has a good chance, because the evidence is strong and the data behind it is genuinely hard to replicate. A financial buyer might see the rollout as a clear part of their own value creation plan and pay for some of it. A strategic buyer with a large field service business might see it as something to deploy across their own operations, which could make it worth even more to them. Either way, the claim survives diligence, which is what lets it influence price.

**MARCUS:** And the disruption question for Harbor?

**CLAIRE:** It's mostly favorable, and Harbor should say why. The core service is physical: technicians on site, fixing equipment. AI makes the business more efficient but doesn't replace it. The main risk is that AI makes it easier for competitors to match Harbor's efficiency, so the advantage from the vendor routing system, in particular, may shrink over time as everyone adopts similar tools. Saying that openly, and pointing to the quoting tool and its data as the more durable advantage, is exactly the kind of honesty that makes the rest of the story credible.

### Key people and keeping the capability

**MARCUS:** You mentioned key people earlier. How much does that matter?

**CLAIRE:** A great deal, especially in mid-sized companies where AI capability often depends on a handful of people. If the quoting tool in our example was built and is maintained by two engineers, the buyer will want to know who they are, whether they're staying, and whether anyone else understands how it works.

**MARCUS:** What can the seller do about that?

**CLAIRE:** Several things, ideally well before the sale. Document the systems properly, so knowledge isn't held only in people's heads. Build a team rather than relying on one person, even if it's small. Make sure the key people have retention arrangements, such as equity or bonuses that vest after the sale. And involve those people in diligence, so the buyer can see their expertise directly.

**MARCUS:** Do buyers ask about this directly?

**CLAIRE:** They do, and they may ask to interview key technical staff. They'll often look at whether those people are likely to stay through a transition, and they may want retention packages put in place as part of the deal. If the AI value depends on someone who's about to leave, that value will be discounted heavily.

### Carve-outs and shared AI

**MARCUS:** One more situation. What if the company being sold is a division of a larger group, or depends on AI tools the private equity firm provides across its portfolio?

**CLAIRE:** That's a real complication, and it's becoming more common because, as we discussed in episode two, many firms now share AI tools, contracts and platforms across their portfolio. If a portfolio company's AI runs on a shared contract that the fund negotiated, or on a platform the fund's operating team built, then at sale the buyer will ask what happens when that access ends.

**MARCUS:** And the answer?

**CLAIRE:** You need a plan. Either the company gets its own contracts before the sale, or there's a transition services agreement that provides access for a period after closing, at an agreed cost, while the buyer sets up its own. And the economics need to be clear. If the company has been benefiting from a portfolio-wide discount on model usage, its costs will go up once it's on its own, and the buyer will adjust for that.

**MARCUS:** So the portfolio approach has a cost at exit.

**CLAIRE:** It can, but it's manageable if you plan for it. The best practice is to set up shared arrangements so that each company's usage is tracked separately, each company has clear rights to its own data and any models trained on it, and contracts allow assignment or separate continuation at sale. That way, the shared benefit during ownership doesn't become a tangle at exit.

**MARCUS:** And for a carve-out from a larger corporate parent?

**CLAIRE:** Similar issues, often worse. The division may rely on the parent's AI tools, data platforms, and even the parent's data. The buyer will need to understand what transfers, what's licensed for a period, and what has to be rebuilt. Those costs belong in the standalone cost analysis, and AI dependencies are now one of the items that analysis has to cover explicitly.

---

### Telling LPs about the exit

**MARCUS:** Finally, there's one more audience at exit, and it closes the loop with episode three. The LPs.

**CLAIRE:** Right. After the sale, the GP will describe the result to LPs, in the quarterly letter, at the annual meeting, and very likely in the next fundraising deck as a case study. And all the rules from episode three apply.

**MARCUS:** Which means?

**CLAIRE:** If you say AI drove part of the value created, you need to be able to substantiate that, and you need to present it fairly. That's actually much easier after a sale, because you now have an independent data point: what a buyer was willing to pay, and in many cases, what the buyer's diligence concluded about the AI value. If the quality of earnings review accepted the AI savings as part of run-rate earnings, that's strong evidence. If the buyer gave little credit for the AI story, you should be cautious about claiming it drove the price.

**MARCUS:** And the case study?

**CLAIRE:** Same principles as before. Baseline, method, costs, what didn't work, and an honest share of the value attributed to AI versus everything else that happened during the holding period. LPs will compare your case study with what they hear from other sources, including sometimes the buyer. Consistency, again, is everything.

### Common mistakes at exit

**MARCUS:** Let's do our common mistakes list.

**CLAIRE:** First, calling the company "AI-powered" in the teaser without the evidence to back it up. It invites scrutiny you can't withstand. Second, adding back projected AI savings to adjusted EBITDA. Third, ignoring AI costs, especially model costs that will rise with usage or at renewal. Fourth, relabeling cost reductions that came from something else as AI-driven. Fifth, having no AI inventory, or an incomplete one, so the buyer finds systems you didn't mention.

**MARCUS:** Keep going.

**CLAIRE:** Sixth, letting the sponsor's operating team present the AI story instead of the company's own leaders. Seventh, being defensive or vague about disruption risk. Eighth, overclaiming a moat when the AI is replicable, rather than presenting it as a strong operational capability. Ninth, forgetting the legal side: data rights, vendor terms and IP questions that can turn into representations and indemnities. And tenth, starting too late, so that the evidence isn't there when it's needed.

**MARCUS:** And the single most important habit?

**CLAIRE:** Assume every AI claim will be tested by a skeptic with access to your data and a reason to pay less. Only make the claims that pass that test. And, as with every audience this season, show what didn't work as well as what did. It's the most reliable way to make what did work believable.

### Questions we keep hearing

**MARCUS:** Let's take a few quick questions we hear about exits.

**CLAIRE:** Go ahead.

**MARCUS:** "Should we delay a sale until our AI initiatives are proven?"

**CLAIRE:** Rarely, on AI grounds alone. Exit timing is driven by markets, the fund's life and the business's overall performance. But if a major initiative is a few months from producing clear evidence, and the timing is otherwise flexible, waiting for that evidence can be worth it. A proven result is worth much more than a promising one.

**MARCUS:** "Will buyers pay for AI at all?"

**CLAIRE:** They'll pay for proven earnings, credible growth and durable advantage. AI that produces those gets paid for, through the earnings, the forecast or the multiple. AI that doesn't, doesn't. The label itself is worth very little now, and sometimes less than nothing.

**MARCUS:** "Should we hire an AI advisor for the sale process?"

**CLAIRE:** If AI is central to the equity story, it's worth having someone independent review it before you go to market, whether through formal vendor diligence or a lighter readiness review. They'll find the weaknesses a buyer will find, while you still have time to address them.
