### THE BOARD PACK

**ASHA:** Six numbers on the board pack. First, Harborline ARR: one hundred ten point three million. Second, NRR: one hundred four percent. Third, AI ARR: four point six million. Fourth, quarterly EBITDA margin: twenty-eight percent. Fifth, net debt: one hundred fifty-two point nine million. Sixth, Beacon's reported growth: sixteen percent.

**COLE:** Which number do you distrust first?

**ASHA:** Beacon's growth, because it is the only number in that pack we did not produce under Harborline's metric contracts. "Sixteen percent growth" can mean ending ARR divided by opening ARR. It can mean bookings. It can mean revenue with implementation services. It can mean a constant-currency adjustment. It can mean the seller selected the twelve months that look best.

**COLE:** And even if it is exactly true, it can hide the mechanism. A company can grow sixteen percent while losing its best customers if new-logo sales are temporarily strong. It can grow sixteen percent with discounts that make the contracts unattractive. It can grow while implementation backlog accumulates. A single growth percentage is an answer with all the causality removed.

**ASHA:** Harborline's one hundred four percent NRR also needs interrogation, even though we trust the definition. It is positive. It is also only three points above where we started and eight points above the Q3 low. One weak renewal cohort could pull it back under one hundred. If integration distracts customer success, the acquisition can destroy more value in Harborline than it adds through Beacon.

**COLE:** The AI ARR needs the same treatment. Four point six million is real contracted ARR, not a pilot count. But it still depends on implementation, usage, quality, and data rights. Beacon appears strategically attractive because its inspection workflow could give Assist another place to operate. That is not permission to capitalize a slide titled "AI synergies."

**ASHA:** And the debt number sets the perimeter. Harborline entered with one hundred fifty-six million of net debt. It has only paid that down to one hundred fifty-two point nine. The transformation improved growth and operations before it produced large cash deleveraging. Forty-two million dollars for Beacon, funded from the balance sheet, takes pro forma net debt to roughly one hundred ninety-four point nine million before Q7 cash generation.

**COLE:** Meaning the deal does not get to be merely strategically sensible. It has to be financeable through a downside without forcing Harborline to starve the exact products and teams that make the acquisition useful.

### WHAT BEACON ACTUALLY IS

**COLE:** Beacon Inspect serves the same broad world as Harborline, but from a different starting point. Harborline began in dispatch and field-service operations. Beacon began with regulated inspections: fire panels, elevator certifications, pressure equipment, and the evidence packages contractors hand to customers and authorities.

**ASHA:** One hundred eighty customers. Twelve million dollars of verified ARR. Average ACV of about sixty-seven thousand dollars. Sixteen percent trailing growth. Ninety-one percent GRR. One and a half million dollars of EBITDA. Asking price: forty-two million, or three and a half times ARR and twenty-eight times EBITDA.

**COLE:** Twenty-eight times EBITDA should stop anyone who thinks this is a cheap cost-cutting deal. Beacon is not being bought for current earnings. Harborline is paying for retained recurring revenue, an adjacent workflow, domain expertise, and the possibility that a combined product is more valuable than either product alone.

**ASHA:** Possibility. Not value yet. Let's reconstruct the growth. Beacon opened the measured year with about ten point three four million of ARR. Ninety-one percent gross retention left roughly nine point four one million before expansion. Existing customers added about one point one four million, taking NRR to approximately one hundred two percent. New logos contributed about one point four five million. Ending ARR: twelve million.

**COLE:** That is healthier than the headline alone suggested in one respect. Growth is not exclusively new-logo fuel poured into a leaking bucket. But ninety-one percent GRR is not excellent for a regulatory workflow that is supposed to be sticky. The churn interviews matter.

**ASHA:** They show three different causes. Small contractors churn because Beacon requires more setup than they can support. A mid-market cohort churns when implementation overruns collide with a renewal. And several industrial customers leave because Beacon's reporting is excellent but its dispatch integration is weak.

**COLE:** That third cause is the strategic fit. Harborline can plausibly solve it. But the first two are warnings. Harborline does not acquire the right to call a product integrated just because sales can bundle it. If identity, equipment records, entitlements, implementation, and support remain separate, the customer receives two contracts and a promise.

**ASHA:** Customer concentration is manageable, not trivial. Beacon's top twenty customers represent thirty-one percent of ARR. Its largest is under four percent. Fourteen contracts representing nine hundred thousand dollars require change-of-control notice. Two of those, together worth about two hundred thousand, require affirmative consent.

**COLE:** And the consent question is not paperwork to finish after the press release. If a customer can leave or demand new terms when control changes, that ARR has a different value until consent is in hand.

**ASHA:** The quality-of-earnings work also removes seven hundred thousand dollars from the seller's promoted ARR. The seller called Beacon a twelve point seven million ARR company. Three hundred thousand was implementation work presented as recurring. Four hundred thousand came from contracts signed but not activated. Harborline applies the metric contract it learned in episode two and verifies twelve million.

**COLE:** That is a quiet but important compounding effect. Harborline almost bought itself on the wrong definitions. Six quarters later, those definitions are now an acquisition capability. An operating system is valuable when it changes the next decision.

### PRODUCT, TECHNICAL, AND DATA DILIGENCE

**COLE:** Financial diligence says what Beacon has earned and retained. Product diligence asks why customers choose it, whether that reason will persist, and whether Harborline can strengthen it without destroying it.

**ASHA:** The strongest evidence is not the roadmap. It is the renewal interviews. Beacon's best customers do not describe it as inspection software. They say it is the record that proves work happened correctly. That is a high-value job with regulatory consequences.

**COLE:** And it changes the integration thesis. If you treat Beacon as a feature to fold into Harborline's mobile app, you can erase the very workflow precision customers buy. The product has forty-seven inspection templates with jurisdiction-specific evidence rules. Technicians can complete them offline. Supervisors can see missing proof before a report leaves the company. Those capabilities are not decorative screens. They encode years of edge cases.

**ASHA:** The product weakness is equally specific. Beacon creates a compliant inspection record, then hands the result to another system to schedule remediation, quote work, dispatch a technician, and invoice. Harborline already owns those downstream steps for many of the same customer types.

**COLE:** So the product thesis is a closed loop: inspect, identify a defect, retrieve asset history, draft the remediation scope, quote it, dispatch it, complete it, and attach evidence. Assist can operate inside that loop. But notice what we have not said. We have not said Beacon's data trains a magical model. We have said the combined workflow can remove handoffs and increase the probability that identified work becomes completed work.

**ASHA:** Now the architecture. Beacon has a modern mobile client and a reasonably clean inspection service. It also has custom authentication, its own customer and asset identifiers, a separate billing system, and a reporting store whose tenant controls were designed before the current enterprise product.

**COLE:** The diligence team runs three tests that matter. First, can Beacon map an inspection to Harborline's equipment identity without manual reconciliation? On the sample, eighty-three percent maps confidently, eleven percent maps with a human review, and six percent has no safe match. That is workable, but not automatic.

**ASHA:** Second, can Harborline enforce tenant permissions when Assist retrieves Beacon evidence? Not on day one. Beacon's reporting permissions are role-based inside Beacon, while Harborline's are account, location, asset, and technician based. Combining retrieval before combining authorization would recreate the cross-tenant risk from episode four in a new form.

**COLE:** Third, can Beacon's core workflow survive Harborline volume? The load tests say yes for inspections and no for report generation at Harborline's peak assumptions. That does not require a rewrite. It requires moving report generation to a queued service and measuring time to completed evidence package.

**ASHA:** Security diligence finds one unresolved high-severity identity issue and weak secrets rotation. There is no evidence of exploitation. The issue is fixable before close, independently verified, and written as a closing condition rather than a hopeful integration task.

**COLE:** Then data rights. Beacon's contracts covering seventy-two percent of ARR clearly permit derived, de-identified product improvement. The rest permit the customer to use the service but are ambiguous about pooled improvement. Historical inspection photographs are less clean because some originated with subcontractors.

**ASHA:** Therefore the acquisition recommendation gives no value to unrestricted pooled training on the full Beacon corpus. Tenant-isolated retrieval can still serve the contracted workflow. Broader improvement rights require amendments or exclusion. That distinction is the difference between having useful customer data and owning a transferable data asset.

**COLE:** Leadership diligence matters just as much. Beacon's founder intends to leave after six months. The vice president of product understands the regulatory ontology. The head of engineering owns most of the architectural context. Two customer-success leaders hold the renewal relationships in the concentrated cohort.

**ASHA:** Retention packages for those four roles are not soft integration spending. They are part of the purchase economics. If the people who know the product, systems, and customers leave before knowledge transfers, the code does not become self-explanatory because Harborline owns the shares.

### THE SYNERGY LEDGER

**ASHA:** Now we reach the most abused word in acquisition finance: synergy.

**COLE:** A synergy is usually born as a colored rectangle in a presentation.

**ASHA:** And then counted three times. Product calls it cross-sell. Sales calls it pipeline. Finance calls it ARR. The sponsor applies a multiple. Nobody has a customer contract.

**COLE:** So Harborline builds a synergy ledger. Every line needs a baseline, a mechanism, an owner, a gross benefit, the cost to realize it, timing, confidence, evidence, and finance validation. If any field is blank, the benefit does not enter the operating case.

**ASHA:** Start with cost synergies. The headline is three million dollars of annual run-rate savings by month eighteen. Six hundred thousand from duplicate finance, insurance, and audit costs. Seven hundred thousand from cloud and observability consolidation after measured migration. Five hundred thousand from overlapping vendors. One point two million from billing, support tooling, facilities, and selected duplicate management work.

**COLE:** Not from firing the product team on Day One. That would be easy to put in the model and expensive to reverse. The people maintaining customer trust and product knowledge remain until the combined operating model proves what work actually disappears.

**ASHA:** The base case credits only one point two million of verified run-rate cost synergy by Q8. The rest remains an initiative, not earnings. Finance signs each line after the cost has left the run rate. A purchase order canceled in six months is not a realized saving today.

**COLE:** Revenue synergy is more uncertain. Harborline sees three cohorts. Fire and life-safety customers have a direct need for Beacon's compliance workflow. Elevator contractors have strong functional fit but long buying cycles. General industrial-maintenance customers sound adjacent in a slide and behave differently in discovery.

**ASHA:** The seller model assumes six million dollars of cross-sell ARR within twenty-four months. Harborline's base case uses three million, weighted by account eligibility, contact rate, qualified opportunity rate, win rate, implementation capacity, and time to activation. The downside uses zero.

**COLE:** And even three million does not enter value as three million of EBITDA. It produces ARR when a contract is live. It produces revenue over the service period. It produces contribution after sales commission, implementation, support, cloud cost, and any Assist workload cost. That sequence matters.

**ASHA:** There is also dis-synergy. Integration leaders are not doing other work. Customers may delay renewals while they wait for the combined roadmap. Two systems run in parallel. Legal amendments cost money. Sales can confuse the message. Harborline budgets three point two million of integration cash over the first one hundred eighty days and includes it in the liquidity case.

**COLE:** The decision is therefore not, "Do synergies exceed forty-two million?" The decision is whether retained Beacon cash flows plus risk-adjusted synergies produce an acceptable return while Harborline survives the downside.

### SOURCES, USES, AND THE DOWNSIDE

**ASHA:** Forty-two million dollars of purchase price is funded on Harborline's balance sheet. Q6 net debt is one hundred fifty-two point nine million. Add forty-two and debt reaches one hundred ninety-four point nine before Q7 cash generation. After approximately two million of Q7 debt paydown, quarter-end net debt is one hundred ninety-two point nine.

**COLE:** Why not fund it with new sponsor equity?

**ASHA:** The sponsor could. New equity would lower risk and dilute the return on the original investment. The lender package permits the add-on, and the pro forma case remains inside its limit, but only barely. At close, one hundred ninety-four point nine million of debt divided by Harborline's twenty-seven point seven six million of trailing EBITDA plus Beacon's one point five million is about six point seven times pro forma trailing EBITDA, before any unverified synergy. That is not a reason to maximize debt. It is a reason to test the downside carefully.

**COLE:** The downside assumes Beacon loses ten percent of ARR, revenue synergy is zero, only half the cost synergy arrives, and Harborline's organic NRR slips two points because leadership is distracted.

**ASHA:** Under that case, cash headroom becomes the binding constraint. Harborline can still service debt, but it cannot fund a big-bang migration, an unplanned pricing concession, and a second acquisition. The integration plan must preserve reversibility because the capital structure has removed room for theatrical mistakes.

**COLE:** There is another form of leverage: architectural leverage. If Harborline forces Beacon onto the core codebase before identity, permissions, and customer workflows are understood, it creates an irreversible dependency at the same time financial leverage is highest.

**ASHA:** Which is why "one platform" is an outcome to earn, not a Day One org-chart decision.

### THE DECISION GATE

[MUSIC STING]

**ASHA:** Three credible choices, Kevin.

**COLE:** Choice one: buy Beacon for forty-two million now. You gain the customer base, inspection workflow, domain team, and the chance to close the service loop. You also raise net debt by forty-two million, inherit security and data-rights work, and expose Harborline's core plan to integration distraction.

**ASHA:** Choice two: negotiate a commercial partnership with an option to acquire later. Harborline integrates Beacon through APIs, tests cross-sell, and preserves capital. But the seller is running an auction, not looking for a strategic experiment. Another owner may buy Beacon, restrict the partnership, raise the eventual price, or sell into Harborline's accounts.

**COLE:** Choice three: build inspection capability. Harborline controls architecture and data rights from the start. The product team estimates eight to ten million dollars and twelve to eighteen months for a credible first set of regulated workflows. That estimate excludes the time required to earn jurisdiction knowledge and displace an installed vendor.

**ASHA:** Grade them on the five dimensions. Value created: buy has the largest immediate strategic surface, build may create the cleanest long-term asset, partner produces the least controlled value. Cash consumed: partner wins, build spreads cost, buy consumes forty-two million immediately. Risk added: buy adds financial and integration risk, build adds execution and timing risk, partner adds dependency risk. Speed to evidence: partner and buy can test cross-sell quickly; build cannot. Reversibility: partner is highest, build is moderately reversible until scale, acquisition is not.

**COLE:** The acquisition is attractive only because six prior quarters changed Harborline's ability to integrate. If this exact target appeared in Q2, the correct answer would have been no. Strategy is state-dependent.

**ASHA:** Make the call. Buy, partner, or build. And write down what evidence would make your choice wrong. We will give you a moment.

### THE CANONICAL CALL

**COLE:** The canonical call is buy, with conditions that are part of the decision rather than tasks to negotiate after it.

**ASHA:** Purchase price capped at forty-two million. The high-severity identity issue is remediated and independently retested before close. The two affirmative change-of-control consents are received. Data-rights schedules become a representation with a specific indemnity. Retention agreements are signed with the four critical leaders. No value is assigned to pooled training rights that Harborline cannot prove.

**COLE:** And the product thesis is not "merge Beacon." It is "close the inspect-to-remediate loop while preserving the evidence workflow customers trust." That wording gives the integration team something observable to produce.

**ASHA:** The decision memo has six lines. Choice: acquire Beacon. Capital: forty-two million purchase price plus three point two million of integration cash over one hundred eighty days. Owner: chief operating officer Malik Chen is the accountable integration executive. He controls sequencing, integration resources, and the right to pause migration or cross-sell; the Beacon product leader controls customer and product continuity, and CFO Priya Shah validates every synergy claim. Target: retain at least ninety percent of Beacon ARR through the first twelve months and produce one point two million of finance-verified cost synergy run rate by Q8. Guardrails: Harborline core NRR does not fall below one hundred four, availability remains above ninety-nine point nine, and no Beacon data enters Assist without mapped permissions and rights. Kill criterion: if migration defects exceed half a percent of records or at-risk Beacon ARR exceeds ten percent, pause customer migrations and cross-sell expansion until the root cause is closed.

**COLE:** The Sunday bid wins exclusivity. Accelerated confirmatory work, the independent security retest, consents, and retention agreements consume the remaining two weeks. Beacon closes immediately after Q6 ends, legally effective on Q7's first day. That exact timing matters: Q6 remains a standalone Harborline close, while Q7 contains a full quarter of Beacon revenue and the acquisition debt. A later close would require partial-period consolidation rather than the full-quarter method we are about to use.

**COLE:** Notice the kill criterion does not pretend the acquisition can be undone. It protects the reversible parts of execution. You can stop a migration wave. You can stop bundling. You can preserve a product longer. Good integration design creates smaller doors even after you walk through the big one.

### DAY ONE IS NOT DAY ONE HUNDRED

**COLE:** The one-hundred-eighty-day blueprint separates what must be unified immediately from what must be learned before unification.

**ASHA:** Before close, Harborline freezes the baselines. Customer-level ARR, renewal dates, product usage, support backlog, security findings, employee roster, cloud spend, vendor contracts, and every synergy line. If the baseline moves after close, the company will never know whether integration created value or merely changed the ruler.

**COLE:** Day One has five jobs. Protect access. Protect cash. Protect customers. Protect people. Protect accountability.

**ASHA:** Protect access means Harborline takes control of privileged identity, secrets, incident escalation, and security monitoring. Not a complete technical integration. Control of the keys.

**COLE:** Protect cash means finance, treasury, payroll, purchasing authority, and revenue reporting move under known controls. Beacon keeps serving customers, but there cannot be two unofficial definitions of bookings and ARR after close.

**ASHA:** Protect customers means every renewal and open implementation has one named owner. The top twenty accounts receive a call that explains what changes now, what does not change, and who is accountable. Nobody promises a unified platform date.

**COLE:** Protect people means the critical leaders know their roles and incentives. Everyone else gets a clear thirty-day operating model, not six months of "business as usual" followed by a surprise reorganization.

**ASHA:** Protect accountability means the integration executive owns the plan, functional leaders own their lines, and finance independently validates benefits. A steering committee that collectively owns the outcome owns nothing.

**COLE:** Days two through thirty are about visibility. Map customer and asset identities. Build the contract and renewal calendar. Reconcile entitlements. Route support escalation. Instrument the Beacon workflow with the same activation and reliability definitions Harborline uses.

**ASHA:** Also conduct the talent decisions quickly enough to be humane. Which roles are essential? Which are duplicated? Which people could fill gaps Harborline already has? Delaying every decision may feel kind to leadership and feels cruel to the organization living inside uncertainty.

**COLE:** Days thirty-one through one hundred are where the technical thesis gets tested. Harborline builds a canonical asset mapping service, but it preserves source identifiers. It introduces Harborline identity and authorization at the boundary before allowing cross-product retrieval. It moves report generation onto the queued service. It exposes inspection defects to the quote and dispatch workflow through a versioned API.

**ASHA:** And it runs one cross-sell cohort, not the whole installed base. Fire and life-safety accounts with clear inspection needs, clean implementation capacity, and executive sponsors. Every stage is measured: eligible accounts, conversations, qualified opportunities, signed commitments, activated customers, retained ARR, and contribution.

**COLE:** Days one hundred one through one hundred eighty decide what deserves to scale. The team may have enough evidence to converge mobile navigation, support tooling, and customer administration. It will not have enough evidence for a complete codebase retirement. That decision stays outside the first one hundred eighty days unless operating facts make it obvious.

**ASHA:** Cost synergies follow the same sequence. Finance and audit duplication can leave early. Cloud commitments leave only after workloads move and reliability holds. Product and customer roles leave only when work actually disappears or account ownership transfers safely.

### THE ADVERSE FACT ARRIVES

**COLE:** The initial fire-safety cohort behaves close to plan. The industrial-maintenance cohort does not. The model expected one in four eligible accounts to accept a qualified discovery meeting. Fewer than one in ten do.

**ASHA:** Sales says the list is wrong. Product says the positioning is wrong. Beacon says industrial customers buy inspection from specialist providers rather than running it internally. This is the moment bad acquisition teams protect the synergy number.

**COLE:** Harborline does not. It cuts the industrial cohort forecast, preserves the fire-safety cohort, and refuses to move unqualified pipeline into ARR guidance. The expected cross-sell case gets smaller.

**ASHA:** That is not an integration failure. It is speed to evidence working. The failure would be paying commissions on signatures, forcing implementation, and discovering twelve months later that the customers never activated.

**COLE:** The team also learns that Beacon's inspection taxonomy improves Assist retrieval for the fire-safety workflow, but only after asset identity maps cleanly. The model-quality gain is cohort-specific. It is not described as a company-wide AI uplift.

**ASHA:** This is why the synergy ledger needs evidence and confidence. One line moves from medium confidence to high. Another moves to low. The total changes. A ledger that never changes is a fundraising document, not an operating instrument.

Net organic creation is six point two million. Ending ARR is one hundred twenty-eight point five million.

### THE OPERATOR DRILL

**ASHA:** Your artifact is a four-part acquisition pack you can build for any add-on.

**COLE:** Part one is the acquisition recommendation. One page. Strategic job to be done, verified financial baseline, customer evidence, product fit, architecture and security findings, data rights, leadership dependency, valuation, downside, and a direct recommendation. If it needs forty slides to explain why the target matters, the thesis is not ready.

**ASHA:** Part two is sources and uses. Purchase price, fees, integration cash, financing source, pro forma debt, interest, covenant headroom, and downside liquidity. Show enterprise value separately from equity funding and debt. Make the cash consequence visible.

**COLE:** Part three is the synergy ledger. One row per benefit. Baseline, mechanism, gross benefit, cost to achieve, timing, owner, confidence, proof source, and finance sign-off. Revenue synergy does not become value until it becomes activated, retained contribution. Cost synergy does not become value until cost leaves the run rate without moving somewhere else.

**ASHA:** Part four is the one-hundred-eighty-day blueprint. Separate pre-close, Day One, days two to thirty, thirty-one to one hundred, and one hundred one to one hundred eighty. For every workstream, state what unifies now, what remains separate, the customer guardrail, and the evidence required for the next move.

**COLE:** Then ask one brutal question: if the revenue synergies are zero, do you still want to own the company at this price? If the answer is no, you are buying a forecast. If the answer is yes for clear, provable reasons, the synergy becomes upside rather than anesthesia.
