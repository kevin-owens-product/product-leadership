### The problem ENTRO solves

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer's problem. Who are the customers, and what do they need?

**JAMES:** The customers are businesses and public bodies with buildings. Industrial sites, warehouses and logistics centres, hospitals and care homes, offices, retail, schools, public buildings. Anyone with doors, gates, fire-safety systems and access control that have to work and, in many cases, have to be inspected by law.

**NORA:** Explain the inspection part.

**JAMES:** As general background, in Germany and across much of Europe, many powered doors and gates, fire doors and smoke and heat extraction systems must be inspected at regular intervals, often annually, by qualified people. The rules come from a mix of building regulations, workplace safety rules and technical standards. The details vary by type of equipment and by German state, and I won't pretend to know every rule. The point is that building owners carry legal responsibility, and they need a documented inspection to show they've met it.

**NORA:** And smoke and heat extraction is what?

**JAMES:** Systems that open vents, usually in the roof or high on walls, to let smoke and heat escape in a fire. That keeps escape routes clearer and helps firefighters. In German it's abbreviated R W A. If those vents don't open when needed, the consequences can be very serious. So they're inspected and maintained regularly.

**NORA:** So what does the customer buy?

**JAMES:** Usually a recurring inspection and maintenance contract, plus repairs when something breaks, plus occasionally new installations or upgrades. And here's the important bit. A large site might have dozens or hundreds of doors and gates from many different manufacturers, installed over decades. The customer wants one firm that can service all of them.

**NORA:** Rather than calling each manufacturer.

**JAMES:** Exactly. That's ENTRO's pitch: service that's independent of any single manufacturer, often shortened to O E M independent, for original equipment manufacturer. One provider, many brands. At the same time, some of its companies are also authorised partners of particular manufacturers, which gives them access to parts and training. For example, Lütjen, which joined in 2026, is an authorised partner of Hörmann, Novoferm and Teckentrup, three well-known German door and gate makers.

**NORA:** What's driving demand?

**JAMES:** According to the group and the private equity firm: stricter regulation, rising awareness of security, energy efficiency, which matters because badly sealing doors and gates waste heat, the skilled-labour shortage, which pushes customers to outsource, and owners of small service firms looking for succession. That last one drives the supply of companies to buy, rather than customer demand.

**NORA:** Why does energy efficiency come into a door business?

**JAMES:** Think of a large logistics hall in winter. Its loading doors open and close hundreds of times a day. If they're slow, badly sealed or left open because a sensor's faulty, a lot of heated air escapes. Fast, well-maintained doors and good seals can cut that loss. As general background, with energy prices a big concern in Germany in recent years, building operators pay more attention to that than they used to. So maintenance and upgrades become partly an energy decision.

**NORA:** And security awareness?

**JAMES:** Access control and intrusion systems, who can get in where, and what happens if someone tries to force a door. Businesses and public bodies have become more conscious of that, according to the group. And it links naturally to doors and gates, because the door is where access control physically happens.

### How the group works

**NORA:** Let's talk about how the group is put together. Is it one company or sixteen?

**JAMES:** Both, in a way. ENTRO buys regional firms and, crucially, the firms usually keep their local names. M and S Türen und Tore in Bisingen, Lütjen in Worpswede near Bremen, SWI Württemberg in Pfullingen, and so on. Other names in the group include KHT, Gebrüder Kuttner, Torpado, Wassermann, ITS, TSB and an electrical firm called Elektro Ingenieur Dietmar Wolf, plus the Dutch companies. And a parts business called OK Parts, in Düren.

**NORA:** Why keep the local names?

**JAMES:** Because in local services, the name is the relationship. A facilities manager at a hospital in Swabia has been calling the same local firm for twenty years. They know the technicians. If that firm suddenly becomes an anonymous national brand, the customer may wonder whether the service will change. Keeping the name reassures customers and staff. It's common in buy-and-build strategies of this kind.

**NORA:** And what does the group add?

**JAMES:** In its January 2025 release, the group named shared purchasing, IT and recruiting as synergies. So the idea is that local firms keep their identity and customer relationships, while the group provides scale benefits behind the scenes: better parts prices, shared systems, help with hiring, and a succession solution for owners.

**NORA:** And it's meant to be a partnership.

**JAMES:** The group calls its approach partnership-based. Owners and managers often stay on, sometimes with a stake. That's similar to what we've seen in other episodes in this series: the founder doesn't just sell and leave.

**NORA:** Let's imagine it from the seller's side. What does an owner get from joining? Invented, of course.

**JAMES:** Picture Herr Bauer, sixty-four, who built a door and gate service firm with twenty technicians over thirty years. His children have other careers. His best technician is good with machines but doesn't want to run a business. Without a successor, his options are to sell to a competitor, to a manufacturer, or slowly wind down. Joining a group like ENTRO lets him sell, often keep some role for a transition period, keep the company's name and keep his team employed.

**NORA:** And what does he worry about?

**JAMES:** Whether his customers will be looked after, whether his staff will be treated well, and whether head office will make everything bureaucratic. Those are the questions any group like this has to answer convincingly, because word travels among owners in a region. If one owner has a bad experience, the next three may say no.

**NORA:** So reputation among sellers is an asset too.

**JAMES:** In buy-and-build, it's a crucial one. The group's ability to keep buying depends on being a home owners want to sell to.

**NORA:** You mentioned OK Parts. Why does a parts company matter?

**JAMES:** In my view it's quietly strategic. If you service doors and gates from many manufacturers, getting the right part quickly is a big deal. A technician who arrives without the right motor or sensor has to come back another day. A group-owned parts business can stock and buy parts across brands for all sixteen companies. That's a scale advantage local firms don't have.

**NORA:** And the service lines?

**JAMES:** Three, as the group describes them. Entrance systems: doors, gates, barriers, loading bays. Fire safety: smoke and heat extraction, fire doors, escape route systems. And access and security: access control and intrusion systems. Bundling those three for one customer is part of the pitch.

**NORA:** And the meetings you mentioned?

**JAMES:** The group's news headlines show a managing directors' meeting in Hamburg in September 2026, where it reported continued growth, and a service managers' meeting in Dortmund in October 2026. We only saw the headlines. But bringing the managers of sixteen companies together regularly is how a group like this starts to build a common culture and common ways of working.

**JAMES:** None found. The group has mentioned certified services as a goal. The market itself is regulated, because the equipment is safety-critical, but that's different from the company holding, say, an information security certification.

**NORA:** What systems do the sixteen companies use?

Some might use a simple scheduling tool and paper inspection forms. Some might rely on an office manager's spreadsheet and an experienced dispatcher's memory. And each will have its own customer records, its own inspection report formats and its own parts lists. That's typical in roll-ups, and nothing ENTRO has published suggests otherwise.

**NORA:** What does a good field-service platform actually do?

**JAMES:** In general terms, it holds every customer, every site and every asset, like each door, gate or smoke vent, with its make, model, installation date and history. It schedules inspections automatically based on due dates. It dispatches technicians to jobs, ideally with their skills and location in mind. It gives technicians a mobile app for inspection checklists, photos and signatures. It tracks parts used, triggers invoices and quotes, and reports on performance. Many vendors sell such systems.

**NORA:** So it's the operating system of the business.

**JAMES:** That's a good way to put it. And the asset history is the part that makes AI possible later. If every fault on every door model across sixteen companies is recorded consistently, you have the training material for a diagnostic assistant.

**NORA:** So the foundation for AI isn't there yet.

**JAMES:** Publicly, no. And that's not a failing. It's simply where a fast-growing, one-year-old platform would be. The point is the order of operations. You can't build a useful AI assistant over sixteen separate data silos in different formats and two or three languages. First you need a common field-service platform. Then AI.

**NORA:** You've said that before in this series.

**JAMES:** I have, and I'll keep saying it. For roll-ups, the first AI project is usually a data and systems project.

### A technician's day

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through a day for a technician. Invented, not real.

**JAMES:** Let's call him Jonas. He works for one of ENTRO's regional companies in southern Germany. He's been fixing doors and gates for fifteen years. He's in a van by seven. Today: three inspections, a breakdown call and a new customer.

**NORA:** First stop?

**JAMES:** A logistics warehouse with twelve high-speed roll-up doors and four fire doors. Annual inspection. He tests each door: safety edges, light barriers, emergency stop, spring tension, drive. He checks that the fire doors close and latch properly. He fills in an inspection report for each, maybe on a tablet, maybe on paper. One door has a worn safety edge. He notes it and recommends a repair.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** At ten, a call comes in from the office. A care home's automatic entrance door is stuck open. That's a security and heating problem, and for a care home, residents with dementia might wander out. Jonas is the closest technician. He drives over. It's a brand he's seen only twice before. The control unit shows a fault code he doesn't recognise.

**NORA:** What does he do?

**JAMES:** Today? He calls a colleague who might know. The colleague's on a roof. He searches on his phone for the manual. He finds a PDF in another language. He tries a couple of things. Forty-five minutes later he works out it's a sensor fault, but he doesn't have the part in the van. He sets the door to a safe manual mode and books a return visit.

**NORA:** So the care home waits.

**JAMES:** And Jonas loses half a day on the return trip. Then in the afternoon, another inspection, at a school, of smoke and heat extraction vents. He checks the vents open, the control panel, the batteries, the triggers. Again, a report. Then a quote visit at a new customer's industrial site, where the facilities manager wants one firm to take over all their doors and gates from three previous suppliers.

**NORA:** And at the end of the day?

**JAMES:** Paperwork. Inspection reports to finish, the parts order for the care home, a note for the office about the worn safety edge at the warehouse so someone can send a quote. Maybe an hour of admin after a full day of physical work.

**NORA:** Where do the problems lie?

**JAMES:** Three places. Finding information: the fault code at the care home. Having the right part: the return trip. And paperwork: the evening reports. Each is a waste of a scarce technician's time.

**NORA:** Now replay it with a common platform and an AI assistant.

**JAMES:** Jonas arrives at the care home. On his tablet, the system already knows this customer and this door, because every asset is in one database. He types or says the fault code. The assistant, built on the manufacturer manuals and the group's own fault histories from all sixteen companies, replies: this code on this model usually means a failed motion sensor; here's the test to confirm; here's the part number. And it adds that technicians at two other group companies fixed the same fault last year.

**NORA:** So he's drawing on the whole group's experience.

**JAMES:** Exactly. That's the power of putting sixteen companies on one platform. Then the system checks stock: OK Parts has the sensor, and a colleague's van ten kilometres away has one too. The dispatcher arranges a hand-over. Jonas fixes the door in one visit.

**NORA:** And the inspection reports?

**JAMES:** He records readings as he goes, and the system drafts the reports and flags the worn safety edge as a repair opportunity, which automatically creates a quote for the office to review. By the time he's back in the van, the paperwork is mostly done. He reviews and signs.

**NORA:** What doesn't change?

**JAMES:** The physical work, his judgement, and his responsibility. The assistant suggests. Jonas decides. If he disagrees with the suggested diagnosis, he goes with his own experience. And the inspection is still his signed record.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's talk challenges.

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, integration. Sixteen companies across three countries in about a year. Each with its own systems, culture and customer relationships. Getting them onto common systems without losing customers or staff is the biggest operational challenge.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** The skilled-labour shortage. The group itself names it. Technicians are the constraint. Every hour lost to searching, return trips and paperwork is an hour not spent on billable service. And new technicians take time to learn the variety of equipment out there.

**NORA:** Let's stay on that for a moment. How does the shortage play out in practice?

**JAMES:** As general background, Germany has a well-known shortage of skilled tradespeople. Many experienced technicians are approaching retirement, and fewer young people are entering the trades. For a firm like Jonas's, that means it may turn down work, or take longer to respond. It also means the knowledge in experienced technicians' heads, which brand does what, which fault code means what, is at risk of walking out of the door when they retire.

**NORA:** So knowledge capture is part of the problem.

**JAMES:** It's a big part, in my view. An AI assistant built on fault histories is, in a sense, a way of preserving and sharing that knowledge. And there's a recruiting angle. Young technicians expect digital tools. A firm that still runs on paper forms is less attractive to them.

**NORA:** Third challenge?

**JAMES:** Third, the digital starting point. No public digital product, portal or software. No named technology leader. That's a lot to build.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Inconsistent group disclosures. Nine, twelve, sixteen companies; two hundred to five hundred staff; sixteen to twenty-five locations. Some of that is real growth. Some may be counting differences. Internally, our inference is that consolidated reporting across sixteen companies is still developing, which matters for running the business and for a future buyer.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, the sub-scale UK platform. About a hundred staff and ten million euros. It needs more deals to be efficient, and running a second country with different standards adds complexity.

**NORA:** Let me push back on integration. If local firms keep their names and relationships, why force common systems on them?

**JAMES:** Fair question. I wouldn't force everything. Keep the names, the local managers, the customer relationships. But some things should be common: one field-service system for scheduling, asset records and inspection reports; one parts and purchasing process through OK Parts; one finance and reporting system. Without those, you can't share knowledge, you can't dispatch across company boundaries, and you can't use AI across the group. And you can't show a buyer one set of numbers.

**NORA:** How do you get local owners to agree?

**JAMES:** Show them what they get: faster parts, fewer return trips, less evening paperwork, help with recruiting. And involve their best technicians and dispatchers in choosing the platform. People resist systems that are imposed and adopt systems they helped shape.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Opportunities.

**JAMES:** The highest-value one, in my view, is an AI field-service assistant: a shared knowledge base over manuals, fault histories and inspection reports, plus AI-assisted dispatch. The payoff is in technician hours and how quickly new technicians become productive.

**NORA:** Can you size it?

**JAMES:** Only as an illustration, built on assumptions, not facts. Take the one hundred and seventy technicians from the September 2025 figure. If each saved twenty minutes a day, that's roughly fourteen thousand hours a year, or about nine full-time technicians' worth of time at sixteen hundred productive hours each. In a market where you can't hire enough technicians, freeing nine technicians' worth of capacity is significant. And the group has grown since, so the number could be larger.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** The common field-service and customer relationship platform across all sixteen companies, before the next wave of deals. Every deal added before the platform exists makes the integration bigger. Shared purchasing, IT and recruiting were already named as synergies, so this fits the group's own plan.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Parts and purchasing. Use OK Parts as the group-wide parts business across manufacturer brands. Better prices, better availability and fewer return trips.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Cross-selling and recurring contracts. Bundle door, fire safety and access service into one multi-year maintenance contract for existing customers. A warehouse that uses one ENTRO company for doors might also need its smoke vents and access control serviced. One contract, one invoice, one inspection calendar.

**NORA:** Fifth?

**JAMES:** Regional expansion. Rhein-Neckar and Rhein-Main, as the group has said. Building out the Netherlands under its new chief executive. And growing the UK platform. Each new region ideally lands on the common platform from day one.

**NORA:** And pricing? You raised the hourly risk earlier.

**JAMES:** Right. If maintenance contracts are priced per inspection or per asset, efficiency becomes margin. If repairs are billed by technician hour, faster diagnosis means less revenue per job, but also more jobs per day and happier customers. In a labour-short market, my view is that ENTRO will usually have more work than technicians, so efficiency translates into growth. Over time, moving more customers to fixed-price service agreements that cover inspection, preventive maintenance and a response-time promise would make that link explicit.

**NORA:** And acquisitions generally?

**JAMES:** Succession deals. The group markets itself to owners looking for a successor, which, given the age profile of many family business owners, as general background, is a steady source of targets. The more attractive ENTRO is as a home, with good tools and a good culture, the more owners will want to join.

**NORA:** Is there a customer-facing digital opportunity too?

**JAMES:** Yes, our inference. A customer portal where a facilities manager can see every door, gate and fire-safety system across their sites, with inspection status, upcoming due dates and open repairs. Larger customers increasingly expect that kind of visibility. It makes the service stickier and helps with cross-selling. But again, it depends on having one asset database first.

**NORA:** Let's look at it from the customer's side for a moment. Another invented example.

**JAMES:** Say a hospital group in the Rhein-Main area, with four hospitals, has around six hundred doors, gates, fire doors and smoke vents between them. Today its facilities manager, call her Frau Keller, deals with three or four different service firms, each with its own inspection schedule, report format and invoices. Before an inspection by the fire authorities, she spends days pulling together certificates from different folders and emails.

**NORA:** And what would she want instead?

**JAMES:** One contract covering doors, fire safety and access across all four sites. One portal showing every asset, its last inspection, the next due date and any open defects. Reports in one format. And a guaranteed response time for breakdowns, especially for entrances to emergency departments. If ENTRO can offer that, through its local companies and a common platform, it wins the whole contract rather than a slice.

**NORA:** And AI helps how?

**JAMES:** In small but meaningful ways. Drafting inspection reports consistently. Summarising open defects across sites for her monthly meeting, ranked by safety risk. Predicting which doors are likely to need major repairs next year, so she can budget. None of it replaces the technician, but all of it makes Frau Keller's job easier, which is what makes her renew.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet?

**JAMES:** The AI technician assistant and dispatch tool, built on one field-service platform. But I'd be explicit that in the first hundred days, most of the work is the platform, not the AI.

**NORA:** So how would you run it?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: map what exists. Which systems each of the sixteen companies uses, how they record assets and inspections, where manuals and fault records live. Pick a target field-service platform, ideally with input from dispatchers and senior technicians. Choose two or three pilot companies, ideally one that already has decent digital records and one that doesn't, plus one of the recent German acquisitions.

**NORA:** What's the hardest part of that mapping, do you think?

**JAMES:** Data quality and language, in my guess. Older firms may have asset records that are incomplete, or no asset records at all, just customer names and addresses with notes. Fault histories may be in free-text notes in German, Dutch or English, written in each technician's shorthand. Inspection report formats will differ. So before any AI, someone has to decide what a standard asset record looks like and clean the data into that shape. It's not glamorous. It's essential.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Start migrating the pilot companies' asset and customer data to the common platform. At the same time, start building the knowledge base: collect manufacturer manuals, the pilot companies' fault histories and inspection reports, and sit down with a few senior technicians about the faults they see most. Clean it, tag it by brand and model. That's the raw material for the assistant.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Put a first version of the assistant in the hands of ten or fifteen technicians in the pilot companies. Measure the basics: time to diagnose, first-visit fix rate, return trips, time spent on reports. Ask the technicians what helps and what's annoying. And measure dispatch: travel time, response time to breakdowns.

**NORA:** And then?

**JAMES:** If the pilots show fewer return trips and faster diagnoses, use that as the business case for rolling the platform out to all sixteen companies, with the assistant as the reason technicians want it. And publish the results inside the group, at the next service managers' meeting.

**NORA:** Any governance questions?

**JAMES:** A few. Manufacturer manuals may have copyright and licensing terms, so check what can be loaded into a knowledge base. Customer data, including site layouts and security system details, is sensitive, especially for access and intrusion systems, so security controls matter. And the assistant must never replace the technician's judgement on safety equipment. Its suggestions are advice. The inspection result is a human decision.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** Someone has to. If there's no technology leader today, hiring one, or appointing one from inside with real authority, is step zero. And pair that person with the chief operating officer, because this is as much an operations project as a technology one.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** What can other businesses learn from ENTRO?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, in a roll-up, the first AI project is a systems project. Every company in this series that's grown by acquisition faces this. The sooner you build the common platform, the cheaper it is.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, capture knowledge before it retires. Many trades businesses depend on experienced people's memories. An AI assistant built on fault histories and manuals turns individual memory into group knowledge. That's valuable whether or not you call it AI.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, in a labour-constrained business, AI is about capacity, not cuts. ENTRO can't hire enough technicians. Anything that frees technician time is growth. That's also the honest and motivating message for staff.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, keep local identity but share the back end. Customers and technicians value the local name. Scale comes from parts, systems and knowledge shared behind the scenes. ENTRO's model already does the first part. The second is the opportunity.

**NORA:** Is there a lesson about naming your problems?

**JAMES:** Yes, and it's a small one I liked. ENTRO publicly named digitalisation and the skills shortage as its challenges in January 2025. Many companies wouldn't. Naming them creates accountability, and it tells potential sellers and recruits that leadership sees the same problems they do.

**NORA:** And a fifth, maybe on speed?

**JAMES:** Yes. Speed of acquisition and speed of integration need to stay in balance. If you buy faster than you integrate, the integration backlog grows with every deal. At some point it's worth slowing deals briefly to build the platform, so that every future deal lands on it.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks.

**JAMES:** One, manufacturer disruption. If manufacturers move service toward remote monitoring and direct contracts, connected doors and gates that report their own faults, the independent service model could narrow. That's our inference. Two, AI affecting the revenue model. If AI cuts technician hours per inspection, and contracts are priced by the hour, revenue per contract falls unless pricing moves to fixed fees. Also our inference.

**NORA:** Three?

**JAMES:** The labour shortage itself, which limits growth no matter what. Four, integration risk across sixteen companies in three countries. And five, larger rivals like Securitas, dormakaba and ASSA ABLOY bundling service with their products.

**NORA:** Let me push on the first. Couldn't connected doors be an opportunity for ENTRO too?

**JAMES:** Absolutely, and I'd argue it should be. If doors and gates increasingly report their own status, then a multi-brand service provider that can read data from many manufacturers' systems and act on it is very valuable. That would need partnerships with manufacturers and a strong digital platform. Which, again, comes back to building the foundation first.

**NORA:** And from the owner's point of view, what's the timeline?

**JAMES:** The investment is about a year old, so a sale is not on the near horizon, as far as we know. But a future buyer of a sixteen-plus company group will ask how integrated it really is. One platform, one set of numbers, measurable improvements in technician productivity, and a credible digital story would all support a higher valuation. In that sense, the platform and the AI aren't just operating projects. They're part of the equity story.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any announcement of a common field-service platform or a technology leader. Second, the next deals, especially in Rhein-Neckar, Rhein-Main, the Netherlands and the UK. Third, any digital product for customers, like a portal. And fourth, any AI pilot, perhaps similar to the feasibility study we mentioned from Baden-Württemberg.
