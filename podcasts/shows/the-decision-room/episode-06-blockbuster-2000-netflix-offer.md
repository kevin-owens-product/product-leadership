### THE ROOM: RENAISSANCE TOWER, DALLAS, 2000 (4 minutes)

**ELI:** It's the year 2000 — accounts differ on the month; Marc Randolph's memoir puts it in early fall — and four men are riding an elevator up Renaissance Tower in downtown Dallas, headquarters of Blockbuster Inc. Three of them are from a two-year-old California startup: Reed Hastings, the CEO. Marc Randolph, the co-founder. Barry McCarthy, the CFO. They chartered a plane at short notice to be here, because getting this meeting took *months* of unreturned phone calls. Their company mails DVDs to people in red envelopes. It's called Netflix.

**DANA:** And let's be precise about what Netflix is in 2000, because the mythology inflates it. It is small and it is bleeding. Roughly three hundred thousand subscribers. Revenue for the year will come in around thirty-five million dollars — and the net loss will be *fifty-seven* million. The subscription model — no due dates, no late fees — is barely a year old. And the outside world has turned hostile: the dot-com bubble is actively collapsing, the NASDAQ is down by nearly half from its March peak, the IPO window has slammed shut, and companies with "dot com" in their pitch are dying weekly. Netflix needs money, and the public market is no longer an option. That's why they're in this elevator. This is not a courtesy call. This is a company that needs the deal.

**ELI:** Waiting for them upstairs is John Antioco, CEO of Blockbuster, with his general counsel. And the second piece of mythology to kill: Antioco is not a fool, and Blockbuster is not yet a punchline. It's a five-billion-dollar-revenue giant with about seven thousand seven hundred stores worldwide and tens of millions of member accounts. Antioco is a genuinely respected operator — the man turned around Circle K and Taco Bell before this. Blockbuster does have problems: it was spun off from Viacom carrying around a billion dollars of debt, and — the number that will hang over this whole episode — a large share of its profit comes from late fees. Hundreds of millions of dollars a year, on the order of fifteen, sixteen percent of revenue. The most profitable thing Blockbuster sells is the customer's own guilt.

**DANA:** The pitch Hastings makes in that office, per Randolph's account, is this: Blockbuster buys Netflix for fifty million dollars. The Netflix team stays and runs Blockbuster.com as the company's online arm — Blockbuster's brand and stores, plus Netflix's subscription engine, logistics, and web operation, which is years ahead of anything Blockbuster has built. Clicks and bricks, one company, ahead of the curve. Randolph writes that at the number "fifty million," Antioco appeared to be struggling not to laugh.

**ELI:** So there's the decision, and notice how different it looks without the ending attached. It's 2000. DVD players are in maybe one in seven or eight American households. DVD-by-mail is a niche of a niche, run by a money-losing startup, in the middle of a crash that is vaporizing exactly this kind of company. Fifty million dollars for *that* — or a polite pass? <breath> I'm taking the pass. Dana's buying. Nothing after the year 2000 exists in this room.

### THE CASE AGAINST BUYING: THE SMART MONEY'S NO (5 minutes)

**ELI:** Start with the price of the asset versus the state of the asset, because that's where every acquisition conversation should start. Fifty million dollars buys us a company with three hundred thousand subscribers and a fifty-seven-million-dollar annual loss — the purchase price is smaller than one year of the thing's own bleeding. That's not a revenue multiple; there's barely revenue to multiply. And the macro context isn't background noise, it's the whole point: it is the year 2000, and internet companies are being repriced toward zero *as we sit in this office*. Every week we wait, distressed dot-coms get cheaper. These men chartered a jet on a day's notice because their funding options are gone. You don't pay the desperate seller's *asking price* in a crash, and honestly — why buy at all? If the mail-order niche survives, we can buy it, or a competitor, or the whole category, out of the wreckage in eighteen months for less.

**ELI:** Second — and this is the argument I'd make even in a bull market — look at what their model does to *our* engine. No due dates. No late fees. That's the entire pitch. Now, our extended-viewing fees run to hundreds of millions a year — a sixth of revenue, and a much bigger share of *profit*. Buying Netflix means acquiring, promoting, and scaling a business whose founding premise is that our single most profitable line item is an abuse. We'd be paying fifty million dollars for the gun, then handing it to the people pointing it at us, then giving them our brand as ammunition. And before you call that late-fee money a dead man walking, remember: nobody in this room has any evidence customers will pay a *subscription* for movies at scale. Three hundred thousand early adopters is not evidence. Our seven thousand stores and the guilt of due dates are, right now, the only proven economics in home video.

**DANA:** And if the niche isn't a niche? If mail is what the customer actually prefers?

**ELI:** Then we build it ourselves — third argument. What, exactly, in Netflix's operation can a five-billion-dollar company with the most recognized brand in the category not replicate? Warehouses? We have the greatest physical distribution footprint in the industry — seven thousand seven hundred points of presence the mail can never match. A website? We'll hire engineers; it's 2000, they're suddenly *very* available. Their subscriber list is a rounding error against our member base. If and when DVD-by-mail proves real, we enter with our brand and crush them on marketing spend alone — the fast-follower play, from an incumbent that actually owns the customer relationship. Fifty million buys us nothing we can't build for less, later, with proof.

**ELI:** And the boring argument last, as tradition demands. We are one year off a spin-out from Viacom, carrying a billion dollars of debt, with a stock the market already distrusts and franchisees who revolt at channel conflict. Our mandate from the board is operational discipline — Antioco's specialty, the reason he's in the chair. Walking back to that board in this market to announce we've spent fifty million dollars on a *money-losing website* is how a turnaround CEO becomes a former CEO. <sigh> The responsible answer, the fiduciary answer, the answer virtually every operator in America gives in the year 2000, is: thank you, gentlemen, safe flight home.

### THE CASE FOR BUYING: A ONE PERCENT PREMIUM ON EXTINCTION (5 minutes)

**DANA:** Everything Eli said is true, and I want you to notice it's the *same shape* of true as episode one. Protect the proven engine. Let the pioneers bleed. Enter late with your brand and your muscle. It was the losing argument in Los Gatos in 2007, and I'm going to show you it's the losing argument in Dallas in 2000 — using only what this room knows. <breath> Start with the number, because Eli wants you to hear "fifty million for a money-loser" and I want you to hear what it actually is: one percent of our annual revenue. That's the entire ask. Not a merger, not a bet-the-company integration — one penny on the revenue dollar. Now ask the episode-one question: what's the *asymmetry*? If we buy and mail-order stays niche, we've overpaid for a website and a warehouse system — embarrassing, forgettable, gone in a footnote. If we pass and the model is real, we have handed a seven-year head start to the one competitor whose product is *our profit engine's mortality*. Those mistakes are not the same size. You pay one percent of revenue as the insurance premium against the extinction-sized one. Every time.

**DANA:** Second: Eli says nothing in Netflix is unbuildable. Correct — and irrelevant, because we wouldn't be buying their assets. We'd be buying their *elapsed time*. Two years of subscription economics — churn curves, usage patterns, the honest answer to "will people pay monthly for movies," which Eli just admitted nobody in this room knows. The answer exists, Eli. It's in their cohort data, and fifty million dollars buys it *today*, while "build it ourselves" buys it in 2003 after we've re-made every mistake they've already paid for. This is the muscles-not-features lesson wearing different clothes: logistics software, licensing instincts, subscriber behavior — these accumulate, they don't install. And notice what else fifty million buys: the founding team, contractually motivated, running *our* online arm instead of raising money to destroy us. The people are the acquisition. The DVDs are packaging.

**ELI:** They're desperate, Dana. Desperate sellers get negotiated down, not bought at ask.

**DANA:** Then *negotiate*, John! That's my third point — the tragedy hiding in this room isn't the price, it's the laugh. Their desperation is our leverage: counter at twenty-five, demand earnouts, take a controlling stake instead — any structure you like. A sophisticated buyer facing a motivated seller of a strategically vital asset has a dozen moves available, and "no meeting" isn't one of them. The only way to get this catastrophically wrong is to treat the smallness of the number as proof of the smallness of the threat. Which is exactly the reflex I'm asking this room to catch in itself: the offer is being rejected not because the analysis says no, but because it feels *beneath us*. Five-billion-dollar companies don't take two-year-old startups seriously in 2000. That feeling is not analysis. That feeling is how every incumbent in episode one's textbook died.

**DANA:** Last argument, and it's the uncomfortable one. Eli's proven engine — the late fee — is not an asset, it's a *grievance with a billing system*. Sixteen percent of revenue extracted from our own customers' resentment; the most-hated line item in American retail, monetized. A profit stream that customers despise is a standing bounty: the market will pay whoever deletes it. These three men in the lobby are the first claimants of that bounty, and they've priced themselves at one percent of our revenue. Someone is going to collect it, John. The only question this room actually controls is whether the company that kills the late fee has our name on it — or theirs. <laugh> Fifty million dollars to own both sides of your own disruption. It's the cheapest insurance policy in the history of retail.

### THE REVEAL: WHAT BLOCKBUSTER DID (5 minutes)

[MUSIC STING]

**ELI:** Here's what actually happened. Antioco passed. By Randolph's account, the fifty-million-dollar number was met with barely suppressed laughter and a version of "is this a joke" — the Netflix team flew home with nothing, and the story entered legend from there. Netflix survived the crash on its own: layoffs and near-death in 2001, an IPO in 2002, first profits in 2003. You know the far end of the story from episode one — streaming in 2007, and today a company worth several hundred billion dollars. The fifty-million-dollar no is probably the most ridiculed decision in business history.

**DANA:** But — and this is why we saved this room for last — the ridicule gets the lesson *wrong*, and the true story is more useful. Because Blockbuster did not simply sleep until it died. Antioco saw the threat clearly, just late. In 2004 Blockbuster launched its own DVD-by-mail service, and in 2006 came Total Access: rent online, return in any store — using exactly the physical footprint Eli's case bragged about. And here's the part almost nobody remembers: *it worked.* Total Access grew so fast through early 2007 that it was out-adding Netflix, and Hastings was rattled enough that Netflix quietly explored buying Blockbuster's online subscriber base. The fast-follower play, seven years after this room, was genuinely winning. The disease Dana diagnosed was real, but it wasn't fatal yet.

**ELI:** What was fatal was the room *above* this room. Total Access won subscribers by being deliberately unprofitable — a war chest decision, exactly like Netflix's own losses. Carl Icahn had fought his way onto Blockbuster's board in 2005, hated the online spending, and in 2007 a compensation dispute pushed Antioco out entirely. His successor, Jim Keyes, came from 7-Eleven, looked at the P&L, and did the fiscally responsible thing: gutted Total Access's marketing, raised its prices, and redirected the company back toward the stores — the proven engine. Subscriber growth reversed almost immediately. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in September 2010 — the same year, from episode one, that Netflix's streaming bet was hitting escape velocity. The company got two clean shots at the future, and governance shot both of them.

**DANA:** Which is the honest, adult version of this story: the 2000 no was not the single fatal blow the legend claims. It was the first symptom of the pattern that *was* fatal — an organization that, every single time the future presented an invoice, chose the stores and the late fees instead. In 2000 the future cost fifty million and they laughed. In 2007 it cost a couple of unprofitable years of Total Access, and the board fired the man paying. Same reflex, escalating stakes. The no in this room didn't kill Blockbuster. The *reflex* did — it just introduced itself here first.

**ELI:** And my case — the smart money's no — deserves its epitaph, because I stand by every sentence of it and that's the horror. Price discipline, fiduciary caution, build-versus-buy logic, all impeccable, all pointing at the cliff. <sigh> Episode one, I told you every dead incumbent had someone smart making the sophisticated case for waiting. I just spent ten minutes being him, again, and it felt *great*.

### WHAT TRANSFERS — AND WHAT THE SIX ROOMS TAUGHT (5 minutes)

**DANA:** Alright. The last "what transfers" of the season. Four from this room, then the season in one breath.

**DANA:** One: price optionality against the size of the threat, not the size of the target. Fifty million looked absurd measured against Netflix's P&L — three hundred thousand subs, giant losses. Measured against what it insured — the mortality of Blockbuster's entire profit engine — it was one percent of revenue for a hedge on everything. When a small company attacks your core economics, its valuation is the least interesting number in the room. Ask what the *threat* is worth, not what the *target* is worth.

**ELI:** Two: when the disruptor needs you, you're not buying their P&L — you're buying time, and time is the one asset on nobody's balance sheet. Netflix in 2000 was two years of subscriber cohort data, licensing scar tissue, and logistics muscle, available at a desperation discount. Muscles, not features — the episode-one lesson, from the other side of the table. The build-it-ourselves case is almost always technically true and almost always four years late.

**DANA:** Three: the second chance dies in the boardroom, not the market. Blockbuster's strategy recovered — Total Access was beating Netflix at its own game. What couldn't recover was the coalition: a board, an activist, and an incentive fight chose the proven engine over the winning experiment, *while it was winning*. If you're driving a transformation, the market case is the entry fee; the governance case — who will defend the losses, for how long, against whom — is the actual game. Count your board votes before you count your subscribers.

**ELI:** And four — the bookend. Hold the two rooms side by side, because together they're the whole season. Los Gatos, 2007: a company votes to fund the thing that kills its golden goose, and lives. Dallas, 2000: a company votes to protect the goose, and dies holding it. Same industry, same decade, overlapping cast. The entire distance between those two fates is a single reflex — what the room does in the moment the future shows up looking small, unprofitable, and slightly ridiculous, asking to be taken seriously.

**DANA:** And that's the season. Six rooms. Netflix taught asymmetry — weigh which direction of wrong is fatal, not which is likely. Apple taught the replacement path — you may remove the beloved thing only if you're holding what comes next. Amazon taught behavior over the P&L — price the habit you're creating, not the quarter you're booking. Instagram taught subtraction — the users have usually already voted, and deleting is a strategy. Microsoft taught the strategy tax — find which moat is load-bearing, and stop your best product from subsidizing your most sentimental one. And Blockbuster taught optionality — the future keeps office hours, and it bills more every time you decline the meeting.

**ELI:** None of it is a framework, and that was the point. Judgment isn't owning the arguments — every room we visited had smart people holding *both* sides. Judgment is feeling which argument the moment actually belongs to. You only build that by standing in rooms and making calls with the ending hidden. We gave you six. <breath> Your calendar has the rest.

**DANA:** Find the decision your own company is currently laughing at. That's your room. Argue both sides like the ending is hidden — because it is. That's The Decision Room.

**ELI:** No hindsight allowed. And the room is yours now. See you out there.
