### The problem ALL4 solves

**NORA:** Let's talk about the customer's problem. Explain air permitting for someone who's never thought about it.

**JAMES:** In the US, as general background, the Clean Air Act gives the Environmental Protection Agency and the states the power to regulate air pollution from facilities. If you want to build or significantly change a facility that emits pollutants, a power plant, a refinery, a chemical plant, a factory, a data centre with large generators, you usually need a permit before construction. Then many larger facilities need an operating permit that sets out every limit, every monitoring requirement, every record they must keep and every report they must file.

**NORA:** And those permits are complicated.

**JAMES:** Hugely. A major facility's operating permit can run to hundreds of pages, with conditions drawn from federal rules, state rules and sometimes local rules, each with its own monitoring and reporting schedule. Miss a deadline or a calculation and you may have a violation. Violations can mean fines, enforcement and reputational damage.

**NORA:** How long has this regime been around?

**JAMES:** The modern Clean Air Act dates from 1970, with major amendments in 1990 that, among other things, created the federal operating permit programme for larger sources, usually called Title V. That's general background. The point is that it's a mature, layered system. Decades of federal rules, plus state implementation plans, plus local air districts, especially in places like California. Each layer adds requirements, and they don't always line up neatly.

**NORA:** Which is why you need specialists.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Knowing where to look, and how a regulator in a particular state tends to interpret a rule, is the kind of knowledge that sits in experienced people's heads. And that's both ALL4's value and, in a sense, its vulnerability, because AI is getting better at finding and reading rules.

**NORA:** So the client needs expertise they don't have in-house.

**JAMES:** Or don't have enough of. Many industrial companies have environmental, health and safety staff, usually shortened to EHS. But they're stretched thin, and permitting is specialised. So they bring in consultants. ALL4 describes itself as becoming "part of their environmental teams," either at a single site or at the corporate level.

**NORA:** And it's not only air.

**JAMES:** No. There's compliance support, environmental planning, programme support across health and safety, chemical management, environmental review for new projects in California and elsewhere, remediation of contaminated sites, emissions testing, wastewater and now acoustics. But air quality is the core identity.

**NORA:** Why is demand rising?

**JAMES:** Two drivers stand out. First, regulatory churn. ALL4 publishes a regular regulatory roundup. In October 2026 it covered California's climate disclosure law known as Senate Bill 253, new EPA designations for fine particulate pollution, called PM two point five, extended producer responsibility laws for packaging, a proposed EPA rule for gasoline distribution terminals, and California's changes to cancer-risk values for two chemicals, acrolein and ethylene oxide.

**NORA:** That's a lot in one month.

**JAMES:** It is, and every one of those generates work for someone: interpreting the rule, figuring out who it applies to, recalculating, reporting. Second, the build-out of data centres and power generation. That's why ALL4 made those sectors a principal focus.

**NORA:** Why do data centres need so much environmental work?

**JAMES:** Our inference on the permit type, based on general knowledge: large data centres often install many backup generators, sometimes dozens, to keep running if the grid fails. Each one is a source of emissions. Some data centres also build on-site power plants. Collectively that can make them significant sources that need air permits. And they're being built at enormous speed, so there's pressure to get permits quickly and right the first time.

The firms that win will be the ones that let software do the routine work and charge for judgement.

### A permit, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through an engagement. Invented, not real.

**JAMES:** Let's say a developer is building a data centre campus in the Southeast. Call the developer Bluewater. The campus will have forty backup diesel generators. Bluewater hires ALL4 to get the air permit and then help it stay compliant.

**NORA:** What happens first?

**JAMES:** A senior consultant, call him Raj, meets the client. He gathers the details: the generator models, their size, how often they'll be tested, the fuel, the site layout, the stack heights. Then a junior engineer, call her Emma, calculates the potential emissions of each pollutant from each unit, using manufacturer data and emission factors.

**NORA:** And then the modelling?

**JAMES:** If needed, a modeller runs a dispersion model to estimate concentrations at the property boundary and in the neighbourhood, and compares them with air quality standards. Meanwhile Raj works out which rules apply. Federal standards for those engines, the state's permitting rules, any local requirements, and whether the total emissions push the facility into a bigger permitting category.

**NORA:** And then they write it up.

**JAMES:** Emma drafts the application. It's a long document: a description of the project, emission calculations, the regulatory analysis, the modelling results, proposed permit conditions. Raj reviews it, sends comments, Emma revises. A principal does a final quality review. That drafting and review cycle might take weeks.

**NORA:** And then the regulator.

**JAMES:** The application goes to the state agency. The agency asks questions. ALL4 responds. Eventually a permit is issued, often with conditions: limits on testing hours, monitoring, record-keeping, reports.

**NORA:** And then compliance.

**JAMES:** Right. Now someone has to turn that permit into a list of obligations. Every condition, who's responsible, what records to keep, what reports are due and when. Then, every month, someone collects the generator run hours and fuel use, checks them against the limits and prepares the reports.

**NORA:** Where does AI fit?

**JAMES:** In several places. First, extracting the requirements from the permit into a structured compliance table, which is exactly what ehsAI was built for. Hours of careful reading become minutes of review. Second, drafting the narrative sections of the application from the engineering inputs and the firm's past applications, so Emma starts from a decent first draft. Third, checking the regulatory analysis against a database of rules, which is what tools like Encamp's Scout claim to do. And fourth, on the compliance side, flagging when run hours are trending toward a limit before it's exceeded.

**NORA:** What stays human?

**JAMES:** The judgement. Whether to accept a permit limit or negotiate it. How to design testing schedules to give the client flexibility. How to talk to the regulator. And the professional responsibility for what's submitted. An AI draft doesn't sign a permit application. A qualified person does.

**NORA:** And what changes for Bluewater?

**JAMES:** If done well, faster permits, fewer revisions and a cleaner compliance programme from day one. For a data centre developer under time pressure, speed to permit is worth a lot.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's talk challenges.

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, the billable-hour model. If AI makes the drafting and extraction work much faster, and ALL4 bills by the hour, revenue per project could fall. That's the classic professional services dilemma.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Integration. Three acquisitions in eight months across California, South Carolina and Florida, on top of five earlier ones. Each firm brings its own people, clients, culture and, often, its own systems: timekeeping, project management, document storage, templates. That's our inference, but it's typical. AI needs consistent data and templates, and integration comes first.

**NORA:** Is there a capacity challenge with the data centre boom?

**JAMES:** I'd think so, in our inference. If many developers want permits at the same time, the bottleneck is the number of experienced air permitting specialists, both at consultancies and at state agencies. A firm can't hire senior experts overnight. That's actually one of the strongest arguments for AI at ALL4: not to cut people, but to let the experts it has handle more projects without quality slipping.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Leadership is still forming. The Principal role was only created in August 2026. Founders still lead. That's not bad, but it means decision rights and succession are still being set. That's our inference.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Talent. Growth depends on a fairly small senior bench of technical experts who know the regulations deeply. They're hard to hire and easy to lose. And if junior work gets automated, there's a longer-term question about how the next generation of experts learns.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** The software squeeze. Partners like VelocityEHS and EcoOnline are friends today. But if they automate more and offer their own advisory services, they could take work from consultants. And flat-fee competitors like Vanguard put pressure on pricing.

**NORA:** And the people inside ALL4. How do engineers feel about AI drafting their work?

**JAMES:** We don't have any public evidence on that specifically, so this is general. In professional firms, there are usually three reactions. Some people are enthusiastic early adopters. Some are sceptical, often the most senior experts, who've seen tools over-promise and who know how subtle the regulations can be. And some are quietly worried about what it means for their jobs. Leaders have to address all three.

**NORA:** How?

**JAMES:** In my view, by starting with the tasks nobody enjoys, like transcribing permit conditions into spreadsheets, by letting the sceptical experts test the tools and find the errors, and by being honest about how efficiency gains will be used. If the message is "we'll take on more data centre work without burning everyone out," that's a very different story from "we'll need fewer of you."

**NORA:** That learning question is interesting. If Emma doesn't spend weeks drafting applications, how does she become Raj?

**JAMES:** It's a real issue across all professional services. My view is that firms will have to design training deliberately, with juniors reviewing and critiquing AI drafts, learning why a calculation is right, rather than learning purely by repetition. Done well, they might learn faster. Done badly, they'll learn less.

**NORA:** Let's do a second walk-through. A new regulation lands. Invented again.

**JAMES:** Say a pharmaceutical manufacturer in California, call it Coastline Pharma, uses ethylene oxide to sterilise medical products. California changes the cancer-risk value for ethylene oxide, which is one of the changes ALL4's October roundup mentioned. Coastline's environmental manager, call her Priya, emails ALL4: what does this mean for us?

**NORA:** And today, how does ALL4 answer?

**JAMES:** A consultant reads the new value and the agency guidance, pulls Coastline's existing risk assessment and permit, works out which calculations change, reruns the risk numbers, and writes a memo. Then multiply that by every client that uses ethylene oxide or acrolein. Each one gets a careful but largely manual analysis.

**NORA:** And with AI?

**JAMES:** The firm's tools would identify every client whose permits or inventories mention those chemicals within hours of the change. For each, AI pulls the relevant permit conditions and past assessments, and drafts a first impact summary: here's what changed, here's which of your calculations are affected, here's the rough direction of the result. The consultant checks the numbers, adds judgement about what the agency is likely to do next, and sends it.

**NORA:** So ALL4 calls Priya before she calls ALL4.

**JAMES:** Exactly. That's the shift from reactive to proactive. And it's a sales opportunity too. "Here's what the new rule means for you, and here's what we suggest" is a very good conversation starter for the next project.

**NORA:** What could go wrong?

**JAMES:** The AI could miss a client, misread an older permit, or get a calculation wrong. In a health-risk context, that's serious. So the human check isn't optional. And the client data has to be organised well enough to be searched in the first place, which brings us back to integration.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** Permit and regulatory requirement extraction, plus draft report generation, for the air quality and compliance practice. It's the core of the business, the work is document-heavy and repeatable, ALL4 has already chosen this direction, and the security certification gives it a foundation to handle client data.

**NORA:** How would you run the first hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: pick one practice area, say air permitting for generators and turbines, where the work is most repeatable. Choose a small team of volunteers across two or three offices, ideally including people from an acquired firm. Collect a library of past permits, applications and compliance tables, with client permission where needed. Agree the measures: hours to produce a compliance table, hours to first draft of an application, number of review comments, and error rates.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Run AI extraction and drafting on real projects in parallel with the normal process. Every output is reviewed by a qualified engineer. Track where the AI gets it wrong. Is it misreading a condition? Missing a cross-reference to a federal rule? That error log is gold.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Compare hours and quality. If the drafts are good and the hours fall, standardise templates across offices, including the acquired firms, and decide how to price. Do you keep billing hourly and accept lower revenue per project, offer fixed-fee permit packages, or bundle it into a compliance subscription? Make that commercial decision deliberately, based on evidence, rather than letting it happen by default.

**NORA:** What about the data itself? Can ALL4 use one client's permits to help another?

**JAMES:** That's a sensitive question, and I'd treat it carefully. Many permits are public records, filed with regulators, so in principle a library built from public permits is fair game. But a client's internal emissions data, process details and draft applications are confidential. So the safe design, in my view, is a library built from public documents and the firm's own templates, with each client's confidential data used only for that client's work, unless contracts explicitly say otherwise.

**NORA:** And governance?

**JAMES:** Write and publish an AI use policy alongside the Trust Center. Which tools are approved, how client data is handled, whether data is used to train models, and the rule that a qualified human reviews and signs everything. Clients in energy and pharma will ask, so answer the question before they do.

**NORA:** Who owns it?

**JAMES:** One person with both technical credibility and authority. The Digital Solutions practice is a natural home, paired with a principal from the air practice, but with one accountable owner.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** What can other businesses learn from ALL4?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, if software is coming for your work, partner with it before it competes with you. ALL4's two alliances are a bet that the expert layer on top of software is valuable. Whether that bet works depends on execution, but it's better than pretending the software doesn't exist.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Decide your pricing model before AI decides it for you. If AI cuts the hours, an hourly firm either gets more work done for the same revenue, or less revenue for the same work. Fixed fees and subscriptions turn efficiency into margin. That's a choice to make deliberately.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Security first, then AI. The ISO certification came before the AI push. In industries with sensitive data, that's the right order.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Integrate acquisitions into one way of working, or AI won't scale. Templates, document libraries, timekeeping, project codes. A roll-up of eight consulting firms with eight ways of writing a permit application can't use one AI drafting tool well.

**NORA:** Anything about the data centre focus that generalises?

**JAMES:** Yes. Follow the build-out. When a whole industry is building at speed, it creates repeatable, urgent work, and repeatable work is exactly where templates and AI tools pay off first. ALL4 created principals around data centres and power. Other services firms should ask where their clients are building fastest, and design their AI around that work.

**NORA:** And I'd add a fifth: train the next generation on purpose.

**JAMES:** Agreed. If AI does the first draft, make sure juniors learn to judge drafts, not just to accept them.

**NORA:** One more lesson. What about being early?

**JAMES:** Being early on an idea isn't the same as being early on adoption. ALL4 announced its ehsAI partnership in 2021, well ahead of the generative AI wave. That's foresight. But five years later, the public evidence of AI in everyday delivery is still thin. The lesson, in my view, is that the idea is the easy part. The hard part is getting it into the daily work of hundreds of engineers across many offices. Any company that announced an AI partnership a few years ago should ask honestly how much it changed the work.

**NORA:** That's a fair challenge to a lot of companies.

**JAMES:** It is. And it's not a criticism of ALL4 specifically. We simply can't see inside. It's a question every firm should ask itself.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI disruption to billable hours. Tools like Encamp's and Benchmark Gensuite's automate duty-tracking and data checks that consultants bill for today. Two, software vendors moving into services and taking share. Three, price pressure from flat-fee rivals. Four, regulatory rollback reducing demand. And five, key-person and talent risk in a small senior bench. Most of those are our inferences rather than reported events.

**NORA:** Let me push back on the first one. Hasn't consulting survived every wave of software so far?

**JAMES:** It has, and that's a fair challenge. Spreadsheets, databases, EHS software in the 2000s and 2010s. Each time, the routine work shrank and the advisory work grew. Regulation got more complex, which created more work. I think that pattern probably continues. But generative AI is different in one way: it reads and writes text, which is what much of consulting delivers. So the routine slice it can take is bigger than before. The firms that adapt will be fine. The firms that keep billing for transcription won't.

**NORA:** And for the owners?

**JAMES:** Integration risk. Eight acquisitions in about six years, three in 2026. The value of a buy-and-build comes from the combined platform being worth more than the parts. That only happens if the parts actually work together.

**NORA:** And when might the owners want to sell?

**JAMES:** We don't know, and nothing has been announced. Private equity holds often run four to seven years, as general background, and this one is under two years old. So the next few years are about building the platform. But a future buyer will look closely at two things, in my view: how much revenue is recurring rather than project-based, and whether the firm has a credible answer to AI. Both are worth working on now.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any evidence of AI inside ALL4's own delivery, like an extraction or drafting tool rolled out across the firm. Second, whether the VelocityEHS and EcoOnline alliances produce named client implementations. Third, any shift toward fixed-fee or subscription compliance offerings. And fourth, more acquisitions, and whether they follow the regional air quality pattern.
