### What mediation and arbitration are

**NORA:** Let's do the basics properly, because I suspect a lot of listeners have heard these words and couldn't tell you the difference.

**JAMES:** Sure. Both are ways of resolving disputes outside a public courtroom. In mediation, a neutral helps the parties negotiate a settlement. The mediator has no power to decide. They facilitate, test arguments, carry offers back and forth, and try to find a deal both sides can accept. If there's no deal, the case goes back to the court.

**NORA:** And arbitration?

**JAMES:** In arbitration, the neutral acts more like a private judge. The parties present evidence and arguments, and the arbitrator makes a decision, often binding. It's usually faster and more private than a trial, and the parties can choose an arbitrator with relevant expertise.

**NORA:** Why would you choose a retired judge for mediation, if they can't decide anything?

**JAMES:** Because of credibility. When a retired judge tells a party, "I've seen a hundred juries, and I think you'll lose this argument," that carries weight. A good mediator helps each side see the risks in its own case. Having been a judge makes that reality check more persuasive.

**NORA:** Can the two be combined? Mediate first, and arbitrate if it fails?

**JAMES:** Yes, as general background there are hybrid processes, sometimes called med-arb, where the parties try mediation and, if it fails, move to arbitration, sometimes with the same neutral and sometimes with a different one. There are trade-offs. Parties may be less candid with a mediator who might later decide their case. But hybrids show how flexible private dispute resolution can be. The parties design the process, rather than accepting the court's.

**NORA:** And that flexibility is part of what they're paying for.

**JAMES:** Very much. Choice of neutral, choice of timing, choice of process, and privacy. A public court can't offer most of those.

**NORA:** And who pays?

**JAMES:** Typically the parties split the neutral's fee, often a day rate or an hourly rate, plus administrative fees to the provider. We didn't find Signature's fee schedule, so I won't quote a number. But as general background, well-known mediators in major markets can command substantial day rates.

**NORA:** What kinds of disputes go to mediation?

**JAMES:** Almost every kind of civil case. From what we can infer from Signature's neutrals' backgrounds and its press, that includes commercial disputes, employment cases, entertainment, healthcare and personal injury. Los Angeles, with its entertainment industry and huge court system, is a natural home for all of those.

**NORA:** And is it voluntary?

**JAMES:** Sometimes. Parties often agree to mediate because they want to settle. Courts often encourage or order it. And many contracts, especially employment and consumer contracts, require arbitration of disputes. That last area is politically contested in the United States, and I won't go into it here, but it's a big part of the arbitration market.

**NORA:** Is California special in any way for this business?

**JAMES:** In a few ways, as general background. It's the largest state economy in the country, with a very large civil court system and a lot of employment, entertainment, technology and real estate litigation. It also has a long tradition of private dispute resolution, including mechanisms where parties can, in certain circumstances, use a privately paid retired judge to hear parts of a case. I'll stay cautious on the legal details, but the point is that California lawyers are very used to the idea of paying a retired judge to help move a case along.

**NORA:** So Signature grew up in a market that already understood the product.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Which is an advantage at home, and a challenge when it expands. Other states have their own traditions and their own established providers. What works in Los Angeles won't automatically work in New York.

### Why private dispute resolution keeps growing

**NORA:** Why is this business growing? What's driving demand?

**JAMES:** I'll be upfront that we didn't find sourced figures for these drivers, so this is our reading of the market. First, court backlogs. Public courts, especially in a big state like California, can take a long time to get a civil case to trial. Private mediation and arbitration let parties move faster.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, cost and certainty. Trials are expensive and unpredictable. A day of mediation that settles the case can save a great deal. Third, privacy. Arbitration and mediation are generally confidential, which matters to companies and individuals who don't want their dispute in the public record.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Fourth, supply. Every year, experienced judges and senior attorneys retire and look for a second career. They're the raw material of this industry. And fifth, technology. Since the pandemic, virtual and hybrid sessions have become normal. A mediator in Los Angeles can now handle a case with parties in three different states without anyone getting on a plane.

**NORA:** That last one cuts both ways, doesn't it?

**JAMES:** It does. Virtual sessions widen the market for every provider. A New York firm can hire a Los Angeles mediator, and the other way round. That's good for Signature's expansion, but it also means more competition, because local presence matters less than it used to.

**NORA:** Is there a market size figure?

**JAMES:** We didn't find a reliable one. There's an industry research page on American mediation services, but we didn't see the number, so we won't quote anything.

### A mediation day, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through a mediation day. An invented case, not a real one.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's say a mid-sized manufacturer in the Inland Empire, call it Desert Steel, is being sued by a former sales director, call her Angela, who says she was fired in retaliation for complaining about unpaid commissions. The case has been in the courts for eighteen months. Both lawyers agree to mediate, and they choose a retired judge on Signature's panel with a lot of employment experience.

**NORA:** How do they choose?

**JAMES:** By name and reputation, mostly. Angela's lawyer has used this judge before and thinks she's fair and persuasive. Desert Steel's lawyer checks with colleagues and agrees. Then the case manager at the provider checks calendars, conflicts and logistics, and books a date, maybe six weeks out.

**NORA:** What happens before the day?

**JAMES:** Each side sends the mediator a mediation brief. That's a written summary of the case, the key evidence, the law and their view of the value. Briefs can be anywhere from ten pages to a hundred, often with attachments: emails, commission spreadsheets, performance reviews, deposition excerpts. The mediator reads all of it, usually the evening or weekend before.

**NORA:** That's a lot of reading.

**JAMES:** It's one of the hidden costs of the job, and a theme we'll come back to. On the day, the parties may meet together briefly, then split into separate rooms, physical or virtual. The mediator spends the day moving between them. In the morning, she listens to each side. Midday, she starts testing. To Desert Steel: "Your timing is bad. She complained in March, and you fired her in April. A jury will notice." To Angela: "Your commission claim is strong, but your emotional distress claim is thin, and the company has documentation of performance issues."

**NORA:** And numbers start moving.

**JAMES:** Slowly. Angela's side opens high, the company opens low. The mediator carries offers back and forth. By late afternoon, the gap has narrowed. Maybe the mediator proposes a number herself, a mediator's proposal, which each side can accept or reject confidentially. At seven in the evening, both accept. The lawyers draft a short settlement term sheet, everyone signs, and the case is effectively over.

**NORA:** Where's the time going for the provider in that story?

**JAMES:** Three places. The neutral's prep time, reading briefs. The neutral's day itself, which is the core of what's billed. And the case management around it: intake, conflict checks, scheduling, invoicing, document handling, setting up the virtual rooms. The neutral's judgment in the room is the irreplaceable part. A lot of the rest is administration.

### The business model

**NORA:** Let's talk about how a firm like this makes money.

**JAMES:** We didn't find Signature's financials, so this is about the general model. The neutrals are often affiliated rather than employed, meaning they're independent professionals who work through the provider. The provider markets them, books their calendars, handles the administration, provides the rooms and the technology, and takes a share of the fees.

**NORA:** So it's a bit like a talent agency.

**JAMES:** That's a good analogy. The provider's value is the brand, the booking engine and the support. The neutral's value is their reputation and skill. And just like in a talent agency, the top names have a lot of bargaining power. If a star mediator leaves for a competitor, many of her repeat clients may follow.

**NORA:** What does growth look like then?

**JAMES:** A few ways. More neutrals, especially in-demand ones. More days booked per neutral, which is utilisation. More cases from more law firms. New geographies. And potentially higher-value services, like complex arbitrations. On the cost side, the main controllable cost is the administrative operation.

**NORA:** Who are the customers, really? The parties or the lawyers?

**JAMES:** Formally the parties pay, but in practice the lawyers choose. And that's a crucial distinction. A provider markets to the litigators who bring cases, the plaintiff firms, the defence firms, the in-house legal teams and, in many kinds of cases, the insurers who often fund the defence and the settlement. Those are repeat buyers. A busy employment defence firm or an insurer's claims team might send dozens of cases a year to mediation.

**NORA:** So winning a firm's habit is worth a lot.

**JAMES:** Enormously. Our inference is that repeat use by the same law firms is typical in this industry. Once a firm trusts a provider's neutrals and finds the booking easy, it tends to come back. That's also why service quality, the case managers and the portal, matters more than you might expect for a business that looks like it's all about the judges.

**NORA:** And technology?

**JAMES:** Signature has a client portal, which it calls SR Global, where users log in. And it offers virtual mediation services. Those are standard digital tools in the industry today rather than AI, but they're the base on which AI could be built.

**NORA:** You mentioned the private equity firm owns Resolution Economics too. Is there a connection?

**JAMES:** They're both in the disputes world, but in different jobs. ResEcon provides the economic analysis and testimony in a dispute. Signature provides the neutral who helps resolve it. They're not competitors. In our view there could be some referral or cross-selling potential, but we haven't seen any evidence of that happening, so that's purely our speculation.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** Who does Signature compete with?

**JAMES:** The best-known name in American private dispute resolution is JAMS, which is a large national provider with a big Los Angeles presence. We didn't verify its scale or ownership in detail. What we do know is that in 2026 JAMS launched what it calls an ADR Technology Industry Group, and it has published AI-specific rules and tools.

**NORA:** AI rules for arbitration?

**JAMES:** Yes. As we understand it, these cover things like how disputes involving AI systems should be handled, and how AI may be used in the process. It's a sign that the big players are positioning themselves as the place to go for technology disputes and for technology-enabled dispute resolution.

**NORA:** Does that create work for providers, or just obligations?

**JAMES:** Both, in my view. Rules about AI mean parties may need to disclose whether they used AI to prepare submissions, and neutrals may need to disclose whether they use AI tools. Someone has to manage those disclosures and keep records. A provider that builds clean, simple workflows for that, rather than leaving it to every neutral to improvise, makes life easier for lawyers. That's an inference about where the market is heading, not something Signature has announced.

**NORA:** Who else?

**JAMES:** Judicate West, a Southern California private provider, overlaps directly with Signature in Los Angeles and San Diego. Miles Mediation and Arbitration is another private provider. And the American Arbitration Association and its international arm, the AAA-ICDR, is a nonprofit administrator with long institutional credibility. It has published guidance on AI use cases in dispute resolution and offers an AI tool to help with drafting arbitration agreements.

**NORA:** So the established competition is already talking about AI.

**JAMES:** It is, and that's important for Signature. When the industry leader and the main nonprofit institution are both visibly moving on AI, a smaller provider that isn't moving risks looking old-fashioned to the law firms that choose providers.

**NORA:** Now the newer challengers.

**JAMES:** Two stand out. Dyspute.ai launched what it calls ADRI V2 in January 2026, described as a twenty-four seven asynchronous AI mediation platform. Asynchronous means the parties don't have to be in the same session at the same time. They submit positions, the platform helps structure the negotiation, and it runs continuously. And ADR Chambers launched something called aiADR, which it describes as a faster, lower-cost arbitration platform for small-dollar disputes.

**NORA:** Is that a threat to a panel of retired judges?

**JAMES:** Not directly, at least not yet. A two-year commercial fight with millions at stake is not going to be settled by a chatbot. The parties want a respected human in the room. But at the bottom of the market, small claims, consumer disputes, simple contract disagreements, AI-native platforms could become the default because they're cheap and fast.

**NORA:** And the risk is that the bottom of the market creeps upward.

**JAMES:** Exactly. In my view the pattern in many industries is that AI-native tools start with the simple cases and gradually move up as they get better. If a growing share of mid-sized disputes find it good enough to resolve online with AI help, the pool of cases that need a premium human neutral could shrink. That's a long-run risk, not an immediate one.

**NORA:** Is there consolidation going on as well?

**JAMES:** We see it in Signature's own story. Private equity backing, rapid expansion through tie-ups. Our reading is that the industry is moving from many local boutiques toward a smaller number of multi-state platforms. That's an inference from the pattern, not something we've seen quantified.

**NORA:** Tell me more about how an asynchronous AI mediation actually works. I'm struggling to picture it.

**JAMES:** We haven't used Dyspute.ai's platform, so I'll describe the general idea rather than its specific features. Imagine a dispute between a small business and a supplier over a late delivery. Instead of booking a day with a mediator, each side logs in when it suits them. They describe their position, upload documents and state what they'd accept. The AI helps each side structure its arguments, summarises the other side's position, points out where they're close, and suggests possible compromises. The parties exchange offers over days rather than hours. A human may step in at the end, or not at all.

**NORA:** That sounds like what online marketplaces have done for years with buyer and seller disputes.

**JAMES:** It's a fair comparison, as general background. Online dispute resolution for e-commerce has existed for a long time, mostly for small, simple claims. What's new is that large language models can handle much messier, text-heavy disputes, and can explain positions in plain language. That widens the range of cases where an online, mostly automated process is plausible.

**NORA:** And the selling point is price.

**JAMES:** Price, speed and convenience. No travel, no scheduling, no day rate. For a dispute worth a few thousand dollars, paying a retired judge for a full day doesn't make economic sense, so these platforms are serving cases that may never have reached a private mediator anyway. The question is where the line settles over the next five years.

### Where Signature is with AI

**NORA:** Let's be direct. Where is Signature Resolution with AI?

**JAMES:** As far as public evidence goes, at the very beginning. We found no AI product, no AI partnership and no AI hire. A search for Signature Resolution and AI in 2026 turned up nothing relevant. The digital assets we found are the SR Global portal and virtual mediation, which are useful but aren't AI.

**NORA:** Is that a problem?

**JAMES:** Not in itself. It's a ten-month-old private equity investment in a relationship business. Many firms like this haven't publicly adopted AI yet. But it does mean the AI story is almost entirely ahead of it, and the competitive context, with JAMS and the AAA moving and AI-native entrants arriving, suggests it shouldn't wait too long.

**NORA:** What about governance?

**JAMES:** We didn't find published security certifications or an AI policy. For a dispute resolution provider, confidentiality is everything. Parties share their most sensitive documents and their real bottom lines with the mediator, on the understanding that it goes no further. Any AI that touches those documents must keep them strictly confidential, must not be used to train models that others benefit from, and must be explainable to neutrals and parties.

**NORA:** And would neutrals even want AI?

**JAMES:** That's the human question. Many retired judges are not early adopters of technology. Some will be enthusiastic, especially if AI saves them reading time. Others will be sceptical or worried about confidentiality. Since the neutrals are independent and in high demand, the firm can't simply mandate a tool. It has to make the tool so obviously useful that they want it.

**NORA:** Play the sceptic for me. What would a retired judge on the panel say about AI?

**JAMES:** I'd imagine something like this. "Reading the briefs is my job. That's how I understand the case. If I rely on a summary, I'll miss the detail that turns out to matter." And that's a fair concern. The answer isn't to replace reading. It's to make reading faster and more focused. A summary that points to the key pages lets the judge spend time on the parts that matter instead of searching through exhibits.

**NORA:** And the confidentiality worry?

**JAMES:** That would be the second objection, and it's the more serious one. "I promised these parties confidentiality. Where are their documents going?" The provider must be able to answer that precisely. Which systems, which locations, who can see the data, how long it's kept, and a guarantee that it isn't used to train anyone else's model. If the provider can't answer that clearly, the judge is right to refuse.

**NORA:** So adoption is as much about persuasion as about software.

**JAMES:** Absolutely. And the persuasion will work best through respected neutrals telling their peers, "I tried this, it saved me four hours on a brief, and it got the key facts right."

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's list the challenges. What keeps leadership up at night, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, dependence on individual neutrals. The business is built on the reputations of specific people. If a few of the busiest neutrals leave or retire, bookings fall.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, integration at speed. In under a year, it has added East Coast, New York and national tie-ups, on top of new California offices. Each new region brings different neutrals, different law firm relationships and possibly different systems. Making that feel like one firm to clients is hard.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, a lack of visible data. Revenue, margins and neutral utilisation aren't public. That's normal for a private company, but internally the firm will need a very clear view of utilisation by neutral, by region and by case type, to manage the expansion well.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, technology catching up. JAMS has an industry technology group and AI rules. AI-native entrants are launching platforms. Signature has a portal and virtual sessions. Closing that gap without losing the personal, high-touch feel that clients pay for is a balancing act.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, pricing. If AI platforms make simple disputes much cheaper to resolve, the market will expect price tiers. Signature will need to decide whether to compete at the low end, for example with a virtual-only or small-dispute tier, or deliberately stay premium.

**NORA:** Is there a brand challenge, moving beyond California?

**JAMES:** I think so. In Los Angeles, a firm founded by four respected Superior Court judges has instant recognition among litigators. In New York or Boston, those names mean less. The firm has to build its reputation through the neutrals it adds in each market. That's why the tie-ups matter. They bring local reputation with them.

**NORA:** What about the founders themselves? Four retired judges.

**JAMES:** That's a sensitive point, but a real one for any firm built on senior founders. Retired judges are, by definition, later in their careers. At some point, each founder will want to reduce their caseload. The firm needs a pipeline of new neutrals, and a brand that stands on its own beyond the founding names. The 2025 and 2026 panel additions suggest it's working on exactly that, which is the right instinct. That's our view, not something the firm has said.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's flip to opportunities. If you were on the board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** First, AI summarisation of case files and briefs for neutrals. Every neutral spends hours reading before each session. A secure tool that produces a structured summary of each side's brief, the key facts, the disputed points, the numbers, the relevant documents, could save hours per matter. That's either more capacity or a better life for the neutrals, and both are valuable.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, automating intake, conflict checks and scheduling. Case managers spend a lot of time on these tasks, and the national expansion increases the volume. Faster intake means a quicker time to the first session, which clients notice.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, pricing and packaging. Tiered fee schedules for small-dollar or virtual-only matters, to compete with the AI-native challengers' price points without discounting the premium service. That's our inference about what could work.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, geographic cross-selling. Bring East Coast and New York neutrals into California matters, and California neutrals into East Coast matters, especially for cases that span both. Virtual sessions make that easy.

**NORA:** And acquisitions?

**JAMES:** Fifth, more add-ons of regional practices, following the pattern of early 2026. And sixth, what I'd call a neutral capacity marketplace. Use the firm's data on neutrals' expertise, track record and availability to match them to incoming cases, and to fill empty calendar days. That raises utilisation, which is the core economics of the business.

**NORA:** Is there a data opportunity, like we saw with BigHand?

**JAMES:** Possibly, but it has to be handled with great care. Over time, a provider sees a lot of patterns. How long different kinds of disputes take to settle, how often first sessions succeed, which types of cases need a second day. With strict anonymisation and aggregation, that kind of insight could help the firm schedule better, set expectations with clients, and price more accurately. I'd stop well short of anything that revealed one party's settlement figures to anyone. In this business, confidentiality is non-negotiable.

**NORA:** What about AI as a product for clients? An AI mediation platform of its own?

**JAMES:** I'd be cautious. Building an AI mediation platform puts Signature in direct competition with venture-funded specialists. In my view the better play is to use AI to make its human neutrals more effective, and perhaps partner with a technology provider for the low end, rather than trying to become a software company.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** One AI bet for the next eighteen months. What is it?

**JAMES:** Case-file and brief summarisation for neutrals, plus intake and scheduling automation. The bottleneck is neutral time per matter, and the firm sells neutral time, so each hour saved is margin or capacity. It's low risk, because the neutral still reads what matters and makes every judgment. And it scales with the new national panel.

**NORA:** How would you run it? Give me a hundred days.

**JAMES:** First thirty days: listen and set the rules. Talk to a dozen neutrals across regions about how they prepare, where the time goes, and what worries them about AI. In parallel, agree strict data rules: client documents stay confidential, are never used to train shared models, are deleted on a set schedule, and are processed only in approved, secure environments. Publish those rules to neutrals and to clients.

**NORA:** That's governance before technology.

**JAMES:** In this business, it has to be. One confidentiality slip could do far more damage than any time savings could make up for.

**NORA:** Then?

**JAMES:** Days thirty to sixty: pilot with volunteers. Pick five to eight neutrals who are curious, across different case types. Give them a secure summarisation tool for their incoming briefs. Ask them to compare the summary with their own reading. What did it get right? What did it miss? Did it save time? At the same time, look at intake: map the steps case managers follow, and automate the most repetitive, like conflict-check searches and calendar matching.

**NORA:** And the last stretch?

**JAMES:** Days sixty to a hundred: measure and decide. Prep hours per matter before and after. Time from first enquiry to first session. Neutral satisfaction. If the numbers are good, widen the rollout and let the pilot neutrals present their experience to their peers. Then set a target for the next six months, for example a share of matters with AI-assisted prep.

**NORA:** Let's replay the Desert Steel case with those tools in place. What changes?

**JAMES:** Start with intake. Angela's lawyer submits a request through the portal. The system pulls the party names and checks them automatically against the firm's records for conflicts, flagging anything for a case manager to review. It suggests three employment-focused neutrals with open dates in the next six weeks, based on the case type and the lawyers' past preferences. The case manager confirms, and the booking that used to take several emails and calls takes a few minutes.

**NORA:** And the briefs?

**JAMES:** Each side uploads its brief and exhibits. Overnight, the secure tool produces a structured summary for the mediator. The timeline of events: hired, complaint in March, terminated in April. The claims and defences. The money each side is asking for or offering, as stated. The key documents, with page references. And the points where the two briefs disagree on facts. The mediator still reads the briefs, but she starts from a map instead of a blank page, and she finishes her prep in perhaps half the time.

**NORA:** Does anything change in the room?

**JAMES:** Very little, and that's the point. She still walks between rooms, still tests arguments, still delivers bad news. Maybe she has the timeline on her tablet so she can quickly check a date when one side makes a claim. The human judgment, the empathy and the persuasion are untouched.

**NORA:** And afterwards?

**JAMES:** The settlement term sheet can be drafted faster from a template, with the agreed figures filled in, for the lawyers to check and sign. Invoicing goes out automatically. And with permission and anonymisation, the firm learns something about how long employment mediations of this type take, which helps it schedule and price future cases.

**NORA:** And who owns it?

**JAMES:** An operations leader with authority over case management and technology, paired with a respected neutral as the champion. The neutral's endorsement will do more for adoption than any memo.

**NORA:** And how would you think about pricing it?

**JAMES:** Initially I wouldn't charge for it. Use it to win neutrals and to improve client experience. The return comes through capacity and utilisation. Later, a premium service, for example a structured case analysis delivered to the neutral and optionally to counsel, could be priced separately.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Signature Resolution?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, when your product is a person, AI should serve the person. Signature sells the judgment and credibility of its neutrals. The right AI strategy makes them more effective, rather than trying to replace them.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, governance first when trust is the product. In confidential businesses, legal, medical, financial, the first AI announcement should be about how data is protected, not about features.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, watch the bottom of your market. AI-native challengers usually start with the simple, low-value work that incumbents don't care much about. That's exactly where to pay attention, because that's where they learn and grow.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, in a roll-up, systems matter as much as signings. Adding new offices and neutrals is the visible part of growth. Behind it, intake, scheduling, conflicts, billing and data need to work as one system, or the expansion creates chaos for clients. AI can help, but only on top of clean processes.

**NORA:** I'll add one. The best time to change habits is right after a big change of ownership.

**JAMES:** That's true. The first year after a deal is when people expect change. It's the natural window to introduce new tools and new ways of working, and Signature is in that window now.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-native asynchronous platforms compressing fees on simpler matters. Two, larger providers like JAMS outspending on technology and brand. Three, commoditisation of virtual mediation, which could lower the premium on in-person sessions. Four, neutral churn, through retirement or departures. Five, integration risk from very rapid expansion.

**NORA:** And the AI-specific risks?

**JAMES:** Those are worth separating out. The first is that an AI summary gets something wrong. If a tool misstates a key fact in a brief and a neutral relies on it, that could embarrass the neutral and damage trust. So the tool must point back to the source pages, and neutrals must treat it as a guide, not a substitute for reading what matters. The second is confidentiality. Any data leak from an AI tool would be very serious in a business built on discretion.

**NORA:** And is there a third?

**JAMES:** A longer-term shift in value. If case preparation and drafting become heavily automated, the value of each matter concentrates in the neutral's judgment, and pricing should reflect that.

**NORA:** Meaning what, in practice?

**JAMES:** Meaning that today a neutral's fee implicitly pays for reading time, preparation and the day itself. If AI makes preparation much faster, clients may start to question paying for the same hours. The firms that do well will, in my view, price around the outcome and the expertise, a day with a highly respected neutral, rather than around the hours of reading behind it. That's a subtle change, but it protects the value of the human part as the administrative part gets cheaper.

**NORA:** And are there ethical questions for mediators using AI?

**JAMES:** Some, yes. Parties may want to know whether a neutral used AI to prepare, and the rules from bodies like JAMS and the AAA are starting to address that. A provider that sets a clear, simple disclosure standard for its own panel, rather than waiting, would look professional and trustworthy. Again, that's our suggestion, not anything the firm has announced.

**NORA:** And for the owner?

**JAMES:** It's early. The owner will be focused on proving that the national expansion works: that new regions book well and that the platform can absorb more neutrals without service slipping. AI can support that, especially on the administrative side.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch for?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any detail on whether the East Coast and Commonwealth moves were acquisitions or partnerships, and how many neutrals they added. Second, the first AI announcement from Signature, and whether it leads with governance or with features. Third, any new price tier for virtual or small-dollar matters. And fourth, further regional add-ons, which would confirm the roll-up strategy.

**NORA:** And a bonus?

**JAMES:** How the big institutions' AI rules evolve. If JAMS and the AAA set the norms for AI in dispute resolution, every smaller provider will have to follow them.
