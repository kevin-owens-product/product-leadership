### The problem Expert Institute solves

**NORA:** Let's go back to the lawyer in the cold open. Why are expert witnesses so important?

**JAMES:** Because a lot of lawsuits turn on questions that a judge or jury can't answer without help. Did the surgeon meet the standard of care? Was the bridge designed safely? Is this software a copy of that software? How much money did the company really lose? In American courts, specialists who are qualified as experts can give opinions on those questions. Ordinary witnesses mostly can't.

**NORA:** So the expert is often the case.

**JAMES:** In many case types, very nearly. In medical malpractice, for example, you generally need a qualified medical expert to say the care was negligent. Without that, a case may not get far. And the other side will have its own expert saying the opposite.

**NORA:** What makes finding one hard?

**JAMES:** Several things at once. First, specificity. You don't want "a doctor", you want a vascular surgeon with experience in a particular procedure, ideally in active practice, ideally with no conflicts with the other parties. Second, credibility. The other side will dig into everything your expert has ever said or written. Third, admissibility. Judges act as gatekeepers on expert testimony, and an expert whose methods were rejected in another case can become a liability. And fourth, availability. The best specialists are busy, and in some fields demand from mass litigation soaks up supply.

**NORA:** That third one sounds like the scary one.

**JAMES:** It is. Imagine building your whole case around an expert, and then discovering in a deposition that a court in another state excluded her testimony two years ago, and opposing counsel found it before you did. That's the nightmare Expert Institute's vetting product is designed to prevent.

**NORA:** And the medical records side?

**JAMES:** Different problem, same customer. In a personal injury or malpractice case, the medical records are the raw material. They can run to thousands of pages: admission notes, nursing charts, lab results, imaging reports, medication logs, discharge summaries, often from several providers, often handwritten in places, often out of order. Before anyone can form an opinion, someone has to turn that into a chronology, a clean timeline of what happened when.

**NORA:** And traditionally that's done by whom?

**JAMES:** By paralegals, nurse consultants or physicians, depending on the firm and the complexity. It's slow, skilled work, and it's expensive. Expert Institute offers medical chronologies as a service. Its own page describes using natural language processing, which is the branch of AI that reads text, together with physician review. It quotes pricing per page and turnaround of "a few days".

**NORA:** So the customer problem, if I sum it up, is two things. Find me the right human to testify, and make sense of a mountain of documents.

**JAMES:** That's it. And the two feed each other, because the expert usually needs the chronology to form an opinion. Selling both as one workflow is a big part of the pitch.

### Inside the product

**NORA:** Walk me through the products. What does a customer actually see?

**JAMES:** The umbrella is something the company calls Expert iQ. It bundles expert search, expert vetting, records services and chronologies into one workflow rather than a set of separate point tools. On the homepage the AI features are described in three short phrases: research and investigate experts with AI, evaluate cases for merit, and summarise medical records with AI.

**NORA:** Let's take them one at a time. Expert search first.

**JAMES:** Expert search is the original business. A firm describes what it needs, and Expert Institute's search team, which Stephanie Santiago leads, finds candidates from its network or recruits new ones. The network of more than a million experts is the company's figure. The value is speed and fit. A firm that would otherwise spend days calling around gets a shortlist of qualified people who are willing to take the case.

**NORA:** And then vetting. That's Expert Radar?

**JAMES:** Yes. Expert Radar is the AI-driven tool for researching experts, including the other side's experts. In July 2024 the company announced enhancements: broader sourcing of information, automated collection, around-the-clock monitoring and document parsing. In the Lawdragon profile Talve talked about an AI and machine learning model that reads court documents. From the way he described it in 2024, as having launched about a year and a half earlier, our guess is that Expert Radar first appeared around early 2023.

**NORA:** What does monitoring mean in practice?

**JAMES:** Think of it as a watch list. Once you've retained an expert, or you know who the opposing expert is, the system keeps looking for new information. A new ruling that mentions them, a challenge to their testimony, a dismissal, a publication. So you're not relying on a one-time background check done months before trial.

**NORA:** That's genuinely useful.

**JAMES:** It is, and it's also a nice example of AI doing something that humans are bad at, which is reading everything, all the time, without getting bored. I should add one caution. The company's marketing includes some big claims, for example linking Expert Radar to a particular large verdict. We couldn't verify those independently, so we're not going to repeat them as fact.

**NORA:** Then the case-merit piece.

**JAMES:** "Evaluate cases for merit" is a short line on the site, and we didn't find much detail behind it. The broad idea, in our reading, is helping a firm decide early whether a case is worth taking, often with input from an expert reviewing the facts. For a contingency-fee plaintiff firm, which only gets paid if it wins, that early screening decision is very valuable.

**NORA:** Explain contingency fee for anyone who hasn't come across it.

**JAMES:** Sure. In a lot of American personal injury work, the client doesn't pay the lawyer by the hour. The lawyer takes a share of whatever is recovered, if anything is recovered. So the firm is effectively investing its own time and money in every case. Every expense, including experts and record reviews, is a bet. That makes these firms very sensitive to both the price and the speed of the services they buy.

**NORA:** And the medical chronologies.

**JAMES:** The chronology product uses natural language processing to read and organise the records, with physicians reviewing the output. Priced per page, turnaround of a few days, according to the company's page. The physician review is the quality claim. A purely automated summary might miss something, or misread a note. Having a doctor check it is a reason a firm might pay more.

**NORA:** Hold that thought, because I suspect it's the whole episode.

**JAMES:** It might be.

### A case, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make this concrete. Give me an invented case, start to finish. Not a real firm, not a real client.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's say there's a small plaintiff firm in Ohio, five lawyers, mostly personal injury and some medical malpractice. One of the partners, call him Marcus, gets a call from a family. Their father went in for a hip replacement, developed an infection after surgery, and it wasn't caught for several days. He's now got serious complications.

**NORA:** And Marcus's first question?

**JAMES:** Is this a case? Because if he takes it on contingency, he might spend tens of thousands of dollars of the firm's money on records, experts and depositions before seeing a cent. So he needs to know, early, whether the care was actually below the standard, or whether this was a known risk that happened despite good care.

**NORA:** So step one is the records.

**JAMES:** Step one is the records. The family signs releases, the hospital and the follow-up providers send them over, and Marcus is looking at, let's say, two and a half thousand pages. Nursing notes every few hours, temperature charts, white blood cell counts, antibiotic orders, physician progress notes.

**NORA:** And in the old world his paralegal reads all of that.

**JAMES:** In the old world, his paralegal spends a week or two on it, or he pays a nurse consultant. In the Expert Institute world, he uploads the records, the system organises them and pulls out the key events, a physician reviews the result, and a few days later he gets a chronology. Day one, surgery. Day two evening, temperature starts rising. Day three, nurse notes redness at the wound, physician not notified. Day four, labs show elevated markers. Day five, antibiotics finally started.

**NORA:** And now it looks like a case.

**JAMES:** Now it looks like it might be a case. But Marcus still needs someone qualified to say so. So step two is an expert. He asks Expert Institute for an orthopaedic surgeon, or perhaps an infectious disease specialist, with experience in post-operative infections, who has testified before but isn't a professional witness who testifies for everyone.

**NORA:** Why does that last part matter?

**JAMES:** Because juries and opposing counsel are suspicious of experts who seem to earn their living in court. A practising surgeon who occasionally does expert work tends to be more credible. Expert Institute's search team pulls a shortlist of three. Before Marcus picks one, he runs them through Expert Radar. One of them turns out to have had testimony limited by a judge in another state. Marcus drops him and goes with one of the others.

**NORA:** And then the other side names their expert.

**JAMES:** Right, months later, the hospital's lawyers name a defence expert. Marcus runs that person through Expert Radar too, and finds a published article where the defence expert took a position that seems to contradict what he's now saying. That becomes material for cross-examination. And monitoring keeps running in the background right up to trial.

**NORA:** So in that story Expert Institute touched the case three or four times.

**JAMES:** Records, merit, expert search, vetting, monitoring. That's the bundling idea. Each piece on its own is useful. Together, they make Expert Institute part of how the firm runs a case, not just a vendor it calls once.

**NORA:** Now let me push. In that story, what's the part a new AI tool could do for much less?

**JAMES:** The first part. The chronology. And that brings us to the competitive picture.

### The market and competitors

**NORA:** How big is this market?

**JAMES:** Honestly, the estimates are messy. One research aggregator, Verified Market Research, puts the expert witness services market at a bit over six hundred million dollars, growing to nearly a billion dollars at under six percent a year. But the same page is inconsistent about the base year and the end year, and other aggregators give different numbers. So I'd treat that as a rough indication that this is a market in the hundreds of millions of dollars rather than tens of billions, and leave it there.

**NORA:** Who are the customers, by segment?

**JAMES:** Three broad groups. Plaintiff firms, doing personal injury, medical malpractice, mass torts and class actions. Defence firms and corporate counsel, doing commercial litigation, intellectual property and regulatory work. And matters involving government agencies, for example securities, tax or financial industry regulators. The ECLS deal strengthens that second and third group.

**NORA:** And how do they buy?

**JAMES:** Mostly per case. A firm has a matter, it needs an expert or a record review, it buys that service. That's very different from BigHand's world of annual software subscriptions. It means revenue is tied to case volume and to the number of needs per case.

**NORA:** What's driving demand?

**JAMES:** The company's own 2026 outlook piece, so take it as their view, points to a few things. Mass tort dockets, the big waves of similar claims against one product or company, which tighten the supply of specialists. Telemedicine in acute care, which creates new standard-of-care questions. Disputes over AI training data and copyright, which need technical experts. And scrutiny of financial misconduct, which raises demand for forensic accountants.

**NORA:** AI creating demand for experts. There's an irony.

**JAMES:** There is, and I think it's real. Every new technology generates new kinds of disputes, and those disputes need people who can explain the technology to a court.

**NORA:** You mentioned mass torts. Explain why they matter so much for supply.

**JAMES:** A mass tort is when a large number of people claim harm from the same product or event, say a drug, a medical device or a consumer product. Thousands of separate cases can end up being managed together. And each side in each of those cases may need experts in the same narrow specialty. If there are only so many qualified specialists in, say, a particular area of toxicology, and many of them are already retained, the rest of the market finds it harder to get anyone at all.

**NORA:** So a network with a million names becomes more valuable when the specialists run short.

**JAMES:** That's the theory, and it's one of the better arguments for Expert Institute's model. When supply is tight, the firm with the deepest bench and the fastest recruiting wins the case. It also means demand can be lumpy. A big new mass tort can create a surge, and a settlement can end it. That's our inference about the dynamics, not something the company has quantified.

**NORA:** Does that make the business cyclical?

**JAMES:** Somewhat, in my view. Litigation overall is fairly steady, because people keep getting hurt and companies keep fighting over contracts. But the mix shifts. That's another reason a broader book, with the defence and regulatory work that ECLS brings, is useful. It spreads the exposure across different kinds of disputes.

**NORA:** Now competitors.

**JAMES:** I'd split them into two groups. The traditional ones first. Crunchbase lists several alternatives. GLG, or Gerson Lehrman Group, is best known as an expert network for investors and corporations doing research, rather than a litigation specialist. Forensis Group and IMS Legal Strategies are expert witness and litigation support firms. Courtroom Sciences does litigation consulting and expert work. We didn't verify the ownership or business models of those firms in detail, so I'll keep it at that.

**NORA:** And the second group?

**JAMES:** The AI-native software companies. These are the ones I'd watch. The most direct example is Chronicle. Chronicle makes software for Social Security disability practices, and in January 2025 it described AI medical chronologies at fifty dollars each, with fifteen to thirty minutes of processing.

**NORA:** Fifty dollars and half an hour, against per-page pricing and a few days.

**JAMES:** That's the gap. Now, to be fair, it's not a like-for-like comparison. Chronicle is self-serve software for a specific niche, disability claims, and it's not a human expert network. Expert Institute's chronologies come with physician review. But for a high-volume firm doing lots of similar cases, a fifty-dollar, thirty-minute benchmark resets expectations.

**NORA:** And the bigger AI companies?

**JAMES:** There's a whole wave of venture-backed plaintiff-side legal AI. EvenUp, which helps personal injury firms write demand letters, has raised three hundred and seventy million dollars according to Crunchbase. Eve has raised one hundred and sixty-four million. Supio, eighty-five million. Darrow, which finds potential class actions, sixty-three million. Crunchbase reported in June 2026 that about seventy-one percent of disclosed legal AI capital had gone to plaintiff-side companies.

**NORA:** So the money is flowing to exactly the customers Expert Institute serves.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Those companies don't compete for the expert's seat in the courtroom. They compete for the firm's AI budget, and for the firm's workflow. And in my view the real risk is that one of them adds records summaries, or even expert search, to a platform the firm already uses every day.

**NORA:** Let's sit on that for a moment. Why is the chronology the soft spot?

**JAMES:** Because of where the cost sits. In a chronology, the expensive part is skilled human time: someone reading and organising thousands of pages, and a physician checking it. AI is now very good at the reading and organising. So the price of that step is falling across the market, whether Expert Institute likes it or not.

**NORA:** But the physician review is the quality claim. Doesn't that protect them?

**JAMES:** Partly. The question is how much a customer will pay for it. For a complex malpractice case going to trial, a physician-reviewed chronology may be worth a lot. For a routine car accident case with a few hundred pages, a fifty-dollar automated summary might be good enough. So I'd expect the market to split. Premium, reviewed work at the top. Commodity, automated work at the bottom.

**NORA:** And if Expert Institute only plays at the top?

**JAMES:** Then it risks losing volume at the bottom, and volume matters, because the chronology is often the first thing a firm buys. If someone else owns the first touch, they have the best chance of selling the second and third. That's my main worry, and it's an inference, not something the company has said.

**NORA:** What about expert search? Can AI do that?

**JAMES:** AI can help a lot with the research part: finding candidates, reading their publications, checking their testimony history. Expert Institute already uses it there. What AI can't easily do is the human part: calling a busy surgeon, persuading her to take a case, checking conflicts, managing the relationship through a two-year lawsuit. The network and the relationships are harder to copy than the software.

**NORA:** So the moat is the people, and the exposed flank is the documents.

**JAMES:** That's how I see it. And the strategic question is whether Expert Institute uses its AI to make the documents business cheaper and faster on its own terms, before a competitor sets the price for it.

**NORA:** You're saying they should cut their own price.

**JAMES:** I'm saying they should be ready to. If you know a fifty-dollar benchmark exists, the worst position is to be surprised by it. The best position is to know exactly what your own chronology costs to produce, how far AI can bring that down, and what price still leaves you a healthy margin. Then you choose when to move.

### AI at Expert Institute today

**NORA:** Let's take stock. What AI is actually in the market today?

**JAMES:** Two things, clearly. Expert Radar, the vetting and monitoring tool, with its AI enhancements announced in July 2024. And AI medical chronologies, using natural language processing with physician review, described on the current website. The homepage also lists AI for researching experts and evaluating case merit.

**NORA:** And what's missing from the public record?

**JAMES:** We didn't find any AI partnership, any named AI leadership role, or any public statement of AI budget. The private equity firm's announcement in 2024 described Expert Institute as offering "AI-enabled due diligence solutions", but that was a description of what already existed, not a new AI plan. And we didn't find an AI acquisition.

**NORA:** So the AI is real, but the story around it is thin.

**JAMES:** I'd put it like this. Among the companies we'll cover in this series, Expert Institute is one of the further along. It has owned, in-house AI products in a core workflow, and about forty people in product development as of 2024. But it hasn't told the market much about how that AI works, how it's governed, or what it delivers in numbers.

**NORA:** Let's talk about governance then. Why does it matter here?

**JAMES:** Because Expert Institute handles some of the most sensitive data there is. Medical records are about as private as information gets. Law firms are bound by confidentiality to their clients, and medical information carries its own legal protections. So a firm uploading records will want to know where they go, who sees them, whether they're used to train models, and how long they're kept.

**NORA:** And what did you find on that?

**JAMES:** Not much, publicly. We didn't find a published AI governance policy. We also didn't see a security certification, such as a SOC 2 report, on the pages that were checked. I want to be careful here. Not finding it on a website is not proof it doesn't exist. Many companies share those reports privately during security reviews. But it's something a buyer would ask about directly.

**NORA:** Explain SOC 2 quickly.

**JAMES:** SOC 2 is a widely used American audit standard for how a service company protects customer data. Enterprise buyers often ask for it before signing. For a company selling into large defence firms and corporate legal departments, which is where ECLS points, those reviews get tougher.

**NORA:** And there's the data about the experts themselves.

**JAMES:** Good point. Expert Radar works by gathering information about experts from many sources. That raises its own questions about privacy and publicity rights, especially if information is collected at scale. We didn't look into whether there's any litigation on this, and we didn't find any. But in my view it's a risk area to manage carefully, and another reason a clear governance stance would help.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's list the challenges. What keeps leadership up at night, in your view?

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, price and turnaround transparency. Per-page pricing and "a few days" feel slow and opaque next to a fifty-dollar, thirty-minute benchmark. Even if the quality is higher, the customer needs to understand why they're paying more.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, the physician bottleneck. If physician review is the quality claim, it's also the capacity limit. Physicians are expensive and scarce. If the company handles thousands of cases a month, every hour of physician time per chronology matters. Using AI so that physicians review only the hard parts, rather than everything, is the obvious fix.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, founder dependence. Talve is in nearly every announcement and press profile. That's a strength, because he clearly has a vision, but it's also a concentration of knowledge and relationships. Building a deeper bench of visible leaders, especially on product and AI, would reduce that risk. That's our inference from the public record, not something anyone has said.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, proof. The marketing makes big claims, number one provider, links to large verdicts, and there's very little independent data behind them in public. As buyers get more sophisticated about AI, they'll want evidence: accuracy rates, turnaround times, customer outcomes. Companies that can show those numbers will win trust faster.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, integration and cross-selling. ECLS is a second brand with a different customer base. Bringing its clients into Expert iQ, or bringing Expert iQ's tools to its clients, sounds easy and usually isn't. Different sales motions, different expectations, different kinds of experts.

**NORA:** Is there a cultural tension as well? A network business becoming a software business?

**JAMES:** I think there is. In a network business, the hero is the researcher who finds the perfect expert overnight. In a software business, the hero is the system that does it automatically. Getting those two groups to see each other as allies, rather than the software as a threat, is a real management task. The best version is where AI does the drudgery and the researchers spend their time on the hard calls and the relationships.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Let's flip to opportunities. If you were on the board, where would you push?

**JAMES:** The first is volume chronologies. Use AI to cut the physician and paralegal hours per chronology, and offer a faster, cheaper tier for high-volume plaintiff firms. The measure that matters is throughput: how many chronologies each physician can support.

**NORA:** And pricing.

**JAMES:** Second, tiered pricing. Instead of one per-page price, offer clear tiers. An automated summary at a low price and fast turnaround. A physician-reviewed chronology at a higher price. A full expert review for complex cases. That lets the company compete at the bottom without giving away the top.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, automated expert matching and outreach. Expert Radar already holds a lot of data about experts. Use it to produce a ranked shortlist automatically, and even handle the first contact, so the search team spends less time per case and more time on the tricky ones.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, cross-selling ECLS. Take ECLS's regulatory and intellectual property network to the existing base, and take Expert iQ's tools to ECLS clients. Revenue per client is the measure. And more broadly, use ECLS to push into defence and corporate work, securities, pharmaceuticals, intellectual property, where expert engagements tend to be higher value. That last part is our inference about the strategy.

**NORA:** What about going abroad?

**JAMES:** I'd be cautious. Expert witness practice is very tied to each country's legal system, and the American system uses experts in a particular way. We saw no evidence of plans outside the United States, and I'd only pursue it with a clear demand signal.

**NORA:** And acquisitions?

**JAMES:** That's my fifth. A small tuck-in acquisition of a chronology or records AI company could be faster than building. The private equity owner has already shown it will do add-ons with ECLS. Buying an AI capability would be a different kind of add-on, but a logical one.

**NORA:** Is there an opportunity in data, the way there was for BigHand?

**JAMES:** I think so, carefully. Expert Institute has seen a very large number of expert searches, by its count nearly two hundred thousand as of 2024. That's a picture of what kinds of experts are in demand, where and for what. With proper care around confidentiality, that could improve matching and help the company predict where supply will be tight. I'd be very careful about anything that looked like sharing one firm's information with another.

### The best AI bet and the first hundred days

**NORA:** One AI use case to bet on for the next eighteen months. What is it?

**JAMES:** AI-first medical chronologies, with physician review on exception only. The AI reads and organises everything, flags the uncertain or high-stakes points, and the physician reviews those, rather than every page. That's where the cost is, where the competitive threat is, and where the company already has a product and a customer base.

**NORA:** Why physician review on exception only?

**JAMES:** Because it keeps the quality claim but changes the economics. If a physician spends twenty minutes on the flagged parts instead of two hours on the whole thing, the same physician can support many more cases. And the turnaround can drop from days toward hours. I want to be clear that the dollar size of this needs internal data: case volumes, hours per chronology, fees per page. None of that is public.

**NORA:** So how would you run it? Give me the first hundred days.

**JAMES:** First two weeks: get the unit economics. What does a chronology cost today, in physician hours, paralegal hours and software, by size and case type? Put that next to the fifty-dollar benchmark and see where the gap really is. That's the single most important fact, and it's easy to get wrong.

**NORA:** Then?

**JAMES:** Days fifteen to forty-five: build a test set. Take a few hundred past chronologies where the final physician-reviewed version is known to be good. Run the AI on the raw records and compare. Where does it agree? Where does it miss? Which kinds of records cause trouble, handwritten notes, poor scans, unusual specialties? That tells you what the exception rules should be.

**NORA:** And the next stretch?

**JAMES:** Days forty-five to seventy-five: shadow mode. On live cases, run the new AI-first process in parallel with the current one, for a group of friendly customers, without changing what they receive. Measure the time saved and any differences in quality. Physicians should help design the flags, because they'll be the ones trusting them.

**NORA:** And the last stretch to a hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days seventy-five to a hundred: decide the offer. Based on the evidence, launch a faster tier to a small group of high-volume firms at a clear published price, and keep the full review tier for complex cases. Set the turnaround target you can actually hit. And publish, at least to customers, how the AI and the physician review work together and how the data is protected.

**NORA:** And who owns it?

**JAMES:** One person, with authority over product, the physician network and pricing. If those three sit in different places and nobody owns the whole, it will drift.

**NORA:** And the second-best bet?

**JAMES:** Automated expert matching and outreach using Expert Radar's data. It saves time for a specific team, the search team, and it makes the core relationship business faster without trying to replace it.

### A second walk-through: the defence side

**NORA:** We did a plaintiff case. Let's do the other end of the market, because that's where ECLS takes them. Another invented example.

**JAMES:** Sure. Imagine an in-house legal team at a mid-sized medical device company. Call the deputy general counsel Priya. The company has been sued by a competitor claiming that its new device infringes a patent. It's a big, technical, high-stakes case, and Priya's outside law firm needs two kinds of experts quickly. A technical expert in the specific engineering field, and a damages expert who can talk about how much money is really at stake.

**NORA:** How is this different from Marcus's case?

**JAMES:** In almost every way. Marcus's case is one of many, and price matters a lot. Priya's case is one of a few, the stakes may be enormous, and quality and credibility matter far more than cost. She won't care whether a chronology costs fifty dollars or five hundred. She'll care whether the technical expert can survive a brutal cross-examination by a top firm.

**NORA:** So what does Expert Institute bring?

**JAMES:** Through ECLS, a network aimed at exactly this kind of high-stakes commercial and intellectual property dispute. The search team finds candidates who are genuine leaders in the field, ideally without conflicts with either company. Expert Radar checks their history: past testimony, rulings, publications, anything opposing counsel might use. And once the competitor names its own experts, the same tool helps Priya's team prepare to challenge them.

**NORA:** Where does AI help most here?

**JAMES:** In research depth. A technical expert may have decades of papers, patents and prior testimony. Reading all of that, and spotting where something they said years ago conflicts with the current case, is exactly the kind of work AI can speed up. That's the vetting side, and it's where Expert Institute already has its own tool.

**NORA:** And what's the business lesson from comparing Marcus and Priya?

**JAMES:** That the company is serving two very different markets with overlapping tools. For Marcus, AI is about price and speed. For Priya, AI is about depth and confidence. The strategy has to work for both. If you only optimise for cheap and fast, you may underserve the high-stakes clients. If you only optimise for premium, you may lose the volume.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses learn from Expert Institute?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four lessons. First, know which part of your service AI makes cheap. Most professional services are a bundle of steps. Some are judgment and relationships, which AI doesn't easily replace. Some are reading, sorting and summarising, which it increasingly does. If you don't know which is which in your own business, a competitor will tell you, usually through their price list.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, know your unit economics before the market moves. The most useful number for Expert Institute right now is the true cost of a chronology. Every services business facing AI should have that number for its core offering, and should know what it becomes with AI doing the first pass.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, reprice on your own terms. It is far better to launch a cheaper, faster tier yourself than to be forced into it. Cannibalising your own premium product feels painful, but letting someone else do it is worse.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, make your trust story as visible as your technology story. In sensitive markets like legal and medical, buyers will choose the vendor they trust with their data. A clear public stance on security, data use and how AI and human review work together is a competitive advantage, not a compliance chore.

**NORA:** I'd add one from the outside. The human part doesn't disappear. It gets more concentrated.

**JAMES:** I agree. The relationships with experts, the judgment about who will be credible in front of a jury, those become more valuable as the routine work gets cheaper. The trick is to let AI take the routine work so people can spend more of their time on that.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's talk risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-native records tools cutting out the physician-review step and setting a low market price. Two, plaintiff AI platforms like EvenUp, Eve or Supio bundling records or expert search into tools firms already use. That one's our inference. Three, pricing pressure on a per-page, per-case model. Four, data and privacy risk, both on medical records and on the expert data Expert Radar collects. And five, founder dependence.

**NORA:** And for the owner?

**JAMES:** The owner is about two years in, so it has time. But the AI market in legal is moving quickly, and the window to reposition the chronology business on its own terms won't stay open forever. Acting in the next twelve to eighteen months matters.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch for?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, any change in how Expert Institute prices and describes chronologies. A published price, a faster tier, or a new turnaround promise would be a big signal. Second, any AI acquisition or AI partnership, which would show the company buying capability rather than only network breadth. Third, any published security certification or AI governance statement, which would tell you it's preparing for bigger enterprise customers.

**NORA:** And a bonus one?

**JAMES:** Whether any of the big plaintiff AI platforms announces expert search or records chronologies. If that happens, the competitive picture changes quickly.
