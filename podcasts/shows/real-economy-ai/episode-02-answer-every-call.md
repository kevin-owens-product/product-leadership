# Episode 2: Answer Every Call
## "Field Services, Business Services, and the AI-Enabled Roll-Up"

**Duration:** ~7 minutes
**Hosts:** Maya & Grant
**Podcast:** AI in the Real Economy

---

### Cold open

[INTRO MUSIC]

**GRANT:** It's nine at night in January. A homeowner's furnace stops working. She calls the first HVAC company on her search results. It goes to voicemail. She hangs up without leaving a message and calls the next one.

**MAYA:** And that first company just lost an emergency job, and maybe a customer for the next ten years. Welcome back to AI in the Real Economy. I'm Maya.

**GRANT:** I'm Grant. Today is services: field and home services, and the business services companies that run on phones, email and paperwork. It's the sector where AI value is easiest to see and, I'd argue, easiest to get wrong.

---

### The economics of a missed call

**MAYA:** Start with the phone. Why does it matter so much?

**GRANT:** Because in home services, the phone is the revenue system. ServiceTitan, which sells software to contractors, cites Invoca research that home service businesses miss about twenty seven percent of inbound calls. It also says that around seventy eight percent of callers who reach voicemail don't leave a message, and, from its own data on more than twelve hundred contractors, that an average HVAC company loses somewhere between forty five thousand and a hundred and twenty thousand dollars a year to unanswered calls.

**MAYA:** Those are vendor numbers.

**GRANT:** They are, and a vendor that sells call handling has a reason to make missed calls sound expensive. But the shape of the problem is easy to check in any business. Pull a month of phone logs, count the unanswered calls by hour, and multiply by your average ticket and your close rate. Most operators are surprised.

**MAYA:** And this is where AI voice agents come in.

**GRANT:** Right. The current generation can answer around the clock, look up the customer in the CRM, book or reschedule into the live dispatch board, flag emergencies like no heat or a gas smell for the on-call technician, and hand off to a person when the call gets complicated. That last part matters more than any of the others.

---

### How to roll it out

**MAYA:** If I'm the operating partner, how do I roll this out without upsetting customers?

**GRANT:** Start after hours. It's the lowest-risk slot because today those calls go to voicemail or an expensive answering service. Any booking the agent makes is pure upside. Then add weekends and holidays, then peak-season overflow, and only then think about the daytime queue.

**MAYA:** What has to be true for it to work?

**GRANT:** Three things. It has to write directly into the system of record. An agent that books into a separate calendar creates phantom appointments and double bookings, which is worse than voicemail. It has to know the trade: what counts as an emergency, which jobs need which skills, what a maintenance agreement covers. And it has to be honest. Tell callers they're talking to an AI, and make it easy to reach a human.

**MAYA:** And measure what?

**GRANT:** Answer rate, booking rate, how often calls escalate to a person, cancellations from AI-booked jobs, and revenue per call. Compare AI-handled hours against the same hours last year. If the booking rate is good but the cancellation rate is high, the agent is booking things that shouldn't be booked.

---

### Beyond the phone

**MAYA:** The phone's the obvious one. What else?

**GRANT:** Dispatch and scheduling is next. Matching jobs to technicians by skill, location and traffic is a hard optimization problem that most companies solve with a whiteboard and one very tired dispatcher. Then the technician's day: AI that drafts the job notes from a voice memo, suggests the right parts, and writes up the quote while the technician is still on site.

**MAYA:** And the back office.

**GRANT:** Invoicing the same day the job closes, chasing unpaid invoices, renewing maintenance agreements before they lapse, and following up on quotes that never converted. None of that is glamorous, and all of it is cash.

**MAYA:** What about business services? Staffing firms, outsourced back offices, inspection companies, that kind of thing.

**GRANT:** Same pattern, different workflow. Business services companies sell expert time, so the question is how much of that time goes into the parts customers don't value. Intake, document review, report drafting, scheduling, quality checks. Permira's example from the legal services world was matching lawyers to projects and giving them specialized tools for contract analysis. The expert still does the expert work. AI takes the rest.

---

### The AI-enabled roll-up

**MAYA:** Let's talk about the thesis a lot of funds are chasing. Buy fragmented service businesses, put them on one platform, and use AI to run the back office centrally.

**GRANT:** The bull case is simple. Plenty of these businesses run at single-digit EBITDA margins with manual scheduling, paper estimates and overloaded phones. One widely shared write-up modeled twenty to thirty percent margin expansion from cost savings alone in an AI-enabled home services roll-up. That's one author's model, not a result, but you can see why it's attractive.

**MAYA:** And the bear case?

**GRANT:** Integration is the whole game, and integration is where roll-ups go to die. Every acquired business has its own software, its own price book, its own way of dispatching, and a founder who built it that way for a reason. If you can't get them onto one system of record, the AI has nothing to work with. And local reputation is the asset you bought. A clumsy AI phone experience can damage a brand that took twenty years to build.

**MAYA:** So the sequencing matters.

**GRANT:** Hugely. I'd say: platform first, then centralize the functions customers don't see, like billing and collections, then the ones they do, like the phone. And keep the local brand and the local technicians front and center.

---

### People and risk

**MAYA:** There's a workforce question we have to address.

**GRANT:** There is. In most of these businesses, the honest story is that AI absorbs growth rather than replacing people. You add revenue without adding call center seats. But where a plan does reduce headcount, the legal side is real. Foley's piece pointed to WARN Act notices, plant-closing rules, severance and union bargaining. Get employment counsel involved before the plan is announced, not after.

**MAYA:** And customer-facing AI has its own rules.

**GRANT:** Disclosure, call recording consent, which varies by state, and data handling for customer records. And if an AI is making decisions about pricing or credit, like financing offers on a new system, several US states now have rules on automated decision-making. Keep a human in the loop on anything that looks like a credit or pricing decision about an individual.

---

### Takeaways

**MAYA:** Sum it up.

**GRANT:** In services, the phone is the revenue system, so start there, after hours first. Write into the system of record or don't bother. Move to dispatch, the technician's paperwork and the back office next. If you're running a roll-up, the platform comes before the AI. And tell people, both customers and staff, what the AI is doing.
