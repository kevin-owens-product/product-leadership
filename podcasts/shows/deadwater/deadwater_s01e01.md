# DEADWATER
## Episode 1: "The Find"
### Season 1

---

[NARRATOR] There is a stretch of highway on the south shore of Long Island that most people drive through without looking.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It runs along the coast between two barrier islands, a thin ribbon of asphalt with the Atlantic on one side and a brackish marsh on the other. In summer, the traffic slows to a crawl as families head to the beach. In winter, it is almost empty. The wind comes off the water at an angle that makes it feel colder than it is. The reeds in the marsh stand perfectly still, even when everything else is moving.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It is a beautiful place, in a bleak sort of way. And for reasons that took thirteen years to fully understand, it was chosen.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] My name is Sam Wilder. I'm an investigative journalist based in New York. For the past three years I've been reporting on the case that locals call Deadwater and that the national press eventually named after the stretch of beach where it began. What you're about to hear is the story of eight women, one methodical killer, and the investigators who refused to let the case die even when the system around them tried to let it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Some names in this series have been changed. The victims are referred to by first name only, with the permission of their families. The investigative team members have given their full cooperation. The killer - Roland Voss - is referred to by name throughout, because he has now pleaded guilty in open court, and because erasing his name would feel like extending a courtesy he does not deserve.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] December. The kind of cold that makes the whole world feel abandoned.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] A search party is moving through the marsh grass along the south shore. They are looking for a missing woman - a young woman named Karen who was last seen leaving a motel in a nearby town four days earlier. Someone called the tip line. Said they thought they'd seen her near the highway. The search coordinator marked the grid. Volunteers showed up at seven in the morning.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They do not find Karen.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] What they find, inside the first hour, inside the first quarter mile, is a bundle of clothing wrapped around human remains.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Detective Sergeant Carol Marsh gets the call at 8:47 in the morning. She is on her second coffee, at her desk, working a different case entirely.

[MARSH] My first thought was that it was Karen. That made sense - we'd been running that missing persons file for four days and the tip had put her in that area. So I grabbed my coat and I drove out there and I was already thinking about the family notification. Thinking about what you say. How you say it.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] By the time I arrived there were three evidence markers in the grass and two of my colleagues were standing very still, looking at the ground in a way that told me immediately this was not Karen.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The remains belong to a woman. She has been dead for some time - the medical examiner will later estimate at least a year, possibly longer. She is bound. She has been placed here deliberately, carefully, in a location that is visible from the road but only barely, and only if you know where to look.

[MARSH] In my career up to that point I had worked nine homicides. I had never seen a victim placed like that. It was precise. There was intention in it. And when you see that kind of intention you understand immediately that whoever did this had done it before.

[NARRATOR] Her name, investigators will later learn, is Ava. She was twenty-three years old. She worked as an escort and advertised online. She disappeared from the eastern end of Long Island fourteen months earlier. Her mother filed a missing persons report within twenty-four hours of her disappearance. The report sat in a queue.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] By four in the afternoon, there are two more sets of remains in the grass.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The South Shore Police Department - I'll call them SSPD throughout this series to protect the identities of specific officers still active on the force - began a large-scale search the following morning. They called in state police. They called in the county crime lab. They marked off a two-mile corridor and walked it in a grid pattern.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Over the following four days, they find seven more sets of remains along the same stretch of road.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Ten victims. All adult women. All bound. All placed in the same deliberate, careful way. All of them had, at some point, advertised escort services online. All of them had been reported missing by someone who loved them, and in most cases those reports had generated minimal follow-up.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The world, at this point, begins to pay attention.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey is at her kitchen table in upstate New York when she sees the news alert. Her sister Renee has been missing for sixteen months. She disappeared from a motel on Long Island where she had been working. Donna has called the police so many times she has the number memorized. She has driven to Long Island herself twice to look. She has set up a Facebook page, printed flyers, contacted news stations. No one has called her back.

[DONNA] When the news broke I was making breakfast for my kids. I had the TV on in the background and I heard them say "unidentified female remains" and I just stopped. I stood there with a spatula in my hand and I thought: that's Renee. I don't know how I knew. I just knew.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I called the tip line they put up and I was on hold for forty minutes. Forty minutes. And when someone finally answered I told them about Renee and they said they'd log my information and someone would be in touch. I waited three weeks before anyone called me back.

[NARRATOR] Donna Kelsey is speaking to me in her living room in the spring of last year. She is fifty-one. She has a quiet, contained intensity to her that feels like a long time in the making - like someone who learned very early that falling apart was a luxury she couldn't afford.

[DONNA] I used to get angry at that. The waiting. The not knowing. Now I understand it a little better because I know how chaotic those first weeks were. They had ten sets of remains and a department that had never run anything like this. I get it. But understanding something doesn't mean it didn't hurt.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Renee is identified as one of the victims six weeks after the initial discovery. She is twenty-six years old. She is the mother of a three-year-old son.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna drives to Long Island to make a formal identification. She meets Carol Marsh in a corridor outside the medical examiner's office.

[DONNA] She was the first person in any official capacity who actually looked at me like I was a human being. She shook my hand and she said, I'm going to find out what happened to your sister. Not "we're going to do everything we can." Not "we'll keep you updated." She said: I'm going to find out what happened. And I believed her. I still don't fully understand why I believed her, given how many times I'd been let down. But I did.

[MARSH] I meant it. I know that sounds like something you say. But I'd been looking at those markers in the grass for four days and I had a very clear feeling that whoever did this thought he was finished. Thought he'd gotten away with it. And I was not going to let that stand.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The investigation in those early months is massive and messy and, in ways that will matter later, fundamentally flawed.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Hundreds of detectives are assigned. Tips flood in at a rate no one is equipped to process. The media attention is enormous and not always helpful - journalists are camped at the crime scene, running stories that compromise potential evidence lines, printing names and details that investigators would have preferred to keep quiet.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] And there is another problem. A problem that Carol Marsh and Danny Reese - her partner, who joins the case in the second week - begin to identify quietly, carefully, without saying it out loud to anyone above them.

[REESE] The department had a history. Not just this case - the whole department had a history of not taking certain kinds of victims seriously. Women who worked in the sex trade. Women who were poor. Women who were people of color. The families had called. The families had filed reports. The families had been told, in various ways, that their loved ones had probably just moved on. That they'd turn up. And now we had ten bodies in the grass and we had to ask ourselves: what if we'd done more, earlier? How many of these women might still be alive?

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese has been a detective for eighteen years. He is precise, methodical, and almost allergic to hyperbole. When he says something, he has already considered it from six angles.

[REESE] I don't say that lightly. I'm not interested in making the department look bad for its own sake. But the truth matters. And the truth is that there were warning signs and they were missed. Part of our job, going forward, was to make sure we understood exactly how that happened.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Within the first month, a profile emerges. The killer is almost certainly male. He is methodical and organized - the placement of the bodies, the consistent binding method, the choice of location suggest someone with deep familiarity with the area and careful advance planning. He is likely older - the oldest of the murders, which investigators trace back by physical evidence to at least the mid-nineties, suggest a pattern spanning decades. He is probably local. He has probably driven this road hundreds of times.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is probably, investigators believe, completely unremarkable on the surface. The kind of man no one would look at twice.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] They are right about all of it. And it will take them twelve more years to prove it.

[MUSIC STING]

[MARSH] People ask me what it's like to work a case that long. And the honest answer is that it's not one thing for that long. In the beginning it's everything - it's all-consuming, it's the only thing you think about. Then it goes cold and it becomes something you carry with you, like a weight in your coat pocket. You feel it every day but you stop actively reaching for it. And then something changes and it comes roaring back and suddenly you're in year twelve and you're more certain than you've ever been and also more exhausted than you've ever been and you're thinking: is this finally it?

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] It was finally it.

[NARRATOR] Next time on Deadwater: Ocean Parkway. As more victims are identified, investigators begin to understand the geography of the killer's method - and the specific local knowledge it would take to use it.

[MUSIC STING]
