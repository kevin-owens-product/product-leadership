### Why operators should care

**THEO:** Let's start with the obvious objection. Most of our listeners run or invest in companies that use AI, not companies that build frontier models. Why should they care about AI regulation?

**HANA:** Four reasons. First, a lot of AI regulation is about use, not just development. The EU AI Act puts obligations on companies that deploy AI in certain ways, not just on the companies that build it. US state laws on hiring, insurance and consumer protection apply to whoever makes the decision. So a company that uses an AI tool to screen job applicants can be regulated even if it bought the tool from someone else.

**THEO:** Second.

**HANA:** Existing law already applies to AI. Consumer protection, anti-discrimination, privacy, product safety, securities law. Regulators have said repeatedly that there's no AI exemption. The US Federal Trade Commission has brought cases about deceptive AI claims. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has addressed discrimination by hiring tools. The Securities and Exchange Commission has brought cases about AI-washing. Most AI enforcement so far has used old laws, not new ones.

**THEO:** Third.

**HANA:** Customers, buyers and insurers are asking. Large customers send AI questionnaires as part of procurement. Buyers in M and A run AI diligence, as our sister show The AI Diligence Room covers. And representations and warranties insurers are starting to exclude AI-specific risks, according to Foley. A company with good answers wins deals and gets better terms. A company without them doesn't.

**THEO:** And fourth.

**HANA:** Directors have duties. In the US, the Delaware courts have made clear that boards must make a good faith effort to oversee major risks to the company, especially risks that are central to the business or subject to regulation. If AI is central to how a company operates, its board needs some oversight of AI risk. We'll come back to that in episode four.

**THEO:** So the summary is: even if you never touch a frontier model, the rules reach you.

**HANA:** They reach you through what you use AI for, through laws that already exist, through your customers and buyers, and through your board's duties.

### Three regulatory philosophies

**THEO:** Let's draw the map. How would you describe the big picture?

**HANA:** I'd describe three broad approaches. The European approach is comprehensive, horizontal legislation: one big law, the AI Act, that applies across sectors, sorted by risk level, with specific obligations for each level. The American approach, at least at the federal level, is mostly sector-by-sector enforcement of existing laws, with a strong emphasis right now on not over-regulating, plus a growing patchwork of state laws. And then there are a range of other approaches, like the UK's, which relies on existing regulators applying principles in their own sectors, and China's, which has a set of targeted rules for specific technologies like recommendation algorithms, deep synthesis and generative AI.

**THEO:** And for a company operating internationally, all of them can apply.

**HANA:** Depending on where it operates and who its customers are. The EU AI Act, like the GDPR, applies to companies outside the EU if they place AI systems on the EU market or if their AI system's output is used in the EU. That's what makes the European rules matter so much for American and British companies.

**THEO:** And the standards?

**HANA:** That's the fourth layer, and in practice it's often the most useful one for companies. Voluntary frameworks like the NIST AI Risk Management Framework in the US and the international management system standard ISO slash IEC 42001 give companies a practical structure for governing AI, regardless of which laws apply. We'll spend real time on them in episode four.

### The European Union

**THEO:** Let's go region by region. Start with the EU.

**HANA:** The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024, and its obligations phase in over several years. It sorts AI into risk tiers. At the top, a short list of prohibited practices, banned outright. Then high-risk systems, which carry the heaviest obligations. Then systems with specific transparency obligations, like chatbots and AI-generated content. And then everything else, which is most AI, and which faces no specific obligations under the Act beyond general AI literacy. Plus a separate set of rules for general-purpose AI models, the big foundation models.

**THEO:** And the timeline?

**HANA:** The timeline is where people get confused, especially after this year's changes. The prohibitions applied from February 2025, along with an AI literacy duty for the companies that provide and deploy AI. The rules for general-purpose AI models applied from August 2025. The transparency obligations under Article 50, like telling people they're talking to an AI, applied from August 2026, with a short grace period for some watermarking requirements.

**THEO:** And the high-risk rules?

**HANA:** Those were originally due to apply from August 2026 for most high-risk systems. But in May this year, the EU institutions agreed what's called the Digital Omnibus on AI, which pushed those dates back. For stand-alone high-risk systems listed in Annex III, like AI used in recruitment, credit scoring or access to essential services, the date moved to December 2027. For AI embedded in products already covered by EU product safety law, like medical devices or machinery, it moved to August 2028.

**THEO:** Why the delay?

**HANA:** A mix of reasons. The technical standards that companies need in order to comply weren't ready in time. Businesses argued the rules were too complex and would hurt European competitiveness. And there was a broader political push in Europe this year to simplify regulation. So the EU gave itself and businesses more time, and made some simplifications along the way.

**THEO:** And the penalties?

**HANA:** Significant. For prohibited practices, fines of up to thirty five million euros or seven percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. For most other violations, lower tiers. These are maximums, and enforcement is mostly by national authorities, so how hard they'll enforce, and how consistently across countries, is still an open question.

**THEO:** We'll do a full episode on the EU Act next time. Give me the one-sentence version for an operator.

**HANA:** If you sell or use AI in Europe, check whether any of your uses fall into the prohibited or high-risk categories, make sure your chatbots and AI-generated content meet the transparency rules that already apply, train your people, and use the extra time before December 2027 to build the documentation the high-risk rules will need.

---

### The United States: federal

**THEO:** Now the US. What's the federal picture?

**HANA:** There's no comprehensive federal AI law, and the current administration's policy is focused on promoting AI development and limiting regulation. In January 2025, the President revoked the previous administration's executive order on AI safety, which had set up reporting requirements for developers of the most powerful models, among other things. In July 2025, the White House published an AI Action Plan with a strong emphasis on innovation, infrastructure and reducing regulatory barriers.

**THEO:** And the states?

**HANA:** That's become the central tension. As Congress didn't pass a federal AI law, states moved ahead with their own. In 2025, Congress debated a moratorium that would have stopped states from enforcing AI laws for a period, but it was stripped from the bill that passed. Then in December 2025, the President signed an executive order aimed at establishing a national framework and challenging state AI laws the administration considers burdensome, including through the courts.

**THEO:** So the federal government is trying to limit state laws.

**HANA:** It's trying, and the legal fights are ongoing. An executive order can direct federal agencies, but it can't on its own repeal state laws. So for now, state laws stay in force unless a court strikes them down or Congress preempts them. Companies should comply with state laws that apply to them, and watch the litigation.

**THEO:** And the federal agencies?

**HANA:** They still enforce existing laws. The FTC on deceptive and unfair practices, including AI claims and data practices. The EEOC on employment discrimination. Financial regulators on fair lending and model risk management. The Food and Drug Administration on AI in medical devices, where it has authorized more than a thousand AI-enabled devices. The SEC on disclosures and AI-washing. The emphasis and the pace of enforcement vary with the administration, but the laws themselves haven't changed.

---

### The United States: states

**THEO:** Let's map the states. Which laws matter most?

**HANA:** I'd group them into five categories. First, broad laws on high-risk or consequential AI decisions. Colorado passed the first comprehensive one in 2024, modeled partly on the EU approach, with a duty of care against algorithmic discrimination, risk management programs and impact assessments. It never took effect. After two delays, Colorado repealed and replaced it this May with a narrower law that starts in January 2027. The new law covers automated decision-making technology that materially influences consequential decisions in areas like employment, education, housing, lending, insurance, healthcare and government services. It drops the duty of care and the impact assessments, and focuses on documentation from developers, notice to consumers before use, an explanation within thirty days after an adverse decision, and, where commercially reasonable, a human review. Only the state attorney general can enforce it.

**THEO:** Second.

**HANA:** Employment. New York City has required bias audits and notices for automated employment decision tools since 2023. Illinois amended its human rights law, effective January 2026, to make it a civil rights violation for employers to use AI in ways that discriminate, and to require notice when AI is used in employment decisions. And several states have rules on AI in video interviews.

**THEO:** Third.

**HANA:** Privacy and automated decision-making rights. A growing number of state privacy laws give consumers rights to opt out of profiling for significant decisions. California's privacy regulator finalized regulations on automated decision-making technology in 2025, with obligations phasing in, including requirements around significant decisions from 2027.

**THEO:** Fourth.

**HANA:** Transparency and disclosure. California's law requiring bots to disclose they're bots in certain commercial and political contexts has been around since 2019. Utah passed a law requiring disclosure of generative AI use in some consumer interactions. California passed rules on generative AI in healthcare communications, and in 2025 a law on companion chatbots, with safeguards for minors. Texas passed a broad AI law in 2025, effective January 2026, which focuses mostly on government use and certain prohibited practices.

**THEO:** And fifth?

**HANA:** Frontier models. California passed the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act in 2025, which requires the largest AI model developers to publish safety frameworks and report certain incidents. New York passed its own frontier model law. Those mostly affect the big labs, not our listeners, but they set expectations for transparency that may spread.

**THEO:** That's a lot of states.

**HANA:** And it changes every legislative session. Hundreds of AI bills are introduced each year. Most don't pass, and many that pass are narrow. The practical approach for a company is not to track every bill, but to know which categories of AI use it has, and to monitor the laws that affect those categories in the states where it operates.

---

### Sector regulators

**THEO:** You said existing sector regulators matter a lot. Give me a quick tour.

**HANA:** Financial services first, because it's the most mature. Banks have had model risk management expectations for years, like the US guidance known as SR 11-7, which requires validation, documentation and governance of models used in decisions. Those expectations now apply to AI models too. Fair lending law requires that credit decisions not discriminate, and lenders must give specific reasons when they deny credit, even if an AI model made the decision. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau said in 2023 that "the algorithm did it" is not an acceptable reason.

**THEO:** Insurance.

**HANA:** Insurance is regulated by the states, and the National Association of Insurance Commissioners adopted a model bulletin on insurers' use of AI in December 2023. A majority of states have since adopted it or something similar. It expects insurers to have a written AI program, governance, risk management and controls, including for AI from third-party vendors, with a focus on avoiding unfair discrimination. Colorado has its own specific rules for life insurers' use of external data and algorithms.

**THEO:** Healthcare.

**HANA:** The FDA regulates AI that qualifies as a medical device. HIPAA governs patient data, including in AI tools. The federal nondiscrimination rule under the Affordable Care Act now requires covered organizations to take reasonable steps to identify and reduce discrimination risks from patient care decision support tools, including AI. And states are adding rules on AI in utilization review and in patient communications. Our sister show, AI in the Real Economy, has a full episode on this.

**THEO:** Employment.

**HANA:** Federal anti-discrimination law applies to AI hiring tools, and employers can be liable for discrimination caused by tools they buy. The emphasis of federal enforcement has shifted with the current administration, but private lawsuits continue. A notable one is a case against the HR software company Workday, where a federal court in California allowed claims to proceed in 2024 and 2025 on the theory that a vendor can be liable as an agent of the employers using its screening tools. That case is ongoing, but it's a signal that vendors aren't immune either.

**THEO:** And everyone else?

**HANA:** Consumer protection, from the FTC and state attorneys general, applies to everyone. State attorneys general have become increasingly active on AI, including through investigations and settlements over AI products. And privacy regulators, especially in Europe, have been some of the most active AI enforcers, because AI usually involves personal data.

---

### The United Kingdom

**THEO:** The UK, since many of our listeners' funds are based there.

**HANA:** The UK has so far chosen not to pass a broad AI law. Its approach, set out in a 2023 white paper, asks existing regulators, like the data protection regulator, the financial regulators and the competition authority, to apply five cross-cutting principles to AI in their own sectors: safety, transparency, fairness, accountability and contestability.

**THEO:** Has that changed?

**HANA:** There's been talk for some time of a narrower AI bill focused on the most powerful models, but as of our recording, the UK still relies mainly on existing law and regulators. The UK GDPR and the data protection regulator's guidance on AI matter a lot in practice. The Data (Use and Access) Act, passed in 2025, adjusted some rules on automated decision-making. And financial services firms face expectations from their regulators on model risk and fair treatment of customers.

**THEO:** So for a UK company, the GDPR-style rules are the main AI rules.

**HANA:** For most UK companies, data protection law and sector regulation are the main constraints, plus the EU AI Act if they sell into the EU. The UK government's emphasis, like the US's, has been on encouraging AI adoption and investment.

---

### China and the rest of the world

**THEO:** And China?

**HANA:** China has taken a targeted approach, with specific rules for specific technologies. Rules on recommendation algorithms from 2022. Rules on deep synthesis, meaning deepfakes, from 2023. Interim measures on generative AI services from 2023, which require providers of public-facing generative AI to meet content and security requirements and register their models. And rules requiring labeling of AI-generated content, which took effect in September 2025.

**THEO:** Does that matter for Western mid-market companies?

**HANA:** Mostly only if they offer AI services to the public in China, which few mid-market companies do. But it matters for supply chains and for companies using Chinese AI models, which have become very capable and are often available as open-weight models. Using them raises questions about licenses, security and in some sectors export controls and government restrictions, rather than about Chinese law directly.

**THEO:** And other countries?

**HANA:** Many countries have national AI strategies, and several have passed or are considering laws. South Korea passed a framework AI act in late 2024, effective in 2026. Japan passed a law in 2025 promoting AI development with a light-touch governance approach. Canada's proposed AI law died when Parliament was dissolved in early 2025. Brazil has been working on a comprehensive bill. And there are international efforts: the Council of Europe's AI convention, the OECD AI principles, and the G7's Hiroshima process code of conduct.

**THEO:** That's a lot of moving parts.

**HANA:** It is. For most of our listeners, the practical map is: the EU AI Act if you touch the EU market, US state laws where you operate, existing sector and consumer laws everywhere, and data protection law everywhere. The rest is context.

---

### The other rulebook: copyright

**THEO:** We haven't mentioned copyright, and it's one of the biggest legal questions in AI. Where does it fit on the map?

**HANA:** It's a parallel rulebook, mostly written by courts rather than legislators. The central question is whether training AI models on copyrighted material without permission is lawful. In the US, that turns on fair use, and the courts are working through dozens of cases. In 2025, two federal judges in California found that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use in cases against Anthropic and Meta, though in Anthropic's case the judge found that using pirated copies was not protected, and Anthropic then agreed to a settlement of around one and a half billion dollars with authors. In another case, a Delaware court found that a legal research startup's use of Westlaw content to build a competing tool was not fair use.

**THEO:** So the answer is "it depends."

**HANA:** It depends on how the material was obtained, what it was used for, and whether the result competes with the original. And many cases are still going, including the New York Times case against OpenAI and Microsoft. For operators, the practical points are narrower. If you're training or tuning models yourself, know where your data came from and whether you have the rights. If you're using models from the big providers, check whether they offer indemnities against copyright claims for business customers, which several do, and what the conditions are.

**THEO:** And Europe?

**HANA:** The EU has a text and data mining exception in its copyright law, which allows mining of lawfully accessible content unless the rights holder has opted out in a machine-readable way. The AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models to have a policy to respect those opt-outs, and to publish a summary of the content used for training. The EU's code of practice for general-purpose AI, published in July 2025, includes a chapter on copyright that many major providers signed up to.

**THEO:** And the outputs?

**HANA:** The US Copyright Office said in a January 2025 report that purely AI-generated material, without enough human creative control, isn't protected by copyright, though human contributions can be. That matters for businesses that sell content. If your product is AI-generated, you may not be able to stop others from copying it, so protect it through contracts, trade secrets and speed instead.

---

### Synthetic media, voices and likeness

**THEO:** One more category that's moving fast: rules about AI-generated images, video and voices. Does that matter for ordinary businesses?

**HANA:** More than people expect, because marketing teams use these tools every day now. Three areas. First, labeling. The EU AI Act's transparency rules require deepfakes to be disclosed as artificially generated, and require providers of generative systems to mark outputs in a machine-readable way. China requires labeling of AI-generated content. And several US states require disclosure of AI-generated content in political advertising.

**THEO:** Second.

**HANA:** Voice and likeness. Tennessee passed a law in 2024, known as the ELVIS Act, protecting people's voices from unauthorized AI imitation, and other states have expanded their rights of publicity to cover digital replicas. So a company that uses an AI voice that sounds like a known person, or creates an AI version of a real person for an ad, without consent, is taking a real legal risk. Use licensed voices and get written consent for any real person's likeness.

**THEO:** And third?

**HANA:** Robocalls. In the US, the Federal Communications Commission ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. So an AI voice calling consumers generally needs the same prior consent as a prerecorded robocall. That ruling came shortly after a fake AI voice of the President was used in robocalls before a primary election, and it affects every business thinking about outbound AI calling.

**THEO:** And intimate or harmful deepfakes?

**HANA:** Those are now crimes or civil wrongs in many places. In the US, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed in May 2025, criminalized publishing non-consensual intimate images, including AI-generated ones, and requires platforms to remove them quickly on request. That's mostly relevant to platforms that host user content, but it shows how quickly lawmakers move when a harm is visible.

---

### Standards: NIST and ISO

**THEO:** You mentioned standards. Why are they so useful?

**HANA:** Because they give companies a way to govern AI that works regardless of which laws apply, and because regulators and courts often treat following recognized standards as evidence of reasonable care. Two matter most. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology in January 2023, with a generative AI profile added in July 2024. And ISO slash IEC 42001, the international standard for AI management systems, published in December 2023.

**THEO:** How are they different?

**HANA:** NIST's framework is a voluntary guide. It organizes AI risk management into four functions: govern, map, measure and manage. Govern is about culture, policies and accountability. Map is about understanding the context and risks of each AI system. Measure is about assessing and tracking those risks. And manage is about acting on them. It's flexible and widely referenced, especially in the US.

**THEO:** And ISO 42001?

**HANA:** ISO 42001 is a management system standard, similar in style to ISO 27001 for information security. It sets requirements for an organization's AI management system: policies, roles, risk assessment, impact assessment, controls, monitoring, internal audit and continuous improvement. The key difference is that you can be certified against it by an accredited auditor. That certification is becoming a selling point, especially for companies that sell AI products to large enterprises.

**THEO:** And they fit with the laws?

**HANA:** They help a lot. Some laws have explicitly rewarded them. Colorado's original 2024 AI Act gave companies a defense if they followed a recognized framework like NIST's or ISO's, and although that law was replaced this year, the idea shows up in other bills and in how regulators judge reasonable care. And the EU's harmonized standards for the AI Act, which are still being finalized, will draw on similar concepts. A company that builds its governance on one of these frameworks is well positioned for whatever laws come next.

---

### What the rules actually ask for

**THEO:** Let's step back. Across all these laws and standards, are there common themes? What do they actually ask companies to do?

**HANA:** Remarkably consistent themes, even though the details differ. I'd list eight. One, know what AI you have: an inventory. Two, assess the risk of each use, especially where AI affects people's rights, health, safety, money or jobs. Three, for higher-risk uses, do more: test for accuracy and bias, document how the system works, keep records.

**THEO:** Four.

**HANA:** Human oversight: someone accountable who can understand, check and override the AI. Five, transparency: tell people when they're interacting with AI, when AI is used to make significant decisions about them, and when content is AI-generated. Six, data governance: lawful, appropriate, good-quality data, with privacy protected.

**THEO:** Seven and eight.

**HANA:** Seven, security and robustness: AI systems that work reliably and resist attack. And eight, accountability: policies, roles, training, incident handling, and the ability to show regulators what you did. If a company does those eight things well, in proportion to its risks, it will be in reasonable shape under most of the laws we've discussed.

**THEO:** That's encouraging.

**HANA:** It should be. The laws look overwhelming in their details, but the underlying asks are things any well-run company would want to do anyway. The work is in doing them proportionately: heavy for high-risk uses, light for low-risk ones.

### AI literacy in practice

**THEO:** You mentioned the AI literacy duty in the EU. What does that mean in practice?

**HANA:** Article 4 of the AI Act originally said providers and deployers of AI systems must take measures to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff and others dealing with AI systems on their behalf. It's applied since February 2025. This year's Omnibus deal softened the wording, so that companies must support the development of AI literacy rather than guarantee a particular level. Either way, there's no specific test or certificate. It's about proportionate, documented effort.

**THEO:** So what would a reasonable company do?

**HANA:** Three levels. Everyone who uses AI tools gets basic training: what AI can and can't do, the company's policy, how to protect data, how to check outputs, and when to escalate. People who use AI in higher-risk processes, like recruiters using screening tools or claims handlers using decision support, get specific training on that tool, its limitations and their oversight responsibilities. And leaders and board members get a briefing on AI risks and governance.

**THEO:** And documentation?

**HANA:** Keep records of who was trained, on what, and when. If a regulator ever asks, that's your evidence. And it's good practice everywhere, not just in Europe. Most AI incidents involve a person who didn't understand what the tool was doing.

### Proportionality: the risk-based mindset

**THEO:** Let's talk about proportionality, because I think it's where companies go wrong most often.

**HANA:** Companies go wrong in both directions. Some do nothing, because AI regulation seems distant and complicated. Others overreact, and build a heavy approval process that every use of AI must go through, including someone using an AI assistant to draft an email. That slows the business down, frustrates staff, and pushes AI use underground, which is worse for risk.

**THEO:** So what's the right approach?

**HANA:** Tier your uses. Most AI use in a typical company is low risk: drafting, summarizing, research, coding assistance, internal analysis. For those, you need an approved tool with proper data protections, a short policy, and some training. That's it. A smaller set of uses is medium risk: customer-facing chatbots, AI-generated marketing content, AI in internal processes that affect customers. Those need an owner, testing, monitoring and the transparency rules.

**THEO:** And high risk?

**HANA:** A small number of uses are high risk: AI that makes or substantially influences decisions about people's jobs, credit, insurance, housing, healthcare, education, or access to essential services, or AI in safety-critical products. Those need the full treatment: risk and impact assessments, bias and accuracy testing, documentation, human oversight, notices and appeals for affected people, and close attention to the specific laws.

**THEO:** And the tiering itself becomes a governance tool.

**HANA:** It's the foundation. When someone wants to use a new AI tool, the first question is which tier it's in. That determines everything else. It keeps the low-risk path fast and the high-risk path careful.

### Where operators trip up

**THEO:** In your experience, where do mid-sized companies most often trip up?

**HANA:** Five places. First, hiring. Companies adopt AI tools for screening resumes or analyzing interviews without realizing that this is one of the most regulated uses of AI, in the US and the EU. New York City's bias audit rules, Illinois's notice requirements, the EU's high-risk category, and federal anti-discrimination law all apply. And the employer is usually responsible, not just the vendor.

**THEO:** Second.

**HANA:** Customer-facing chatbots. A chatbot that gives wrong information can create liability, as Air Canada learned in 2024 when a Canadian tribunal held it responsible for its chatbot's wrong answer about bereavement fares. Plus disclosure rules, and, for chatbots that interact with minors, a growing set of specific protections.

**THEO:** Third.

**HANA:** Marketing claims about AI. Overstating what your product's AI does can be deceptive under consumer protection law, and for public companies or companies raising money, it can be a securities issue. Keep AI claims accurate and supportable.

**THEO:** Fourth.

**HANA:** Personal data in AI tools. Staff putting customer, patient or employee data into AI tools without the right agreements. That's a privacy problem under GDPR, HIPAA and state laws. The fix is approved tools and clear rules.

**THEO:** And fifth?

**HANA:** Vendors. Assuming that because you bought the AI from a reputable vendor, the vendor is responsible for compliance. Under most laws, the company that uses AI to make decisions has its own obligations. The vendor's compliance helps, but it doesn't replace yours. Ask vendors for their documentation, testing results and contractual commitments, and keep your own records.

---

### The private equity angle

**THEO:** Let's talk specifically about private equity, since many listeners are investors.

**HANA:** Private equity firms face AI regulation at two levels. At the fund level, they use AI themselves: for deal sourcing, diligence, portfolio monitoring, investor reporting. Those uses raise questions under securities law, privacy law and their own fiduciary duties to investors. And at the portfolio level, they own companies that are subject to all the laws we've described.

**THEO:** And investors are asking.

**HANA:** Limited partners, the investors in the funds, increasingly ask about AI in their due diligence questionnaires: how the firm uses AI, how it governs AI in the portfolio, and what risks it's monitoring. Some large investors have published expectations on responsible AI. Our sister show, Talking AI to the Board and LPs, goes into how to answer those questions well.

**THEO:** What's the fund's role in portfolio compliance?

**HANA:** It varies by firm, but the good practice we see is a light framework at the fund level: a model AI policy that portfolio companies can adapt, a standard AI inventory template, a short list of vetted vendors with good terms, a requirement that portfolio boards review AI risk periodically, and expertise the companies can call on. The company remains responsible for its own compliance, but the fund makes it easier to do well.

**THEO:** And at exit?

**HANA:** Buyers will diligence AI governance. A portfolio company with an inventory, a policy, tiered controls, documentation for its higher-risk uses and a clean record will sell more easily. One without them will face questions, price chips, and insurance exclusions.

### A first-year plan for a mid-sized company

**THEO:** Let's make this practical. A mid-sized company, say five hundred people, uses AI in a dozen ways, and has done nothing formal on governance. What should it do in its first year?

**HANA:** Quarter one: build the inventory and the policy. Find every AI use, including the AI features in existing software, and tier them. Write a one-page policy on acceptable use, approved tools and how to get new uses approved. Give everyone an approved AI tool with proper data protections, so they stop using unapproved ones. And start AI literacy training, which, if you operate in the EU, the AI Act expects anyway.

**THEO:** Quarter two.

**HANA:** Focus on the medium and high-risk uses. For each, a named owner, a short risk assessment, and the controls the tier requires. Check the specific laws for any high-risk uses, especially hiring. Review the chatbot and other customer-facing AI for accuracy and disclosure. And review vendor contracts for AI terms.

**THEO:** Quarter three.

**HANA:** Build the routine. Monitoring for customer-facing and high-risk AI. An incident process that covers AI. A quarterly report to the leadership team and an annual one to the board. And align the whole thing with a framework, NIST or ISO, so it's structured and defensible.

**THEO:** And quarter four?

**HANA:** Review and prepare for what's coming. Check which new laws take effect next year in your states and markets. If you have EU high-risk uses, plan the documentation work for the December 2027 deadline. Decide whether ISO 42001 certification makes sense, which it often does for companies that sell AI to enterprises. And run an internal audit of how well the policy is actually followed.

**THEO:** That sounds manageable.

**HANA:** It is, for most companies. It's a few people's part-time effort, with some legal help. The companies that find it overwhelming are the ones that try to do everything at once or apply high-risk controls to low-risk uses.

### How to keep up

**THEO:** Last practical question for today. How does a mid-sized company keep up with all this without a team of lawyers?

**HANA:** Four habits. First, know your categories. If you have an inventory with each use tiered and tagged by what it does, like hiring, customer chat, credit or marketing content, you only need to track laws that hit those categories. That cuts the reading by ninety percent.

**THEO:** Second.

**HANA:** Use your advisors efficiently. Most law firms publish free updates on AI law. Pick one or two that cover your main jurisdictions and sectors, and have someone read them monthly. Your outside counsel can do a short annual review of your inventory against new laws, which is cheaper than reacting to each one.

**THEO:** Third.

**HANA:** Ask your vendors. Major AI and software vendors track the laws that affect their products and often publish compliance documentation. Ask them, at renewal, what's changed and what they need from you.

**THEO:** And fourth?

**HANA:** Put it on the calendar. A quarterly fifteen-minute item at the leadership meeting: what new rules take effect next quarter, which of our uses do they touch, and who owns the response. And an annual item for the board. That rhythm is usually enough for a company that isn't in a heavily regulated sector.

### Questions we keep hearing

**THEO:** Let's do some quick questions. If we only use AI tools from big vendors, are we covered?

**HANA:** Partly. The vendor carries obligations for the tool itself, especially under the EU AI Act if it's the provider. But you carry obligations for how you use it, especially for high-risk uses, and for transparency, data protection and anti-discrimination. Vendor documentation helps you meet yours. It doesn't replace them.

**THEO:** We're a US company with no EU customers. Can we ignore the EU AI Act?

**HANA:** Probably, as a legal matter, if you truly have no EU market presence and your AI outputs aren't used in the EU. But many US companies have more EU exposure than they think, through European customers, employees or subsidiaries. And the Act's concepts are influencing US state laws and customer expectations. It's worth understanding even if it doesn't apply.

**THEO:** Is the federal push against state laws a reason to wait?

**HANA:** Not a good one. State laws are in force unless and until a court or Congress says otherwise, and the litigation could take years. And most of what state laws ask for is good governance practice anyway. Waiting is a bet that could leave you non-compliant and unprepared.

**THEO:** What's the single most important thing to do first?

**HANA:** The inventory. You can't govern AI you don't know about. Almost every company that does an honest inventory finds uses it didn't know it had.

### Takeaways

**THEO:** Sum up the map.

**HANA:** One: the rules reach operators, not just AI builders, through what you use AI for, through existing laws, through customers and buyers, and through board duties.

**THEO:** Two.

**HANA:** The EU AI Act is the most comprehensive law. Prohibitions and AI literacy have applied since February 2025, general-purpose model rules since August 2025, transparency since August 2026, and the high-risk rules now from December 2027 for stand-alone systems and August 2028 for AI in regulated products.

**THEO:** Three.

**HANA:** The US has no comprehensive federal law, a federal government pushing against state regulation, and a growing patchwork of state laws on consequential decisions, employment, privacy, disclosure and frontier models. Existing federal laws still apply.

**THEO:** Four.

**HANA:** Standards like the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO 42001 give you a structure that works whatever the laws say, and some laws reward following them.

**THEO:** And five.

**HANA:** Across all of it, the asks are consistent: inventory, risk assessment, proportionate controls, human oversight, transparency, data governance, security and accountability. Tier your uses so the low-risk path stays fast and the high-risk path is careful.
