### Cold Open: The Metric That Could Break a Supplier

**MORGAN:** Calder's AP learning loop worked. First-pass match is now sixty-seven percent, verified end-to-end touchless is forty-nine percent, supplier inquiries have fallen to seven hundred a month, and accrual variance is down to four-point-five percent. Treasury finally trusts more of the invoice-readiness data. Then the board asks the obvious question: if the system is healthier, why not extend every supplier by six days and collect the cash target immediately?

**KEVIN:** Because a six-day average sounds clean only if you ignore who carries those six days. Two suppliers make safety-critical assemblies for Calder. Both are growing, both have thin liquidity, and one already funds raw materials before Calder confirms demand. A blanket extension would make our board metric while increasing the probability of a production interruption.

**MORGAN:** Good. That is the consequence of becoming operationally fluent. You can now see that DPO is not a knob attached to one homogeneous liability. It is the weighted outcome of thousands of promises to suppliers with different economics, criticality, bargaining power, dispute histories, and access to capital.

**KEVIN:** So today's decision is not whether higher DPO is good. Season One settled the cash arithmetic. Today's decision is which invoices should be paid when, and what evidence must govern that choice.

**MORGAN:** Exactly. At month twelve, Calder has spent five-point-seven million dollars of the approved seven-point-five million. Finance has validated two-point-nine million of recurring annual benefit. DPO has moved from forty-two to forty-six days, which releases about thirteen-point-two million dollars of working capital on one-point-two billion of annual supplier disbursements. The thirteen-week supplier-cash forecast error has improved from fourteen percent to eight percent. Those are real outcomes, but only the recurring benefit belongs in the recurring benefit column. The thirteen-point-two million is a one-time working-capital release.

**KEVIN:** And we still owe the board two more DPO days without destabilizing the supply base.

### The Six-Metric CFO Pack

**MORGAN:** Put six metrics on the page, Kevin. No decorative dashboard.

**KEVIN:** First, DPO at forty-six days against a month-eighteen target of forty-eight. Second, thirteen-week supplier-cash forecast error at eight percent against a target of six. Third, on-time payment at ninety-one percent on the way to ninety-six. Fourth, critical-supplier late-payment incidents: three this quarter, and the target is zero. Fifth, recurring benefit validated at two-point-nine million. Sixth, cumulative program spend at five-point-seven million.

**MORGAN:** Why include on-time payment when you are trying to pay later?

**KEVIN:** Because later within an agreed term is policy; late beyond the agreed term is failure. DPO can rise because we stop paying unnecessarily early, because we negotiate different terms, because we choose supplier-finance arrangements, or because we simply become delinquent. Only the first three can be legitimate. A DPO result without the on-time-payment guardrail hides bad execution.

**MORGAN:** And why count critical-supplier incidents rather than an average supplier-satisfaction score?

**KEVIN:** Averages dilute asymmetric risk. One interrupted sole-source supplier can erase years of payment savings. I need a red metric that can stop the program, not a green average that reassures us after damage occurs.

**MORGAN:** What is missing from this pack?

**KEVIN:** The portfolio detail sits underneath it: approved invoices by due-date bucket, disputed versus clean liabilities, supplier criticality, available discounts, funding cost, forecast confidence, and cash constraints. The six-metric pack is the executive surface, not the decision engine.

### Start With a Liability-Readiness Forecast

**MORGAN:** Build the thirteen-week forecast from the spend-to-cash states Calder defined in Episode Eleven. What goes into week one?

**KEVIN:** Posted and approved invoices due in week one are the highest-confidence obligation. Approved but not posted items come next, adjusted for posting failure. Matched invoices awaiting final approval get a probability based on historical approval behavior. Goods received without invoice create an expected liability using receipt and contract data. Purchase commitments without receipt are lower confidence and should be scenario-weighted, not presented as payable fact.

**MORGAN:** And disputed invoices?

**KEVIN:** They remain visible but separate. A dispute changes expected timing; it does not make the liability vanish. We show the gross amount, the disputed amount, the likely resolution date, the owner, and a confidence range. That allows treasury to see both the contractual due date and the operational expectation.

**MORGAN:** Calder used to export the AP aging report every Friday and call it a cash forecast. Why was the error fourteen percent?

**KEVIN:** The aging report looked backward from posted invoices. It missed received-but-not-invoiced liabilities, approvals likely to clear, contract milestones, recurring service invoices, disputed timing, and payment batches already scheduled outside the ERP extract. It also treated duplicate supplier records as separate relationships. The forecast became more accurate when supplier identity, buying commitments, receipts, AP states, and payment schedules shared definitions.

**MORGAN:** Do not promise that one application is a complete treasury workstation.

**KEVIN:** Right. Treasury still owns liquidity policy, bank relationships, financing, investments, and the enterprise cash position. Medius can improve the spend-side evidence: invoice readiness, planned payment timing, execution status, and reconciliation. The treasury system consumes that evidence alongside payroll, tax, debt, customer cash, and other flows.

**MORGAN:** What operating cadence keeps the forecast honest?

**KEVIN:** Every Monday, AP certifies the near-term readiness states. Procurement explains material changes in contract terms and critical suppliers. Treasury publishes liquidity constraints and funding cost. Controllers review unusual cutoffs and supplier-finance classifications. On Wednesday, owners resolve the largest variances. On Friday, the team measures forecast error by cause, not just total dollars. Every error must land in a taxonomy: missing commitment, late receipt, delayed approval, dispute, supplier master issue, schedule override, execution failure, or model error.

### The Portfolio Is Not One DPO Number

**MORGAN:** Segment Calder's suppliers in a way that changes a payment decision.

**KEVIN:** I would begin with four dimensions. Criticality measures the operational consequence of disruption. Supplier liquidity estimates whether delayed cash creates fragility. Commercial leverage reflects alternatives and negotiated rights. Transaction confidence distinguishes clean, accepted obligations from disputes or uncertain data.

**MORGAN:** Give me the resulting policies.

**KEVIN:** A critical, liquidity-constrained supplier with clean invoices belongs in a protect lane: pay on the agreed date, consider earlier payment if the resilience value exceeds the cost, and never extend terms through a unilateral campaign. A noncritical, well-capitalized supplier with short legacy terms belongs in a renegotiate lane: align terms to the category standard at renewal and consolidate spend if that improves leverage. A supplier offering a high, credible discount belongs in a yield lane when the annualized net return exceeds Calder's hurdle after operational cost and risk. A disputed or suspicious obligation belongs in a hold-and-resolve lane, not a working-capital lane.

**MORGAN:** What about the small supplier that is not operationally critical but is financially vulnerable?

**KEVIN:** Supplier resilience is not identical to criticality. Calder can adopt a small-supplier prompt-payment policy because harm, reputation, and administrative escalation still have cost. The policy should use objective criteria and be approved by procurement, finance, and legal, not improvised by AP clerks.

**MORGAN:** What is the dangerous shortcut?

**KEVIN:** Assigning every supplier a black-box risk score and letting software pick the date. Scores can inform a decision, but payment authority needs explainable policy inputs, reliable identity, contractual boundaries, exception ownership, and the ability to revoke an automated rule.

**MORGAN:** Now test the segmentation against a real portfolio rather than four tidy labels.

**KEVIN:** Calder takes the top two hundred supplier relationships by cash value and adds every sole-source or safety-critical supplier regardless of size. Those suppliers receive named relationship owners and individual policy review. The remaining population is governed by rules, but the rules use category, country, contract, size, risk, dispute behavior, and dependency rather than spend alone.

**MORGAN:** Why include low-spend sole sources?

**KEVIN:** Because annual spend is a poor proxy for disruption cost. A sixty-thousand-dollar calibration provider can stop a production line carrying millions of dollars of weekly margin. The matrix needs a consequence field: revenue at risk, replacement lead time, qualification requirement, customer commitment, and safety impact.

**MORGAN:** What evidence would justify extending a term?

**KEVIN:** A valid contract or negotiated amendment, supplier acknowledgment, a peer or category reference used only as context, stable supplier-health evidence, alternate supply where appropriate, and a quantified benefit that exceeds implementation and relationship cost. The change must propagate to purchase orders, invoices, scheduling, forecasts, and supplier communication. A term in a negotiation tracker that never reaches transactions is not working-capital policy.

**MORGAN:** And what evidence justifies protecting a term?

**KEVIN:** Critical dependency, constrained supplier liquidity, documented strategic commitment, statutory or contractual requirement, material switching cost, or evidence that extension would be priced back into the relationship. The protected decision receives an expiry date. Protection is a deliberate investment, not a permanent favor no one revisits.

**MORGAN:** How do disputes alter the analysis?

**KEVIN:** Calder must separate genuine commercial dispute from administrative blockage. If Calder failed to record a receipt or routed an approval to an absent manager, withholding payment is not leverage; it is self-inflicted delinquency. If quantity, quality, price, or contractual performance is disputed, the uncontested portion should be considered separately where the contract allows. The dispute queue needs amount, age, cause, owner, evidence, next action, and expected resolution date.

**MORGAN:** What would a bad product metric reward?

**KEVIN:** A payment optimizer rewarded only for DPO could delay clean invoices, exploit internal errors, concentrate pain on suppliers with the least bargaining power, and celebrate the resulting cash. A discount optimizer rewarded only for gross savings could drain liquidity and accelerate invoices likely to receive credits. A good objective function needs financial value plus hard constraints for terms, supplier resilience, on-time payment, data confidence, approval authority, and liquidity.

**MORGAN:** Can Calder fully automate the decision?

**KEVIN:** It can automate low-risk execution inside a reviewed policy envelope. For example, schedule a clean invoice on its contractual due date when identity is verified, the obligation is approved, no dispute exists, and forecast data reconciles. It can recommend an early payment when the hurdle clears. It should escalate a novel term, a critical-supplier exception, a sudden risk change, or a recommendation near the boundary. The authority to change policy remains human and named.

**MORGAN:** That distinction matters. Automation performs an authorized decision repeatedly. It does not invent the company's appetite for liquidity, supplier harm, or accounting risk.

**KEVIN:** We also need a feedback rule for prices. If a supplier accepts longer terms but raises unit prices at renewal, procurement must connect that increase to the payment decision. Otherwise treasury reports a cash win while the income statement absorbs the cost elsewhere. The relationship record should compare pre-change and post-change price, service, minimum order, lead time, and credit terms, with category and volume effects separated.

**MORGAN:** Good. The policy ledger therefore records both the cash-timing result and the commercial counter-effect. A day of DPO is not valuable if Calder buys it back through higher total cost or brittle supply.

### The Discount Hurdle Model

**MORGAN:** Calder has a supplier offering a one-point-two percent discount for payment twenty-five days earlier. Treasury's short-term marginal funding cost is six-point-five percent. Is the discount attractive?

**KEVIN:** The simple annualized gross yield is roughly one-point-two percent divided by twenty-five days and multiplied by three hundred sixty-five, or about seventeen-and-a-half percent. That clears a six-point-five percent funding cost.

**MORGAN:** And your answer is incomplete.

**KEVIN:** We need the net eligible amount, supplier acceptance, transaction fees, tax treatment, operational workload, payment failure risk, and whether early payment gives up another contractual right. We also need to avoid counting a discount on spend that Calder would not otherwise buy. If the invoice is disputed, accelerating it to chase yield would be absurd.

**MORGAN:** How does the model treat excess cash versus borrowed cash?

**KEVIN:** The hurdle changes with the actual alternative use of funds. If cash would sit idle, the hurdle may be the short-term after-tax return plus a liquidity premium. If Calder must draw a revolver, use the incremental all-in borrowing cost. If the company is near a covenant threshold, preserve liquidity even when a discount looks attractive in isolation. Treasury owns that rate and can change it as conditions move.

**MORGAN:** What should the product show the operator?

**KEVIN:** Not a badge saying save money. It should show the invoice amount, days accelerated, gross discount, annualized gross yield, funding hurdle, net expected value, liquidity consumption, supplier segment, and policy result. If any required input is stale, the recommendation expires. The user should see why an invoice qualified and which authority approved the rule.

**MORGAN:** And if Calder pays one invoice early while stretching another invoice from the same supplier?

**KEVIN:** The policy must operate at relationship level, not line level. Otherwise the optimization engine creates contradictory behavior and supplier confusion. Aggregate exposure, disputes, credits, financing arrangements, and contractual terms before recommending timing.

### Supplier Finance Without Accounting Theater

**MORGAN:** There is another route: a finance provider pays the supplier early and Calder pays later. What must the operator understand beyond the commercial pitch?

**KEVIN:** The economic substance, the balance-sheet presentation, the cash-flow effect, the concentration of finance providers, and the liquidity risk if the facility disappears. The arrangement cannot be used to make trade payables or operating cash flow look healthier than the underlying economics justify.

**MORGAN:** Name the primary accounting source.

**KEVIN:** The International Accounting Standards Board amended IAS Seven and IFRS Seven for supplier-finance transparency. The requirements call for disclosure of terms and conditions, the liabilities included in the arrangements, where those liabilities sit on the balance sheet, payment-due-date ranges, and liquidity-risk information. The point is not that the software decides the accounting. The point is that the operating system must preserve the data controllers need to make and support that judgment.

**MORGAN:** So what must Calder capture?

**KEVIN:** The supplier, finance provider, enrolled invoices, date the supplier was paid, date Calder owes the provider, original trade term, extended term, liability classification, payment range, limits, utilization, and cancellation rights. We also keep an audit trail of changes and reconcile enrolled liabilities to the ledger and cash forecast.

**MORGAN:** What is the guardrail?

**KEVIN:** No working-capital claim from supplier finance enters the board pack until controllership confirms classification and treasury models withdrawal risk. No product message calls every extension free cash. The arrangement may improve resilience and timing, but it creates dependencies that must be visible.

### The Three-Option Gate

**MORGAN:** Calder has three credible options. State them fairly.

**KEVIN:** Option one is stretch all eligible suppliers by six days through a broad terms campaign. It is fast, simple, and likely to hit the headline target. It also ignores supplier liquidity, may trigger price increases or disruption, and could turn on-time payments into strategic delinquency.

**KEVIN:** Option two is maximize every available early-payment discount. It can create attractive yield and support suppliers, but it consumes liquidity, can reduce DPO, and may optimize discounts while undermining the board's cash objective.

**KEVIN:** Option three is a segmented portfolio policy. Protect critical and vulnerable suppliers, use discounts only above a treasury-owned hurdle, renegotiate robust noncritical relationships at natural commercial events, resolve disputed items on evidence, and use supplier finance selectively with accounting transparency. It is harder to operate and slower to explain, but it aligns cash, yield, resilience, and control.

**MORGAN:** Which option is obviously foolish?

**KEVIN:** None in all circumstances. A distressed company might need a broad preservation action with executive approval. A cash-rich company could rationally maximize discounts. But Calder's mandate explicitly requires six DPO days without critical-supplier disruption. That makes portfolio segmentation the fit-for-purpose choice.

**MORGAN:** Guardrail?

**KEVIN:** No unilateral term extension for a supplier classified critical or liquidity-constrained. No early-pay recommendation below the treasury hurdle after fees and risk. **MORGAN:** Stop condition?

**KEVIN:** Suspend the affected payment rule if a critical supplier reports credible liquidity distress, if on-time payment in a segment falls below ninety percent for two consecutive weekly runs, if forecast error worsens above ten percent for two weeks, or if the source data loses reconciliation to approved invoices and the ledger. Manual execution remains available while the root cause is investigated.

### Four-Day Result, Not the Finale

**MORGAN:** What happens by month twelve?

**KEVIN:** Calder removes habitual early payment where no discount or resilience rationale exists, aligns terms during renewals for well-capitalized noncritical suppliers, and accelerates selected invoices where the discount clears the hurdle. DPO reaches forty-six days. On one-point-two billion dollars of annual supplier disbursements, four additional days equal one-point-two billion divided by three hundred sixty-five multiplied by four: about thirteen-point-two million dollars released.

**MORGAN:** Say the classification again.

**KEVIN:** It is a one-time working-capital release. It is not recurring EBITDA, not sourcing savings, and not program payback revenue. It improves the cash position relative to baseline while the higher payable balance is sustained.

**MORGAN:** What happens to recurring benefit?

**KEVIN:** Finance validates two-point-nine million dollars of recurring annual benefit cumulatively by month twelve. The bridge is one-point-two million from sourcing and demand, six hundred thousand from contract and guided-buying compliance, nine hundred thousand from AP capacity, one hundred fifty thousand from supplier service, and fifty thousand from net payment economics. The AP lane is unchanged and already fully validated; the six-hundred-thousand increase since Month Ten comes only from the other four lanes. We do not insert the thirteen-point-two million into that bridge.

**MORGAN:** And the two critical suppliers?

**KEVIN:** They remain on protected terms. One receives an optional early-payment offer that clears Calder's hurdle and helps fund raw material. The other gets improved forecast visibility and a contract review rather than an extension. Calder gives up a fraction of theoretical DPO to avoid asymmetric disruption risk.

**MORGAN:** That is portfolio optimization. The aggregate target survives because you make different decisions for different relationships.

### The Working-Capital Bridge

**KEVIN:** Walk me through the bridge the CFO should see.

**MORGAN:** Start with baseline payable days at forty-two and baseline annual disbursements at one-point-two billion. Show each policy lane separately: removal of unjustified early payment, negotiated term alignment, schedule accuracy, discount acceleration, protected-supplier exceptions, dispute resolution, and any supplier-finance effect. For each lane show gross day movement, dollar effect, confidence, owner, and whether the movement has actually appeared in the balance sheet and cash flow.

**KEVIN:** Why not simply show forty-two to forty-six and thirteen-point-two million?

**MORGAN:** Because the bridge explains causality and durability. If two days came from invoices stuck in a broken approval queue, the cash release is not controlled and may reverse suddenly. If one day came from seasonal mix, it may not persist. If a negotiated term has not reached invoices, it is implemented intent, not realized movement. The CFO needs to distinguish policy from accident.

**KEVIN:** We should also reconcile the cash release to the forecast and ledger.

**MORGAN:** Yes. The spend-to-cash contract links approved liabilities, scheduled dates, payment execution, bank confirmation, and ledger posting. The bridge cannot be a spreadsheet detached from the transactions that produced it.
