## Episode 2: "Ocean Parkway"

[NARRATOR] Before Roland Voss was an architect, before he was a husband and a father and a youth sports coach and the kind of neighbor who shovels the elderly woman's walk across the street without being asked, he was a beach kid.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He grew up on the south shore of Long Island in the nineteen-seventies and early eighties, in a modest house in a neighborhood where summer meant the ocean and winter meant watching that same ocean empty out - the parking lots gated, the concession stands shuttered, the long stretches of access road running parallel to the highway vacant and gray and windswept and belonging, for those months, to almost no one.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] His father worked in construction and taught him how things were built. His mother kept the books at a local dentist's office and taught him, by example, precision and patience. He was a quiet child, not troubled, not conspicuous in any direction. He liked to be outside. He preferred the margins of places to their centers - the service areas behind the dune lines rather than the beach itself, the maintenance roads that most people didn't know existed, the sections of the barrier island complex that were technically off-limits to the public and were therefore, in practice, empty.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] By the time he was a teenager he knew every parking lot along that stretch of coast. Every access trail. Every service road that ran behind the dunes when the park was officially closed. He knew where the cameras were positioned - the handful of fixed installations at the main entry points - and more importantly where they were not. He knew which sections of the marsh grass grew tallest, where the sight lines from the highway were blocked by natural terrain features, where you could be for hours and not be seen by anyone on the road.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He knew this landscape the way children know the landscapes of their childhood - not as facts but as something bodily. Something that lives in the muscles and the feet, in the part of the mind that doesn't have to think to navigate. Where things are. How they connect. Where you can go without being found.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In his early twenties he takes a summer job at the beach complex on the barrier island. Parking enforcement, ticketing, light maintenance. He drives the service roads in a department vehicle when the park gates are closed to the public. He has key access to the maintenance road network. He learns the camera system in operational detail. He learns the schedules - when the patrol car comes through, how often, at what intervals. He learns when the parking areas are observed and when they are not.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He is in his mid-twenties when he leaves the beach job and starts architecture school. He carries this knowledge with him. It is not relevant to architecture school. It is not relevant, for the next several years, to anything in his visible life. He builds a practice, marries, moves into a house in Massapequa Park about fifteen miles from the barrier island corridor, has a daughter, coaches sports, builds the architecture that a life like this requires.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The knowledge sits in him. Patient. Waiting.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In 2003, he writes a letter to the local newspaper. The county has proposed lighting upgrades to the access road parking areas along the barrier island corridor. Improved safety and security, the proposal says. A letter appears in the following week's edition opposing the measure, arguing that the lights would disturb wildlife and compromise the natural character of the landscape. The letter is signed with his full name and his address in Massapequa Park.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He wants those lots to stay dark. He is willing to say so in print. He has been operating in those dark lots for years at this point. He is confident enough to advocate, in public, for the conditions that make his operation possible. That confidence is not arrogance, or at least not only arrogance. It is the product of more than a decade of total impunity. He has never been caught. He has never come close to being caught. The world has given him no reason to believe he will be.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] When Carol Marsh finds this letter in the second week of the task force investigation, she reads it twice and then sits very still at her desk for a moment.

[MARSH] He wanted those lots to stay dark. He made a public argument for it. He signed his name to it. You have to sit with that for a minute. Because what it tells you, beyond the obvious operational significance, is something about who this person is internally. The certainty. The complete absence of any sense that this might come back to him. Twenty years of operation and not a single moment of visible alarm.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] That certainty is actually useful to an investigation, in retrospect. Because it meant he did things that a less certain person wouldn't do. He went back to the site repeatedly, for years after the bodies were found. He wrote the letter to the editor. He maintained his patterns and his routines without significant deviation. He felt safe, and feeling safe makes people legible in ways that paranoia would prevent. We could read him because he didn't think we were reading.

[NARRATOR] In the weeks following the discovery of the bodies, while the initial investigation is surging with resources and generating its first enormous volume of tips and leads, Danny Reese begins a specific piece of work that will become the analytical backbone of the eventual case.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He calls it the geographic architecture. Not a map of where the bodies were found - that work is well underway, managed by the state police forensic team. What Danny is building is different: a spatial analysis of the entire behavioral pattern. Where each victim was last seen. Where her phone last connected to a cell tower. Where her last financial transaction was processed. Where her last contact with family or friends took place. And what the collective geometry of all those last-known-locations tells you about where the killer was when he found them, and by extension where he came from.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] This is slow, careful, systematic work. It requires pulling records from multiple jurisdictions across multiple years. It requires legal process for some of the financial data. It requires cross-checking inconsistent accounts and assigning probability weights to each data point based on the reliability of its source. It requires, above all, the discipline to resist the temptation to fit the data to an existing theory rather than letting the data build its own picture.

[REESE] The discipline question is real. When you have a theory - when you think you know roughly what happened - the data looks different than it does when you have no theory and you're just looking at it. You start to see the points that confirm the theory and discount the points that don't. That's human. It's very hard to prevent. My approach has always been to do the data work first, completely, before I allow myself to have a theory. Build the picture and then see what it shows, rather than having a picture and looking for data to fill it in.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] With Deadwater, the picture took weeks to build. And what it showed was extremely consistent and extremely clear. Every victim's last confirmed contact originated within a roughly twenty-two-mile radius centered on the south shore corridor. Without exception. I tested this multiple ways, varied the parameters, used different subsets of the data. It always came back to the same circle.

[NARRATOR] The circle doesn't tell you who. A twenty-two-mile radius in Nassau and Suffolk counties contains close to a million and a half people. But it tells you something important: the killer is not ranging widely. He is operating locally. His center of gravity - his home, his workplace, his daily territory - is inside that circle. And when you add the behavioral evidence of deep familiarity with the specific dump site, you can narrow the circle further. The geographic center of maximum probability - the location that best fits all the data simultaneously - is approximately fifteen miles from the barrier island.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] Massapequa Park is fifteen miles from the dump site.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese notes this. He notes it in the official case file and he notes it in the small handwritten notebook that he maintains alongside the official system, the notebook he calls his thread book, in which he records the connections and observations that don't have a clear formal home but that he is unwilling to discard.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The Massapequa Park notation goes into the thread book in the third month of the investigation. It is not, at that point, connected to any specific person. It is a geographic observation about probability. It sits in the book through the following twelve years, waiting for something to connect to it.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] The identification of the ten victims proceeds through the first months at a pace that is simultaneously as fast as the forensic processes allow and agonizingly slow for the families who are waiting. Some identifications come quickly - fingerprints matched, dental records confirmed, missing persons files cross-referenced. Others take months. Two will not be complete until after the arrest.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Each identification changes the case in a specific way. Before identification, a victim is a forensic profile: sex, approximate age, estimated time of death, physical evidence of cause of death and manner of placement. After identification, she becomes a person with a history that can be investigated. People who knew her. Places she was last seen. Phones she used, credit cards she carried, networks she moved through. All of that becomes available when a name is attached to the remains.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] It also becomes personal. It becomes individual. It is the difference between a category and a life.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Sandra is identified six weeks after the discovery. She is thirty-one years old. The forensic timeline puts her death in approximately 1993, making her the earliest confirmed victim by a considerable margin. This pushes the established timeline of the killings back seventeen years from the discovery, which forces a fundamental reassessment of who investigators are looking for.

[MARSH] When the Sandra identification came in with a 1993 timeline, the first thing I thought was: seventeen years. He has been doing this for seventeen years. This is not someone who started recently and escalated quickly. This is someone who built this into their life, who incorporated it into their regular existence, who maintained it alongside marriage and employment and community membership for seventeen years without anyone around them seeing anything wrong. That is not a psychological profile I had dealt with before. It required a complete recalibration of how I thought about the suspect.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Sandra's daughters are in their late twenties when they receive the notification that their mother's identity has been established. The older daughter has a single memory of her mother that she has held for seventeen years: the smell of a particular soap. That is what she says when Donna Kelsey calls her a few weeks after the identification, after the daughter has found the family advocacy network through an online search and reached out. That is the memory she leads with. The smell of a soap.

[DONNA] She told me about that memory in our first conversation. She said she doesn't know the brand, she has never been able to find it again, but sometimes she walks past someone on the street who uses it and her whole body responds. She said she used to go to department stores and walk through the soap section smelling things, trying to find it. She stopped doing that at some point. She said she couldn't remember when she stopped.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I think about that a lot. The specific grief of losing a parent young - losing them to violence, to absence, to years of not knowing. How that grief doesn't look like most grief. How it doesn't have an event to organize itself around. It's diffuse and it's carried and it shows up in unexpected places, like the soap section of a department store.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In the second year of the investigation, a decision is made that will shape the case for the following decade. It is made at a level of institutional authority above Carol Marsh's rank, and it involves the allocation of resources as the initial surge of investigative momentum begins to slow.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The decision: scale back the field investigation - the geographic analysis work, the barrier island employment history research, the physical background checks on persons of interest in the target area - and concentrate remaining resources on the digital forensics track. The logic is that the killer contacted his victims online. The digital trail, if it can be followed far enough, is the most direct path to identification.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Carol Marsh argues against the reallocation. She believes the geographic investigation has not been exhausted, that there is specific research into the barrier island employment and access history that has not yet been completed. She puts her objection in writing. She is told the decision is final.

[MARSH] I was wrong about where the geographic thread would eventually lead. I was right that it hadn't been exhausted. In my mind, the unexplored geographic work was about the barrier island employment records - who had worked there, who had formal access, who had the institutional knowledge of the layout that the crime scenes suggested. What I didn't anticipate was that the geographic connection would come through a completely different avenue: a parking citation, a letter to the editor, a woman walking a dog. The geography was always the key. I just didn't know which specific door it would open.

[NARRATOR] The digital forensics work in those years produces one significant lead that will eventually become part of the case. The investigators pull, through a multi-step legal process, burner phone records connected to several of the victims in the period before their disappearances. The analysis reveals a pattern: multiple victims, across multiple years, were contacted by unregistered numbers - prepaid SIM cards, different numbers each time - but traceable, through hardware-level network metadata, to the same physical handset.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The handset is never registered. Its SIM cards are discarded after use. But its hardware signature appears in the cell tower records with a consistency that is unmistakable once you know to look for it. And the cell towers it connects to most frequently cluster in two geographic areas: the barrier island corridor where the bodies were found, and a residential cluster in Nassau County approximately fifteen miles from the dump site.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Massapequa Park is in that residential cluster.

[REESE] When the cell cluster analysis came back, I pulled out my thread book and looked at the Massapequa Park notation. And I added a second note underneath it: cell cluster. Massapequa Park. The circle is getting smaller. I don't have a name yet. But the circle is getting smaller.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The investigators flag this as a high-priority lead and request resources to pursue it. The resources are not forthcoming. The department, under the leadership that will eventually lead to Gary Holden's arrest, is not in a position to commit additional investigative capacity to a cold case that has generated no new physical evidence in two years. The lead is acknowledged, documented, and sits.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] That lead sat for years. It sat until the task force was formed and we had the capacity to do something with it. When we finally did do something with it - when we ran the full analysis with modern tools and a proper team - it pointed exactly where I had thought it would. But we lost years. Real years. That's not an abstraction. That's time.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] Meanwhile, Roland Voss is living his life with the complete, settled confidence of a man who has never been caught and has no reason to believe he ever will be.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He commutes to Manhattan five days a week on the same train. He sits in the same section of the same car. He reads on the train - architectural journals, professional publications, sometimes a novel. He arrives at his office before most of his colleagues. He is there, in his chair, working, when the rest of the staff filters in around him. He is methodical about his projects in a way that his clients consistently describe as exceptional. He does not miss deadlines. He does not lose track of details. He manages complexity with what one colleague, interviewed after his arrest, calls "a kind of serenity that most architects never develop."

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Serenity. The word keeps appearing in the accounts of people who knew him professionally. Serenity. Steadiness. Patience under pressure. The ability to absorb a difficult situation and continue performing without visible distress.

[REESE] These qualities - the serenity, the patience, the apparent imperviousness to stress - are not a personality type. They are a trained behavior. They are what develops in a person who has spent two decades maintaining an extreme double life under continuous existential pressure. The self-control required to do that, day in and day out, for twenty years, does not stay in a box. It radiates into every part of how you operate. It becomes who you appear to be, to everyone around you. His colleagues found it admirable. I find it, looking back, one of the most disturbing things about this whole case.

[NARRATOR] At home in Massapequa Park, the same presentation. Quiet. Considerate. Reliable. The man who shovels the walk, who helps carry the groceries, who shows up when he says he will and does what he says he'll do. His wife works and his daughter grows up and his neighbors wave to him from their driveways and nobody looks at him twice in twenty-five years.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He follows the investigation from the first day it becomes public news. This is reconstructed years later from his digital records, when the task force finally has access to them. From the initial discovery through the years of reduced public coverage through the announcement of the task force, he monitors every public statement, every press briefing, every piece of journalism about the case. He reads it carefully and repeatedly. He is, in effect, the most well-informed civilian observer of the hunt for himself.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] In the years when the investigation visibly stalls - when there are no press conferences, no significant coverage, nothing to report because there is nothing to report - his digital monitoring activity shows what investigators will later describe as relaxation. Less frequent checking. More time between visits to news sites covering the case. A behavioral signature that reads, in the data, like someone who has been holding their breath for a while and has slowly, carefully, let it out.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He felt safe. He had been safe for a long time and the world kept confirming it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He did not know that Carol Marsh read his case file every year on the anniversary of the discovery.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He did not know that Danny Reese had a thread book with his neighborhood in it.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He did not know that Donna Kelsey was in Albany, year after year, testifying before committees, pushing for the exact legislative reforms that would eventually make it harder for someone like him to stay invisible.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He did not know that Dr. Nina Park was watching the development of forensic genealogy methodology with the patient attention of someone who has a specific piece of evidence in her storage facility that is waiting for exactly that technology to mature.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] He felt safe because the surface of the world showed him safety. Underneath that surface, very quietly and with great patience, the people who were going to find him were working.

[MUSIC STING]

[NARRATOR] In year ten of the investigation, Donna Kelsey's advocacy organization reaches a milestone that she marks with characteristic ambivalence. The Renee Kelsey Missing Persons Coordination Act, the legislation she spent four years pushing through the Albany process, has been in effect for two years and has already resulted in three missing persons cases being connected to active homicide investigations that would otherwise not have been cross-referenced. Three families who might have waited much longer for answers have those answers now because of a law named after a woman who was missing for sixteen months and dead for fourteen of them before anyone connected her case to the investigation that should have found her sooner.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Donna marks the milestone by going to dinner with her son Marcus, who is eleven, and her mother, who is seventy-five and still keeps the photograph of Renee on the mantelpiece and calls her daughters every Sunday. They go to the restaurant Renee liked, the Italian place near Donna's mother's house, and they eat the pasta Renee always ordered and they talk about her the way you talk about someone who has been gone a long time - not always sad, not always formal, sometimes just the ordinary way you talk about someone you love who isn't there anymore.

[DONNA] Marcus asked that night why the law had Renee's name on it. He was eleven - he knew the basics of what had happened to his mother, as much as an eleven-year-old can know. I tried to explain what the law did and why it mattered and why naming it after her felt right. And he listened, and when I finished he thought about it for a moment and then he said: so other kids won't lose their moms the same way?

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] I said: that's right. That's exactly right.

[PAUSE]

[DONNA] He went back to his pasta. He's a practical kid. Very much like Renee in that way - he doesn't need things to be more than they are. It's enough that it matters. It's enough that it does something.

## Extended Act: What Geography Knows

[NARRATOR] The geographic analysis of the Deadwater case generates, in Danny Reese's estimation, the single most important document in the pre-task-force phase of the investigation. Not the forensic reports. Not the tip analysis. The spatial map he builds over the course of three months in year one, which establishes with clarity the twenty-two-mile circle and the fifteen-mile center point.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The document matters not because it identifies Roland Voss - it does not, not yet, not for a decade - but because it is correct. It is pointing at the right place from the beginning, waiting for someone to have the resources to look closely enough at what it is pointing at. In year one of the investigation, those resources exist and are not directed at the geographic center. In year twelve, the task force is formed specifically to follow the geographic thread to its conclusion.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Danny Reese shows me the original analysis document in our second interview. He keeps it in a manila folder in his home office. The document is printed on paper from a twelve-year-old printer and has handwritten annotations in the margins - notes added over the years as new data became available and Reese continued refining the analysis on his own time.

[REESE] This document was in the official case file. It was not secret, not buried. Anyone who read the file thoroughly would have seen it. The problem was not that the analysis was unavailable. The problem was that the analysis pointed at a geographic area that we then failed to search with sufficient granularity. We narrowed the suspect pool by geography and then stopped narrowing. We should have kept going.

[PAUSE]

[REESE] When I show the document to new investigators now, as part of training I do occasionally on cold case methodology, the first question they always ask is: why didn't you follow the geographic center more aggressively in year one? And the answer is resources. And resources means a decision that was made above our level, by people who were looking at a budget and a department under significant pressure and making allocation choices that seemed reasonable at the time.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] The answer is always the same. Resources. Which is to say: priority. Which is to say: a judgment about whose case mattered enough to receive what it needed.

[NARRATOR] Dr. Nina Park, who joins the investigation in year three as a forensic consultant and who eventually leads the DNA analysis that breaks the case, has a precise memory of her first read of Reese's geographic document.

[DR. PARK] I read it in my car, in the parking lot outside the SSPD building, the first day I came in as a consultant. I had been given a briefing packet and I read it before going inside. When I got to the geographic analysis, I sat there for about fifteen minutes. Because it was right. The analysis was methodologically correct and the conclusion it pointed at was almost certainly correct. And I thought: if the right analytical tools are available, this case is solvable. The geography is pointing at the answer. You just need the DNA to confirm it.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] I went inside and I found the evidence storage and I looked at the hair sample. I looked at its condition. I looked at the documentation of how it had been preserved. And I thought: this is remarkable. This is a forensic gift. Someone has taken extraordinary care with this evidence and it is ready to be used when the technology exists to use it.

[PAUSE]

[DR. PARK] I wrote a note in my consulting report recommending that the hair be treated as a primary evidence item and that the case be flagged for revisit when forensic genealogy methodology became operationally viable. The note went into the file. It sat there for nine years. And when I came back to it, the hair was exactly where I'd left it.

[NARRATOR] The barrier island corridor - the stretch of road that Roland Voss chose, that he knew, that he returned to for decades - is worth spending a moment with. Not as a crime scene. As a place.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] I have driven it several times while reporting this series. I have walked sections of the access road in different seasons. In the summer it is loud and crowded and full of families doing ordinary summer things. In the winter it is something else entirely - quiet in a way that feels absolute, the marsh grass moving in patterns that have nothing to do with human activity, the water the color of pewter or slate or the specific gray that is its own category of gray.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] There are places in the world that have a quality of indifference to human presence. Places that predate us and will outlast us and that accommodate our presence without being changed by it. This corridor has that quality. The crimes that were committed here, the investigation that followed, the arrest and the plea and the sentencing - the corridor does not seem to have registered any of it. The marsh grass still moves in the same patterns. The water is the same gray.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Roland Voss chose this place because he knew it, because it offered him specific operational advantages, because his relationship to it was specific and long-standing and, in its terrible way, personal. That is what investigators eventually understood and what eventually helped identify him. But the place itself is indifferent to all of that. It does not bear his mark. It does not carry the weight of what happened there in any visible way.

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] I think that is the right thing. The women who were left here deserved to not be defined by having been left here. They are not the marsh. They are not the corridor. They are Sandra and Renee and Ava and the others. They had lives before this place and they deserved lives after it. The place has no claim on them.

[NARRATOR] The letter to the editor that Roland Voss wrote in 2003 is three hundred and forty-one words. Carol Marsh has read it many times since the task force investigation. She has shown it to colleagues. She has referenced it in briefings. She has thought about what it reveals about the person who wrote it.

[MARSH] People ask me if the letter makes me angry. It does. Not in a way that is useful operationally, but yes, it does. The confidence of it. The comfort with which he advocates, in public, for the conditions that make his crimes easier to commit. But what I find more interesting than the anger is what the letter reveals about his psychology.

[PAUSE]

[MARSH] He is not hiding when he writes that letter. He is participating. The two things coexist in him so completely that writing to the editor about wildlife doesn't even register as ironic. It just is who he is.

[PAUSE]

[PAUSE]

[NARRATOR] Three hundred and forty-one words, arguing for darkness on a winter access road. Signed with his full name. Hiding in plain sight, the way he always was.
