### How it got here

**NORA:** Let's do the ownership history.

**JAMES:** The founding story isn't something we could find. The first firm date we have is November 2019, when a company called Crimson Investment sold Milton Industries to Levine Leichtman. That was covered in the private equity trade press, and the law firm Lowenstein Sandler, which represented the seller, put out a deal notice.

**NORA:** And then the acquisitions start.

**JAMES:** They do, and this is where Milton's strategy becomes visible. In February 2022 it bought Global-Flex Manufacturing. In December 2022 it bought ProMAXX Tool. In March 2023 it bought Steck Manufacturing. Those three have dates in the trade press.

**NORA:** And undated ones?

**JAMES:** Quite a few. Thunder Technologies. LTI Tools, which is also known as Lock Technologies, together with Casey Tools. The National-Spencer brand and the Zee Line product line. We couldn't pin dates on any of those, so some may be older and some newer.

**NORA:** That's a lot of names. What's the logic?

**JAMES:** In private equity it's called buy and build. You start with a platform company, in this case Milton, and you add smaller businesses in the same or adjacent niches. Each add-on brings products, customers and sometimes manufacturing capability. If it goes well, the combined company is worth more than the parts, because it can sell more products to each distributor, buy materials in bigger volumes, and share one back office.

**NORA:** And that "if it goes well" is doing a lot of work.

**JAMES:** It always is. We'll come back to it.

**NORA:** Now tell me about this continuation fund.

**JAMES:** On the twenty-first of November 2024, Levine Leichtman closed what it called a five hundred and seventy-five million dollar multi-asset continuation fund, led by the investment firm Hamilton Lane. It moved three companies out of an older fund and into the new vehicle. Milton was one of them. The other two were Blue Ridge ESOP Associates and Resolution Economics, which we cover in other episodes.

**NORA:** Explain a continuation fund like I've never heard the term.

**JAMES:** A private equity fund normally has a fixed life, often around ten years. Investors give it money, the firm buys companies, improves them, and sells them, and the investors get their money back. But sometimes the firm thinks a company still has a lot of growth ahead, and the fund's life is running out. A continuation fund lets the firm sell the company from the old fund to a new vehicle that it also manages. Investors in the old fund can take their money out or roll into the new one, and new investors come in.

**NORA:** So the owner is effectively buying the company from itself.

**JAMES:** With outside investors and an independent price check involved, yes. Levine Leichtman said the aim was more time and additional capital to support the companies' growth. For Milton, that means fresh money, probably for more acquisitions, and a new holding period. Our guess is a few more years before the next sale.

**NORA:** And what does a buyer want to see at the end of that?

**JAMES:** In my view, a company that runs as one. One view of customers, one product catalogue that makes sense, one set of systems, and margins that show the scale is real. That's the operating story Milton has to tell.

### Air power, explained

**NORA:** Before we go further, I need a little education. Why do shops use air tools at all? Why not just electric?

**JAMES:** Great question, and it's general background rather than anything Milton-specific. Air tools have a few big advantages. They're light for the power they deliver, because the motor in the tool is simple and the heavy work of making power happens back at the compressor. They're very durable, because there's not much inside to burn out. They handle heat and constant use well. And in a shop that already has a compressor and air lines, every tool can share one power source.

**NORA:** And the downside?

**JAMES:** You need the whole system. A compressor, which is expensive and noisy. Piping or hoses to get the air where it's needed. Something to clean and dry the air, because compressed air carries moisture and oil that can damage tools. Something to control the pressure, because different tools want different pressures. And connectors at every point where a hose meets a tool or a wall outlet.

**NORA:** So Milton sells the bits in between.

**JAMES:** A lot of them, based on what we've seen. And that's an interesting place to be. A compressor is a big, occasional purchase. Fittings, couplers, gauges and hoses wear out, get lost, get run over by a car, get dropped. They're bought over and over again.

**NORA:** Consumables, basically.

**JAMES:** Close to it. Not quite as frequent as shop rags, but far more frequent than the compressor. And because they're small and relatively cheap, shops tend to buy them from whoever is in front of them, a distributor's van, the parts counter, or an online search.

**NORA:** You mentioned air preparation. What does that mean?

**JAMES:** It's the cleaning and conditioning of the air before it reaches a tool. In general terms, there are three classic devices: a filter to catch water and dirt, a regulator to set the pressure, and a lubricator that adds a fine mist of oil for tools that need it. Engineers sometimes call that set an F R L. Milton's announcement of the Arrow Pneumatics deal specifically talks about air preparation, so that's clearly a category it wants to be stronger in.

**NORA:** And the industrial side? Is that the same products in a different building?

**JAMES:** Partly. A factory may have far bigger air systems, running presses, clamps and assembly tools. The buyer is often a maintenance engineer or a purchasing team rather than a mechanic. They care about uptime and leaks, because a leaking air system is quietly expensive. Compressors use a lot of electricity, and every leak means the compressor runs more.

**NORA:** So there's an energy angle to this too.

**JAMES:** There is, in general. Good fittings that don't leak save money. That's a point a company in this space can make to industrial buyers, though we didn't see Milton making it publicly.

### Customers and the channel

**NORA:** Let's talk about who buys and how. Paint me the picture of an automotive customer.

**JAMES:** Picture an independent repair shop with six bays. The owner is often a former mechanic. The technicians may own their own hand tools, which is common in the American trade, while the shop owns the big equipment like lifts and compressors. Air fittings and hoses are a bit of both. The shop buys some, individual technicians buy others.

**NORA:** And where does a technician buy a coupler?

**JAMES:** In general, a few places. The mobile tool dealer, the van that visits the shop every week, which is how companies like Snap-on famously sell. The local auto parts store. An industrial or automotive distributor. Or online. Our inference is that Milton's brands show up through several of those routes, but we don't know the mix.

**NORA:** Does brand matter for something as small as a fitting?

**JAMES:** More than you'd think. Fittings have to match. There are different styles and sizes of coupler, and a plug from one style won't fit a coupler from another. So a shop tends to standardise on one style, and once it has, it keeps buying the matching parts. That creates a kind of quiet loyalty. Not to a logo exactly, but to a system.

**NORA:** That's a nice moat for a small part.

**JAMES:** It's a modest moat, in my view. Popular styles are made by many companies, so a shop can usually find a compatible part from a competitor. But familiarity, quality and availability all count. If the distributor always has your part in stock, you keep buying it.

**NORA:** And the distributor's view?

**JAMES:** A distributor wants a supplier that's easy to deal with. Good fill rates, meaning when they order a hundred items, all hundred arrive. Clean product data, so they can list items in their own catalogue and website. Fair margins. Reliable lead times. And increasingly, they like suppliers that can cover a whole category, so they don't have to manage ten small vendors.

**NORA:** Which is exactly what a buy-and-build can offer.

**JAMES:** In theory, yes. That's one of the strongest arguments for Milton's strategy. If Milton can say to a distributor, we can supply your couplers, your hoses, your gauges, your air preparation and some specialist tools, on one account, with one delivery, that's valuable.

**NORA:** And if it can't say that?

**JAMES:** Then the distributor is dealing with nine brands that happen to share an owner. Different order systems, different price lists, different part numbers. That's the integration question in one sentence.

**NORA:** What's happening to the repair shops themselves? Is that market changing?

**JAMES:** A few general trends are worth knowing, and none of these are Milton-specific. Cars on the road have been getting older on average in the United States, which tends to mean more repair work for independent shops. At the same time, the trade has talked for years about a shortage of skilled technicians. Put those together and a shop's most precious resource is technician time.

**NORA:** Which brings us back to Rosa and the leaking coupler.

**JAMES:** Exactly. If a technician is the bottleneck, anything that wastes their time is expensive. A leaking fitting, a missing adaptor, a gauge that reads wrong. Shops may not think of air accessories as strategic, but they do notice downtime.

**NORA:** And is the distribution side changing too?

**JAMES:** In general, distribution in many industrial and automotive categories has been consolidating, with larger groups buying up regional players, and those larger groups tend to invest more in digital ordering and catalogue systems. Again, that's background rather than anything we found about Milton's distributors. But if it's true of Milton's channel, the bar for supplier data keeps rising.

### A morning at Dave's garage

**NORA:** Let's make this concrete. Walk me through a day in the life. Invented, not a real customer.

**JAMES:** Sure. Let's say there's an independent shop called Dave's Garage, in a mid-sized town. Six bays, eight people. Monday morning, a technician called Rosa is doing a brake job. She connects her impact wrench to the drop hose from the ceiling, and the coupler won't hold. It's worn. Air hisses out. She swaps to the next bay's hose, which costs her five minutes and annoys her colleague.

**NORA:** Small problem.

**JAMES:** Small problem, but it happens every week in some form. Rosa tells Dave. Dave looks in the drawer where spare fittings live and finds a mix of styles from three different brands, half of which don't match the shop's system.

**NORA:** I have a drawer like that for phone chargers.

**JAMES:** Everyone does. So Dave has three choices. Wait for the tool van on Thursday. Drive to the parts store, which may or may not have the right style. Or order online, where he searches for something like "industrial coupler quarter inch" and gets four hundred results, many of which look identical.

**NORA:** And how does he pick?

**JAMES:** He picks the one that says the right style clearly, has good reviews, and arrives tomorrow. If the product page is vague about which style it is, he skips it. So the quality of the product information is literally what wins or loses the sale.

**NORA:** And from Milton's side?

**JAMES:** From Milton's side, that purchase is invisible unless it comes through its own channels. If Dave buys from a distributor, Milton sees the distributor's order weeks later, as a restock. If he buys on a marketplace, Milton may see a sale of one unit with no context. What Milton doesn't see is the worn coupler in Rosa's hand, which is the real signal of demand.

**NORA:** So the demand signal is very indirect.

**JAMES:** Very. And that's the setup for the AI opportunity. Milton's job is to have the right part, with the right product data, in the right warehouse, when that distributor reorders. Across nine brands and, our guess, thousands of part numbers, that's a forecasting and data problem.

**NORA:** Let me push on one thing. Rosa doesn't care who owns the brand.

**JAMES:** Correct. She cares that it fits, it holds, and she can get it today. Everything Milton does with technology should be judged by whether it makes that more likely.

**JAMES:** Right, and I want to be fair. A lot of companies like this use AI quietly, inside off-the-shelf software, or are running pilots they haven't announced. Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. But from the outside, there's nothing to report.

**JAMES:** The visible digital piece is e-commerce. Milton's own website has a store-style layout, and its news is published as blog posts on that site. We couldn't verify how many of its brands sell online directly, or what share of sales that is.

**NORA:** Is it unusual for an industrial supplier to have no visible AI story in 2026?

**JAMES:** Not at all. In my view, it's probably the norm for companies of this type. The ones that do have an AI story tend to be software businesses or companies with big direct-to-customer channels. Industrial suppliers that sell through distributors often move later, because the pressure is less visible.

**NORA:** But the pressure is there.

**JAMES:** It's there, and it's coming from two directions. First, distributors themselves are investing in digital catalogues and AI search, and they want suppliers who can feed them rich, accurate data. Second, the way buyers search is changing. More people ask an AI assistant what to buy, or use AI-powered search on a marketplace. If your product data is thin or inconsistent, those systems are less likely to recommend you.

**NORA:** You mentioned that e-commerce platforms are pushing this.

**JAMES:** Yes. Shopify, for example, has published material about AI trends saying that AI-generated product content and AI search are central to e-commerce in 2026. That's a vendor talking about its own market, so it's naturally enthusiastic. But the direction is real.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's talk challenges. What's hard about Milton's position?

**JAMES:** I'd put brand sprawl first. When you buy many companies, you usually inherit many of everything. Many product catalogues, each with its own part numbering. Many price lists. Possibly many order systems, websites and warehouses. Overlapping products, where two brands sell nearly the same coupler under different names. This is our inference, but it's almost universal in buy-and-build.

**NORA:** Why does that matter so much?

**JAMES:** Because it hides costs. Duplicate products mean duplicate inventory. Two brands holding stock of nearly identical items means more cash tied up on shelves. Duplicate systems mean duplicate licences and duplicate staff effort. And messy data makes everything downstream harder, from pricing to forecasting to AI.

**NORA:** Second challenge?

**JAMES:** Visibility, in two senses. Inside, the company may struggle to see its whole business in one place if the brands still run separately. Outside, the lack of public information makes it harder to benchmark. For a future buyer, that means more due diligence work, and less certainty usually means a lower price.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Price pressure. Many air accessories are commodity-like. Imported and private-label alternatives exist. A branded supplier has to justify its price with quality and service, and that argument only works if service is genuinely better.

**NORA:** And the market itself?

**JAMES:** Concentration in automotive aftermarket demand is a challenge, in my view. In general, that market has been fairly resilient, because people repair cars in good times and bad. But it's not immune to shifts. Electric vehicles, for example, have fewer moving parts in some areas, though tyres, brakes and suspension still need air tools. I'd call that a slow-moving question rather than an immediate threat.

**NORA:** Tariffs? Supply chains?

**JAMES:** We didn't find anything about Milton's sourcing, so I won't speculate on its exposure. In general, any company that manufactures with metals and imports some components has to manage trade and supply risks. That's one more reason good forecasting and inventory planning matter.

**NORA:** What about people and culture? You're stitching together a lot of small companies.

**JAMES:** That's a challenge people underestimate. Many of the businesses a buy-and-build acquires were founder-led or family-run. They have their own ways of doing things, their own loyal customers, and often a handful of people who carry the knowledge in their heads. The person who knows which seal goes in which coupler, or which distributor always orders late in the quarter.

**NORA:** And that knowledge doesn't live in a system.

**JAMES:** Usually not. So integration isn't only about software. It's about getting that knowledge written down before it walks out of the door, and persuading people that the new shared way of working is better than the one they built. If you get that wrong, you can lose the very customers you bought.

**NORA:** And the clock.

**JAMES:** The clock is real. Levine Leichtman has been involved since November 2019, so seven years. The continuation fund reset the timeline and added capital, but the next owner will want to see a business that has been integrated, not just accumulated.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Okay, opportunities. You're on the board. Where do you push?

**JAMES:** The first one is demand forecasting and inventory optimisation across the brands. Our view is that this is where the money is. A multi-brand portfolio usually carries duplicated products and safety stock. Better forecasting lets you hold less stock while still filling distributor orders, which releases working capital. That's cash the company can use for more acquisitions or debt reduction.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Product data and catalogue clean-up, with AI doing a lot of the heavy lifting. Take every product across every brand. Standardise the names, the specifications, the coupler styles, the thread sizes, the pressure ratings. Write clear descriptions. Tag them so distributors and marketplaces can list them properly. That's laborious work that AI tools are now genuinely good at drafting, with humans checking.

**NORA:** That sounds unglamorous.

**JAMES:** It's the least glamorous and maybe the most important. Everything else depends on it. Forecasting needs to know which products are really the same. Cross-selling needs to know what goes with what. AI search needs good descriptions.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Consolidating the digital storefronts and back office onto one platform, if they aren't already. Our guess is that several brands may have their own sites. One shared platform with brand-specific front ends would be cheaper to run and much easier to improve.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Pricing and promotion analytics across the portfolio. When you have many brands selling to the same distributors, you need a coherent view of pricing. Are two of your brands undercutting each other? Is a promotion on one brand just stealing sales from another? AI and analytics can help answer that.

**NORA:** And growth?

**JAMES:** Cross-selling into existing distributor accounts. If a distributor buys couplers from one Milton brand but air preparation equipment from a competitor, that's an obvious conversation. A simple model that looks at what each distributor buys and suggests what they're missing can make the sales team's time much more productive.

**NORA:** And more acquisitions?

**JAMES:** Very likely, given the fresh capital. Adjacent niches in air preparation, fittings, hoses and specialist tools follow the pattern of the Arrow Pneumatics and Steck deals. But I'd want each new brand to land on a common data and systems platform from day one. Otherwise you just make the sprawl worse.

**NORA:** Anything customer-facing on the AI side?

**JAMES:** Two ideas, both our own. The first is a part finder. Remember Dave staring at his drawer of mismatched fittings? Imagine he could take a photo of the worn coupler, or describe it, and a tool tells him which style it is, which size, and which current part replaces it, across every Milton brand. That's a well-understood kind of AI problem, and it turns confusion into an order.

**NORA:** And the second?

**JAMES:** A technical support assistant, for distributors' counter staff and for customers. Questions like, what pressure is this regulator rated for, will this hose work with that fitting, what's the replacement for a discontinued item from a brand Milton acquired. Those questions probably land today on a small number of experienced people. An assistant trained on clean product data can answer the routine ones and hand the tricky ones to a human.

**NORA:** Both of those depend on the clean catalogue again.

**JAMES:** Everything does. That's why I keep coming back to it. The part finder and the assistant are only as good as the product data underneath. Get the data right and you get three or four AI products almost for free. Get it wrong and every one of them gives confident wrong answers.

**NORA:** Anything in the back office that's quicker to win?

**JAMES:** Order entry, in my view. In a lot of industrial businesses, distributors still send purchase orders as emails, attachments or scanned documents, and someone retypes them into the order system. Modern AI document tools can read those orders, match the line items to the right part numbers and put a draft order in front of a person to approve. We don't know how Milton takes orders today, so this is a general pattern rather than a finding. But where it applies, it saves hours every week and cuts errors.

**NORA:** And errors in orders are expensive.

**JAMES:** They're expensive and they annoy distributors, which is worse. A wrong item shipped means a return, a credit, a re-ship and a frustrated customer. If the AI flags an order line that doesn't match anything in the catalogue, that's a mistake caught before it costs money. And again, notice that it relies on the same clean product list.

**NORA:** What about the products themselves? Any role for smart tools?

**JAMES:** It's possible in principle. Connected tyre inflation equipment or sensors that detect leaks in an air system are things that exist in the broader market. But we found no public evidence of Milton selling anything like that, so I'd treat it as a later, optional bet, not the core story.

### The best AI bet

**NORA:** If you had to pick one AI bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** Demand forecasting across the brands, built on a cleaned-up, unified product catalogue. I'll call it one bet, because the second half makes the first half work.

**NORA:** Why that over, say, AI product content?

**JAMES:** Because of where the money is. Inventory is usually one of the largest uses of cash in a business like this. If better forecasting lets you carry noticeably less stock without hurting fill rates, that's a one-off release of cash plus an ongoing saving in storage, handling and obsolete stock. That's a number a board and a future buyer both understand.

**NORA:** And product content is the second bet.

**JAMES:** Yes. It saves labour hours and helps sales, but the big financial prize is inventory. Although, as I said, you need the clean catalogue anyway, so you get the content benefits along the way.

**NORA:** How sophisticated does the AI need to be?

**JAMES:** Less than people think. You don't need a cutting-edge model. Modern forecasting tools can take sales history, distributor orders, seasonality, promotions and lead times, and produce a forecast by item and by warehouse. Many are available off the shelf. The hard part is the data, not the algorithm.

**NORA:** So the AI bet is really a data bet.

**JAMES:** In this case, almost entirely. And that's true for a lot of industrial companies. The AI is the easy bit. Knowing that part number A from brand one and part number B from brand four are the same coupler is the hard bit.

### First 100 days

**NORA:** Let's get practical. You're asked to run this. What happens in the first hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: find out what you've got. Map every brand's systems. Where does each one keep its product list, its sales history, its inventory? How many warehouses, how many order systems? Pull a year or two of sales history into one place, even if it's messy. And pick one owner for the whole programme, someone with authority across brands.

**NORA:** Not a committee.

**JAMES:** Never a committee. One person, with a small team and a sponsor on the leadership team.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Build the master product list. Use AI to propose matches across brands: these items look like the same coupler, these are true variants, these are unique. Have product experts confirm the matches. They're the people who know that two items that look identical have different seals or different pressure ratings. At the same time, choose a forecasting tool and run it in shadow mode on the historic data.

**NORA:** Shadow mode meaning?

**JAMES:** The forecast runs alongside the planners' existing process without replacing it. You compare what the tool would have ordered with what actually happened. Where the tool was better, you learn to trust it. Where it was worse, you learn why.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Pick one product family, say couplers and plugs, and one warehouse. Let the forecast drive real reorder suggestions there, with planners approving. Measure three things: stock on hand, fill rate to distributors, and the number of urgent expedited orders. If stock goes down and fill rate stays up, you've got your proof point. At day one hundred, decide the rollout plan for the rest of the catalogue.

**NORA:** What could go wrong?

**JAMES:** The classic failure is trying to boil the ocean. Building a perfect master catalogue of every item before forecasting anything. That can take a year and lose momentum. Better to do one family end to end and show results.

**NORA:** Let's do a second walk-through, then. A planner at an invented version of this company.

**JAMES:** Okay. Imagine a supply planner called Marcus. Today, his week looks like this. On Monday he exports sales reports from two different brand systems into spreadsheets. He has a third brand that's still on its own system, so he emails someone there for their numbers. He notices that a popular coupler is running low in the Midwest warehouse, so he places an order. He doesn't notice that a nearly identical coupler under another brand is overstocked in the same building, because the two items have different part numbers and live in different spreadsheets.

**NORA:** So the company buys more of something it already has.

**JAMES:** In effect, yes. Now replay it with the system we've described. On Monday, Marcus opens one screen. It shows every product family across brands, with a forecast for the next twelve weeks. The coupler that's running low is flagged, but the system also notes that a functionally identical item is overstocked, and suggests rebalancing or substituting before buying. It also flags a hose that's selling faster than usual in the South, perhaps because a distributor there ran a promotion, and asks Marcus whether that spike will last.

**NORA:** It asks him.

**JAMES:** It should. Marcus knows things the data doesn't, like that the promotion ended last week. He answers, the forecast adjusts. The point isn't to replace Marcus. It's to turn his week from data gathering into decision making.

**NORA:** And the result?

**JAMES:** In our hypothetical, fewer emergency orders, less duplicate stock, fewer stockouts. In real life, the size of the gain depends on how messy things are today, which we don't know. That's exactly what the first hundred days should measure.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's widen out. What can other businesses learn from Milton's situation?

**JAMES:** Four lessons. First, in a buy-and-build, the first AI project is usually joining up the data. Before you do anything clever, you need to know what you sell, across all the businesses you've bought. That's true far beyond air tools.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, look for AI where the cash is. Lots of companies start AI with a chatbot because it's visible. For a products company, the biggest wins are often in inventory, pricing and working capital. They're less exciting to announce, but they move the numbers.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, product data is now a sales channel. When buyers search with AI tools and marketplaces, the quality of your product descriptions and specifications decides whether you show up. Treat your catalogue like a product, with an owner and a standard.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, quiet isn't the same as safe. A company with no public AI story might be doing fine today. But its distributors, competitors and customers are changing how they work. The risk is waking up in three years to find that your distributors prefer suppliers who can plug into their systems more easily.

**NORA:** Is there a lesson about continuation funds specifically?

**JAMES:** I think so. A continuation fund buys time, and time is only valuable if you use it. The owners have said they want more time and capital for growth. The companies that make the most of that period are the ones that pick a small number of operating improvements and finish them.

**NORA:** And for a small business listening, who doesn't own nine brands?

**JAMES:** Even a single-brand distributor or manufacturer can do a version of this. Clean up your product list. Use an AI tool to improve your descriptions. Look at your stock and ask which items you're carrying too much of. You don't need a big programme to start.

**NORA:** Is there a lesson in how little Milton says publicly?

**JAMES:** Maybe a gentle one. Being quiet is a perfectly legitimate choice for a business that sells through distributors. But in a world where buyers, partners and even future employees research you through AI assistants and search, having almost nothing public has a cost. A little clear, factual information about who you are, what you make and who leads you goes a long way. It also helps when you're trying to hire the data and digital people this kind of plan needs.

**NORA:** Because nobody wants to join a company they can't find out anything about.

**JAMES:** Exactly. Talent is a channel too.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's do risks.

**JAMES:** One, competition on price, from P and F Industries, private players and private label. If the integration doesn't produce real service advantages, Milton's brands are vulnerable to cheaper alternatives. Two, channel shift. If more professional buyers move to marketplaces and AI-led search, traffic may move away from brand sites and from traditional distributors, and Milton needs to be strong in both places.

**NORA:** Three?

**JAMES:** Integration risk. A long acquisition programme with many brands is hard to integrate. Pushing too hard can disrupt service to distributors, which is the one thing you must not break. Four, concentration in the automotive aftermarket, which we discussed. Five, people. Integration programmes depend on a few key people who know the products and the systems. If they leave, knowledge goes with them.

**NORA:** And the information gap?

**JAMES:** I'd call that a risk for us as observers more than for the company. But it does matter for the exit. When the time comes to sell, a business that can show clean, consistent numbers across brands will be easier to value.

**NORA:** Are there risks in the AI itself?

**JAMES:** Yes, and in this category they're specific. If you use AI to write product descriptions, it can invent details. In most products that's embarrassing. With pressurised air equipment, a wrong pressure rating or a wrong compatibility claim could actually be a safety issue. So any AI-written content has to be checked against real specifications by someone who knows the product, and the specifications should come from a controlled source, not from the model.

**NORA:** So a human in the loop isn't optional.

**JAMES:** Not for anything that describes what a product can safely do. The same goes for the support assistant. It should quote from approved data, say when it doesn't know, and hand off to a person. That's good practice anywhere, but it matters more when the product holds air at pressure.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Three things. First, new acquisitions funded by the continuation fund capital, and whether they're described as joining a common platform. Second, any sign of a unified catalogue or a single digital storefront across brands. Third, leadership news, including clarity on the chief executive and whether the company hires anyone in a data, digital or supply chain leadership role. Any of those would tell you the operating story is moving.

**NORA:** And something that would worry you?

**JAMES:** More brands added with no sign of integration. Or a sale process that starts before the operating improvements are visible.
