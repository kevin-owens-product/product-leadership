### BOARD PACK: SIX NUMBERS, THREE VERSIONS OF REALITY

**ASHA:** Here are the six cards in the investment-committee pack. Card one: seller ARR, one hundred million; verified ARR, ninety-six million. Card two: seller organic ARR growth, eleven percent; verified, eight percent. Card three: seller NRR, one hundred and six percent; verified, one hundred and one. Card four: seller adjusted EBITDA, twenty-eight million; verified, twenty-six. Card five: net debt, one hundred and fifty-six million, equal to six times verified EBITDA. Card six: AI readiness, sixty-three percent of relevant data usable and forty-six percent of ARR covered by clear improvement rights.

**COLE:** The apparently positive number is one hundred million of ARR. Round numbers are persuasive. They look audited even when they are assembled. But two and a half million is signed and not live, and another one and a half million is recurring services presented as software ARR. Those may be commercially useful. They are not the same thing as live recurring software revenue. The seller did not invent customers; it promoted categories.

**ASHA:** That distinction changes price because price is quoted as a multiple. At six times ARR, every million dollars that does not qualify removes six million dollars of enterprise value. Four million of promoted ARR is twenty-four million of price. This is why definition work is not bookkeeping after the deal. Definition work is part of the bid.

**COLE:** The seller's NRR has the same problem. Several downgrades are excluded. If a customer renews from one hundred thousand dollars to eighty thousand, twenty thousand of contraction happened whether the account executive calls it a packaging migration, a right-sizing event, or a customer-success save. The system experienced a loss of recurring value. Removing that loss from NRR does not make the customer healthier.

**ASHA:** And the EBITDA adjustment schedule includes recurring cloud expense. A cost does not become nonrecurring because management wishes the migration had ended. If the platform needs the cloud service to deliver next year's revenue, the expense belongs in the economic baseline. The verified EBITDA is twenty-six million, not twenty-eight. That difference makes entry leverage six times rather than five point six times. Small definition choices have just moved both the numerator of the valuation and the denominator of the leverage ratio against you.

**COLE:** There is a product clue hiding in the finance pack. Median onboarding is one hundred and twelve days, not the eighty-eight days in the seller presentation. Availability is ninety-nine point seven percent, not ninety-nine point nine. Fifty-eight percent of engineering capacity is spent maintaining or reconciling existing systems. Those facts explain why booked demand is not becoming live ARR quickly and why customers may contract even in a sticky market.

**ASHA:** The job is to connect those facts, not admire them separately. Slow onboarding delays activation. Delayed activation delays billing or creates shelfware, depending on the contract. Shelfware weakens renewal. Weak renewal compresses NRR. Lower NRR reduces ARR growth. Lower ARR growth weakens the exit multiple. Meanwhile reliability work and onboarding work consume the cash that debt service has already claimed. That is the operating chain inside the underwriting model.

### CROSS-EXAMINATION: WHAT ARE YOU ACTUALLY BUYING?

**COLE:** Let me challenge the finance framing. Suppose the verified ARR is ninety-six million but the four million difference is recoverable. The signed contracts will activate. The recurring services can be productized. If we reduce price now for conditions we can fix, aren't we asking the seller to fund our upside?

**ASHA:** We are asking the seller to be paid for what exists. Upside belongs to the buyer when the buyer must supply the capital, execution, and risk required to create it. A signed-not-live contract can belong in backlog, bookings, or a commercial pipeline. It enters ARR when the recurring service has begun under the agreed definition. Recurring services may be valuable revenue. They enter software ARR only when the customer is paying for a recurring software right, not for people repeatedly doing work.

**COLE:** So your objection is not conservatism for its own sake. It is category integrity.

**ASHA:** Exactly. Conservative underwriting does not mean pretending every good thing will fail. It means separating operating facts from future choices. If a contract activates next quarter, the return model improves next quarter. If services become software, prove the delivery model and margin first. You can always recognize upside when evidence arrives. You cannot recover the price you paid for evidence that never did.

**COLE:** Then let's define the asset beyond the numbers. Harborline has twelve hundred customers at an average ACV of eighty thousand dollars. The top twenty customers are twenty-three percent of ARR. The market is regulated and operationally sticky. Equipment histories become more useful over time. Field technicians repeat jobs on the same assets. There is a plausible data flywheel.

**ASHA:** Plausible is the correct word. A data asset becomes an advantage only when five things are true. You have the rights to use it. You can resolve it to the correct customer, site, asset, and work order. It is sufficiently complete and fresh. It improves a workflow customers value. And competitors cannot reproduce the outcome cheaply. Harborline currently clears pieces of that test, not the whole test.

**COLE:** The workflow candidate is still attractive. A technician arrives at an unfamiliar rooftop unit. Harborline can retrieve service history and manuals, surface prior faults, draft a diagnosis, and help produce a quote before the technician leaves. Better first-time fix rates and faster quoting are measurable customer outcomes. Harborline's baseline first-time fix rate is seventy-one percent. That is not a chatbot looking for a problem.

**ASHA:** Agreed. But the underwriting question is not whether the workflow could work. It is whether we need it to work in order to earn an acceptable return. If the answer is yes, we are not buying vertical software with AI upside. We are financing a venture bet with buyout debt.

**COLE:** That line matters. A credible opportunity can support the investment thesis without entering the base-case revenue model. We can reserve capital for the experiment, require stage-gated evidence, and let successful AI revenue become upside. We should not type ten million of future AI ARR into year five because the demo looked good.

**ASHA:** Nor should we type speculative headcount savings into EBITDA. The company has three codebases and seventeen schemas. AI may reduce manual work eventually. In the first two years it will also require data engineering, product work, evaluation, security, customer enablement, and human review. The first cash effect may be negative. If your model assumes the transformation funds itself immediately, the model is concealing the transformation.

[PAUSE]

**COLE:** Kevin, here is the first operator habit of the season: every exciting product claim must enter the value bridge through a named mechanism. Retained ARR. New ARR. Price. Gross margin. Operating expense. Cash conversion. Or lower risk that a buyer can verify. If you cannot name the mechanism, timing, owner, evidence, and cost, it is not yet in the return case.

### THE DEAL MATH: ENTERPRISE VALUE IS NOT EQUITY VALUE

**ASHA:** Now we build the transaction from first principles. The proposed enterprise value is five hundred and seventy-six million dollars. Enterprise value is the value of the operating business available to all capital providers, debt and equity. It is not the cheque the seller's shareholders receive.

**COLE:** Harborline has one hundred and fifty-six million of net debt. Net debt means interest-bearing debt minus cash available to offset it. To move from enterprise value to equity value, subtract net debt. Five hundred and seventy-six minus one hundred and fifty-six equals four hundred and twenty million dollars of equity value.

**ASHA:** In the simplified sources-and-uses table, uses are what the transaction must fund. Four hundred and twenty million goes to acquire the seller's equity, and one hundred and fifty-six million accounts for the debt that remains in or is refinanced with the capital structure. Total uses: five hundred and seventy-six million. Sources are one hundred and fifty-six million of debt and four hundred and twenty million of sponsor equity. Sources equal uses. If they do not, the spreadsheet is not unfinished; it is wrong.

**COLE:** Why quote the deal at six times verified ARR rather than roughly twenty-two times EBITDA? Because vertical SaaS assets are often discussed on recurring revenue quality and growth, while debt capacity is assessed against EBITDA and cash flow. The two lenses coexist. The ARR multiple prices the asset. The EBITDA multiple tells you how much operating earnings support the capital structure. Confusing them can make a moderately priced revenue asset look safely levered when it is not.

**ASHA:** At entry, net debt divided by verified EBITDA is one hundred and fifty-six divided by twenty-six: six times. Cash interest at ten percent is fifteen point six million per year. EBITDA divided by cash interest is about one point seven times. That is not a full cash-flow coverage ratio because EBITDA is before taxes, capital expenditures, capitalized development, working-capital movements, and mandatory amortization. It is simply an early warning. The cushion is thinner than the EBITDA headline suggests.

**COLE:** And software capital intensity can hide in accounting choices. If development spending is capitalized, cash leaves today while the expense appears later through amortization. An operator cannot service debt with adjusted EBITDA. Debt is serviced with cash. So the underwriting needs an EBITDA-to-cash bridge, even if our season ledger starts with EBITDA and net debt.

**ASHA:** The base case starts at ninety-six million of ARR. Grow that at approximately twelve percent for five years and you reach about one hundred and sixty-nine million. The target EBITDA margin is thirty-two percent. Target net debt is ninety million. At an exit multiple of seven times ARR, enterprise value is one billion one hundred and eighty-three million. Subtract ninety million of net debt and sponsor equity value is approximately one billion ninety-three million dollars.

**COLE:** The sponsor invested four hundred and twenty million and receives one billion ninety-three million. Divide ending equity value by beginning equity: two point six times money on invested capital, or MOIC. MOIC tells you how many dollars came back for each dollar invested. It does not care whether that took two years or ten.

**ASHA:** IRR adds time. For one initial investment and one exit five years later, solve for the annual compound rate that turns four hundred and twenty million into one billion ninety-three million. That is roughly twenty-one point one percent. A two point six times return in five years is twenty-one percent IRR. The same two point six times in three years would be far better; in eight years, materially worse. MOIC measures magnitude. IRR measures magnitude and speed.

**COLE:** Neither tells you everything. IRR can reward quick distributions even when total value is modest. MOIC can flatter a slow investment. An operator should watch both, plus the actual cash demands and the assumptions underneath them. You are not managing a mathematical output. You are managing the customer and operating system that produce it.

### THE RETURN BRIDGE: WHERE THE BILLION COMES FROM

**ASHA:** The underwrite becomes useful when we decompose the increase in equity value. Starting sponsor equity is four hundred and twenty million. Target exit equity is one billion ninety-three million. The increase is six hundred and seventy-three million. Where does it come from?

**COLE:** First, ARR growth. Hold the entry multiple constant at six times. Growing ARR from ninety-six million to one hundred and sixty-nine million adds seventy-three million of ARR. At six times, that adds four hundred and thirty-eight million of enterprise value.

**ASHA:** Second, multiple expansion. Applying seven times rather than six times to one hundred and sixty-nine million adds another one hundred and sixty-nine million of enterprise value. Third, debt paydown. Reducing net debt from one hundred and fifty-six million to ninety million adds sixty-six million to equity value, all else equal.

**COLE:** Four hundred and thirty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-nine plus sixty-six equals six hundred and seventy-three million. Approximately sixty-five percent of the equity-value increase comes from ARR growth, twenty-five percent from multiple improvement, and ten percent from debt paydown. That is the season's economic map.

**ASHA:** Notice what is not a separate bar: AI. AI is not a fifth source of value floating above the business. If Harborline Assist improves retention, creates paid workflow volume, raises contribution margin, or makes revenue more durable, it moves one of those bars. If it merely earns a press release, it moves none.

**COLE:** The same is true of product quality and technical debt. Reliability work may look like cost. If it reduces churn and makes implementation faster, it enters ARR growth and possibly the multiple. A platform consolidation may reduce cloud and maintenance cost, allowing debt paydown. But if it becomes a four-year rewrite that delays customer outcomes, it can destroy all three bars.

**ASHA:** Multiple expansion deserves special suspicion. The model assumes an exit at seven times ARR after entering at six. That can be defensible if Harborline becomes a better business: faster organic growth, higher retention, stronger margins, cleaner product architecture, proven AI revenue, and lower execution risk. It is not defensible because the market might be generous in five years.

**COLE:** So the one-turn multiple uplift is a target condition, not a gift. Every quarter we should ask what evidence makes a buyer pay it. Contract-level recurring revenue. Cohort retention. Implementation speed. Product telemetry. Reliable workload economics. Security controls. Data rights. A buyer pays for durable cash-flow potential and lower uncertainty, not our enthusiasm.

### DOWNSIDE: WHICH DIRECTION OF WRONG HURTS?

**COLE:** Let's build a downside without making it theatrical. Assume Harborline grows ARR at six percent rather than twelve because NRR slips and onboarding remains slow. After five years, ARR is about one hundred and twenty-eight and a half million. Assume the business earns only a six-times exit multiple, the same as entry, because quality did not improve. Assume net debt falls only to one hundred and thirty million because cash was absorbed by remediation.

**ASHA:** Six times one hundred and twenty-eight and a half is about seven hundred and seventy-one million of enterprise value. Subtract one hundred and thirty million of net debt and equity value is roughly six hundred and forty-one million. Divide by the four hundred and twenty million invested: about one point five-three times MOIC. Over five years, that is an IRR of roughly eight point eight percent.

**COLE:** The company survives in that scenario. Customers still use the product. Revenue still grows. The sponsor outcome is weak because three modest misses compound: slower growth, no multiple expansion, and less debt paydown. This is why a downside case should not be a meteor strike. It should show what ordinary execution disappointment does to equity.

**ASHA:** Now consider the operating pinch inside it. Fifteen point six million of annual cash interest does not fall when NRR falls. If EBITDA declines by three million and remediation requires another three million of cash, the six-million gap must come from liquidity, delayed investment, or more debt. Delaying reliability investment can worsen retention, creating the loop we opened with. The first thing that breaks may be strategic freedom.

**COLE:** That answer is better than saying the covenant breaks on Tuesday. We do not yet have the full covenant package or cash-flow schedule. We do know the direction: high fixed cash interest makes the product plan sensitive to retention and conversion of EBITDA to cash. The right diligence response is to demand the covenant definitions, amortization schedule, minimum liquidity, capex history, capitalized-development policy, and monthly cash model before signing.

**ASHA:** Good underwriting distinguishes a known risk from a fabricated precision. We know the lender case barely tolerates a retention miss. We do not invent an exact breach date. We define the information required, run sensitivities, and place a condition on the bid.

**COLE:** Product and AI findings can alter price in three ways. They can reveal hidden required investment, such as identity resolution or contract remediation. They can change the probability of the growth case, such as strong evidence that a frequent workflow improves first-time fix rates. Or they can create a liability, such as data rights that prevent the intended use. They do not alter price merely because the technology is fashionable.

### DECISION GATE: THREE DEFENSIBLE CALLS

**ASHA:** You have three choices. Choice one: bid five hundred and seventy-six million dollars based on verified ARR and EBITDA. Fund it with one hundred and fifty-six million of debt and four hundred and twenty million of sponsor equity. Treat AI as unpriced upside and reserve the right to return to committee if confirmatory diligence weakens the baseline.

**COLE:** Choice two: bid from the seller's promoted numbers. At the same six-times multiple on one hundred million of ARR, enterprise value is six hundred million dollars. The argument is that the market is competitive, the four-million gap is recoverable, and losing a scarce vertical-software asset over four percent of claimed ARR could be penny-wise. You preserve seller goodwill and win certainty.

**ASHA:** Choice three: walk. Six-times leverage, one-point-seven-times EBITDA-to-interest coverage, declining verified retention, slow onboarding, weak availability, fragmented systems, incomplete data, and unclear rights may be too many coupled risks. Walking protects all four hundred and twenty million of equity for a cleaner asset.

**COLE:** None is foolish. Choice two may win an auction and the operational upside may repay the premium. Choice three may be correct if the lender terms or data-rights exposure deteriorate. Choice one accepts real risk but refuses to pay for unverified value.

**ASHA:** Kevin, commit now. Which choice? Then state the assumption that would make your choice wrong. Do not say, "It depends," without naming what it depends on.

### CANONICAL CALL: BUY THE VERIFIED DOWNSIDE

**ASHA:** Our call is choice one: bid five hundred and seventy-six million from the verified downside. We are buying Harborline at six times ninety-six million of live recurring software ARR, with twenty-six million of adjusted EBITDA after restoring recurring costs. We are not paying six hundred million for categories we hope to convert.

**COLE:** This is not a declaration that the seller is dishonest or that the AI opportunity is worthless. It is a claim about burden of proof. Signed-not-live contracts become upside when activated. Recurring services become a software asset when productization changes delivery and margin. AI revenue enters the model when customers use a defined workflow, pay for it, renew it, and the contribution economics are visible.

**ASHA:** The decision memo has six fields. Choice: acquire at five hundred and seventy-six million enterprise value. Capital: four hundred and twenty million sponsor equity and one hundred and fifty-six million net debt, subject to the final lender case. Owner: the deal partner owns price and financing; Kevin, in the operator seat, owns validation of the value-creation thesis with the portfolio CEO and CFO.

**COLE:** Target: reach one hundred and sixty-nine million of ARR, thirty-two percent EBITDA margin, and ninety million of net debt in year five, creating approximately one billion ninety-three million of sponsor equity value. **ASHA:** Guardrail: no unproven AI revenue, speculative headcount savings, or assumed market multiple expansion is counted as operating fact. Every initiative must have a customer or financial mechanism, a cost, an owner, timing, confidence, and an evidence source.

### OPERATOR DRILL: THE ONE-PAGE IC MEMO

**ASHA:** Your drill is to build a one-page investment memo and return bridge for a real business. One page forces selection. Start with the decision in one sentence: buy, do not buy, or buy only below a named price and subject to named conditions.

**COLE:** Then write the verified baseline. Keep it to six metrics: recurring revenue, organic growth, gross retention, net retention, adjusted EBITDA, and net debt or cash. If the business is not subscription software, choose the equivalent operating quantities. Define each one. A number without a definition is not compact; it is ambiguous.

**ASHA:** Next, sources and uses. State enterprise value, net debt, equity purchase price, new or continuing debt, sponsor equity, and transaction costs if you have them. Make sources equal uses. Then show entry leverage and annual cash interest. Add the two or three cash items EBITDA excludes that matter most.

**COLE:** Build the five-year bridge with only controllable mechanisms. Opening ARR. New logos. Expansion. Churn and contraction. Acquired ARR if it is truly part of the plan. Gross margin. Operating expense. Cash conversion. Net debt. Exit multiple. For every improvement, name the operating action that causes it.

**ASHA:** Show MOIC and IRR together. Then create a downside made of ordinary misses: lower retention, slower implementation, one delayed product, less margin expansion, a flat exit multiple. Do not make the downside so apocalyptic that nobody learns from it.

**COLE:** Finish with three thesis-breakers and three evidence requests. A thesis-breaker is not "AI adoption is slower." It is "fewer than twenty percent of target customers have both usable equipment histories and rights-clear data, making the chosen workflow commercially too narrow." The evidence request is the contract sample, data audit, and customer test that answers it.

**ASHA:** And ask the governing question: what moves, by how much, by when, with what confidence, and how will you prove it? If your memo cannot answer that, your deck has more pages than your thesis has evidence.
