### Donuts, kolaches and the morning

**NORA:** Okay. Kolaches.

**JAMES:** A kolache, broadly, is a pastry with Czech roots. Czech immigrants brought it to Texas in the nineteenth century, and it became a Texas staple. The traditional version is a soft, slightly sweet yeast dough with a fruit or cheese filling in the middle. But in a lot of Texas, when people say kolache, they often mean a savoury version, sausage wrapped in dough, which purists would call by a different name. Either way, it's a hand-held breakfast item.

**NORA:** And Shipley sells both donuts and kolaches.

**JAMES:** Right. And that's strategically interesting, in our view. Donuts are a treat. Kolaches, especially savoury ones, are a breakfast. Having both means Shipley can be an indulgence and a meal at the same time. For a group order, like our office breakfast, that's a real advantage. You can feed people who want something sweet and people who want something filling in one stop.

**NORA:** What's the business rhythm like?

**JAMES:** It's a morning business, overwhelmingly. This is general industry knowledge, not Shipley data. Donut shops do much of their trade in the early hours, with a rush before work and school. Products are made fresh early in the morning, and much of what isn't sold the same day can't be sold the next. That creates two classic operational problems.

**NORA:** Which are?

**JAMES:** Production and labour. Production, because every morning a shop has to decide how many of each item to make before it knows how many customers will come. Make too many and you throw food away. Make too few and you disappoint customers and lose sales at the busiest time. Labour, because you need people in very early to bake and then enough on the counter for the rush, and those hours are hard to staff.

**NORA:** And groups.

**JAMES:** Groups are a big part of the picture. Party packs, office breakfasts, school events, church gatherings, sports teams. The AI article specifically mentions party packs and a fifty-person catering order. Group orders are high value, but they're also awkward. The customer has to work out quantities, the shop has to plan production, and the timing is often tight.

**NORA:** Tell me about the people in the shop. Who's actually working there at four in the morning?

**JAMES:** In general, a donut shop crew splits into two jobs. The bakers or finishers, who come in very early to make, fry, glaze and fill. And the counter and drive-thru team, who serve the rush. This is general industry knowledge. Those early hours are hard to staff, and restaurant work in general has high staff turnover, so franchisees are constantly hiring and training.

**NORA:** Which is a cost and a quality problem.

**JAMES:** Both. Every new hire has to learn the recipes, the timing and the standards. If the shop is short-staffed, the morning rush gets slower, and customers notice. So anything that makes a shift more predictable, like knowing in advance that a big group order is coming, or having a reliable forecast of how busy Tuesday will be, helps a franchisee schedule the right number of people and keep them.

**NORA:** Is there a risk that AI ordering makes the crew's life harder?

**JAMES:** It could, if it's done badly. If the website happily accepts a fifty-person order for eight in the morning when the shop is already at full stretch, the crew bears the pain. That's why connecting the assistant to shop capacity, and giving shops enough notice, matters. A good system should know when to say "we can do that at nine" rather than just saying yes. We don't know how Shipley handles this today.

**NORA:** And the growth levers?

**JAMES:** Shipley's own releases point to limited-time offers as a key driver. There's trade coverage of a strong quarter driven by LTOs, limited-time offers, and the first-half 2026 release mentioned menu innovation alongside new markets and franchise growth. Limited-time offers are a classic restaurant tool. They give regulars a reason to come back and give marketing something to talk about.

### The franchise model

**NORA:** Let's talk about the business model, because a franchisor is not the same as a donut shop.

**JAMES:** Very much not. This is general knowledge about franchising. Shipley, the franchisor, owns the brand, the recipes, the operating system and the support structure. Franchisees pay to use all of that, usually with an upfront fee and then ongoing royalties based on their sales, plus contributions to a marketing fund. The franchisees invest the capital to build and run the shops and employ the staff.

**NORA:** So Shipley makes money when franchisees sell more.

**JAMES:** Right. Royalties scale with system sales, so anything that increases sales across the network helps. And Shipley makes development money when it sells new territories. That's why the franchise deals in Michigan, Maryland and Raleigh matter. A fifteen-unit agreement is a franchisee committing to build fifteen shops over time.

**NORA:** What's the catch?

**JAMES:** The catch is control. Shipley can't simply order its franchisees to adopt new technology the way a company with its own stores could. Franchise agreements set out what the franchisor can require, and anything beyond that needs persuasion. Franchisees are business owners. They'll ask: what does it cost me, what does it do for my sales, and how much hassle is it?

**NORA:** So for AI, the franchisees are the real customers.

**JAMES:** In a sense, yes. And that's why the AI ordering assistant is interesting. It sits on the central website, which the franchisor controls, so it could be rolled out across the system without every franchisee buying their own technology. And the benefit, faster orders and more group sales, lands in franchisees' shops. That's a very franchise-friendly way to introduce AI.

**NORA:** What about the risk side for franchisees?

**JAMES:** The thing franchisees worry about with digital ordering is fees and control. If ordering moves to third-party delivery apps, franchisees often pay significant commissions, and the brand loses the direct relationship with the customer. A brand's own digital channel, done well, keeps more of the margin and the data in the system. We don't have Shipley's channel mix, but that's the general dynamic.

**NORA:** And the catering side has another tool, you mentioned.

**JAMES:** Yes. Shipley launched an online catering program powered by Olo, which is a well-known restaurant ordering platform. We didn't see a date for that. So Shipley's digital stack seems to include at least Olo for catering ordering and an AI layer for the ordering conversation. How those fit together, we don't know in detail.

### The AI ordering assistant

**NORA:** Let's get to the main event. What did Shipley launch?

**JAMES:** On the twelfth of November 2025, Shipley relaunched its website with what it called industry-leading ordering technology, including an AI-powered online ordering assistant. Instead of browsing a menu and adding items one by one, a guest can tell the assistant what they need, in plain language, and the assistant builds the order.

**NORA:** Like our fifty-person breakfast.

**JAMES:** Exactly that. The launch article specifically talks about party packs and catering. And it claims that a fifty-person catering order can be built in about ten seconds rather than ten minutes. That's a company claim. But it's a plausible one, because the hard part of a group order isn't clicking buttons. It's working out the quantities and the mix, and that's exactly what a conversational assistant is good at.

**NORA:** And the adoption number.

**JAMES:** The article, which we did read, said nearly one in four guests had completed orders using the assistant. That's the key number of this episode. I want to be precise about what it says and doesn't say. It's a company figure. It refers to guests ordering online, not all customers including walk-ins. And we don't know the exact time period or the denominator. But even with those caveats, it's a meaningful sign that customers are choosing to use it.

**NORA:** Why is that so unusual?

**JAMES:** Because most consumer companies that launch AI features tell you about the launch, not the usage. Launch is easy. Adoption is the hard part. Lots of chatbots on lots of websites are ignored by almost everyone. Having roughly a quarter of online orders go through the AI within months suggests the assistant is actually useful, and that it's positioned at a point where customers have a real problem to solve.

**NORA:** Who built it?

**JAMES:** There's a small conflict in the sources. The franchise trade article presented it as Shipley's own technology and didn't name a vendor. Separate press coverage, from a food ordering trade site in November 2025, named a company called Saivory as the provider of the AI ordering experience. Our reading is that Shipley worked with Saivory and branded the experience as its own, which is completely normal. But it does mean that the underlying technology likely depends on a vendor.

**NORA:** Does that matter?

**JAMES:** It's a trade-off, not a problem. Buying from a specialist vendor got Shipley to market quickly and probably cheaply. The risks are that competitors can buy similar technology, and that Shipley depends on the vendor's roadmap. We'll come back to that in the challenges. The key thing is that Shipley owns the customer relationship and the order data, as far as we can tell, because the assistant runs on Shipley's own site.

**NORA:** And the assistant learns?

**JAMES:** The launch materials say it learns from guest orders. What that means technically, we don't know. It could mean remembering returning customers' preferences, or improving its suggestions over time from aggregate patterns. Either way, the more orders flow through it, the more data Shipley has about how groups order.

**NORA:** How does something like this work under the hood, in general terms?

**JAMES:** In general terms, and not specific to Shipley's vendor, a conversational ordering assistant has a few layers. A language model understands what the guest typed, including vague phrases like "a mix" or "for fifty people". Then there's a layer that knows the real menu, prices, what's available at that shop and at that time. And a set of rules that decides quantities, for example how many items per person for a breakfast, and checks the order makes sense. The output isn't free text. It's a real basket the guest can check and pay for.

**NORA:** So the AI isn't making things up, it's choosing from the menu.

**JAMES:** That's the design you want. The model handles the conversation, but the order is built from actual menu items with actual prices. That reduces the risk of the assistant promising something the shop can't make. It's also why a restaurant assistant is a much easier AI problem than, say, an open-ended advice chatbot. The universe of possible answers is small and well defined.

**NORA:** What would you measure, beyond the one in four?

**JAMES:** I'd want average order value through the assistant compared with the normal menu, because if the AI suggests sensible add-ons, baskets should be bigger. Completion rate, meaning how many people who start talking to it actually check out. Repeat use. And error rate, meaning orders that had to be corrected at the shop. Those four would tell you whether the assistant is making money or just moving orders from one screen to another.

**NORA:** Anything else on AI?

**JAMES:** Not publicly. We found no evidence of AI in store production, scheduling or forecasting. No dedicated AI roles. No public AI policy or security statement. So Shipley's AI story today is essentially one product, in one channel, with good adoption. That's a strong start, and it's narrower than it might first appear.

### Taking a Houston brand north

**NORA:** I want to dig into the expansion, because it seems to be where most of the energy is. What does it actually take to open a Shipley in Michigan?

**JAMES:** Let me describe the general shape of franchise development, because we don't have Shipley's internal process. First, the franchisor sells a territory to an operator, often someone who already runs other restaurant franchises. That's the Auburn Hills franchisee with the fifteen-unit agreement. Then the operator finds sites, gets permits, builds out the shops, hires and trains staff, and opens, usually over several years. The franchisor provides the design, the training, the supply chain and the marketing playbook.

**NORA:** Why would an experienced operator in Michigan pick Shipley over a bigger name?

**JAMES:** A few plausible reasons, in our view. The bigger names may already have their territories sold, so there's no room to grow with them. A challenger brand often offers better terms or more attention from head office. And the Franchise 500 ranking and the run of positive sales quarters are exactly the kind of signals operators look at. Plus the dual menu is a genuine point of difference. Lots of places sell donuts. Fewer sell kolaches.

**NORA:** But nobody in Michigan knows what a kolache is.

**JAMES:** That's the marketing challenge, and it's probably part of why the new chief marketing officer matters. You have to introduce a product as well as a brand. That's harder than it sounds. In Texas, people walk in already knowing what they want. In a new market, a customer looks at the board and doesn't know what a kolache is, so they order a glazed donut, and you've lost the most distinctive part of your menu.

**NORA:** Could AI help with that?

**JAMES:** Quite naturally, in our view. A conversational ordering assistant is a great place to explain a product. A customer in a new market who types "breakfast for my team" can be gently introduced to kolaches as the savoury option. That's a form of product education that a static menu can't do. It's speculative, but it's one reason I think the assistant is more strategic than it first looks.

**NORA:** What about the supply side? Do they ship donuts from Houston?

**JAMES:** No, donuts are made fresh in or near the shops. But the franchisor typically controls the recipes, the mixes and approved suppliers, so that a glazed donut in Michigan tastes like one in Houston. Keeping that consistency across thirteen or more states is a real operational task, and it gets harder with every new market. We don't have details on Shipley's supply chain, so I'll leave it there.

**NORA:** And how do you know if a new market is working?

**JAMES:** The classic signals are the sales of the first few shops compared with expectations, how quickly they reach a steady level, and whether the franchisee signs for more. From outside, the best signal we get is the pace of further announcements. The October 2026 release about deepening the push into the Midwest suggests the company, at least, is pleased with what it's seeing. But that's a company release, so we'd want to see more shops actually open before drawing conclusions.

**NORA:** Is there a risk of growing too fast?

**JAMES:** There always is in franchising. If you sign lots of territories, you need the support capacity to help all those new operators succeed. Training, site selection, opening support, field visits. If head office is stretched thin, quality slips, early shops disappoint, and the brand gets a bad name in a new market before it's had a chance. That's one reason the March 2026 senior appointments, especially in operations and development, look like the right move, in our view.

### An office breakfast, step by step

**NORA:** Let's make it concrete. Walk me through the office breakfast, before and after. Invented customer.

**JAMES:** Let's call her Denise. She's an office manager in Houston, and she's ordering breakfast for a fifty-person team meeting next Tuesday. Her boss said "get Shipley".

**NORA:** Old way.

**JAMES:** Denise opens the website. She sees a menu: dozens of glazed, assorted dozens, kolaches of various kinds, drinks. She has to work out how many items fifty people will eat. Is it one donut each? Two? How many kolaches for the people who want something savoury? She guesses, adds items, second-guesses, maybe calls the shop to ask what they'd recommend. Then she picks a pickup time, worries whether the shop can handle it, and checks out. Ten minutes, maybe more, and she's not confident she got it right.

**NORA:** And from the shop's side?

**JAMES:** The order arrives. If it's a big one, the shop has to make sure it's included in the morning's production plan. If Denise ordered a strange mix, the shop just makes it. Nobody suggested a better balance.

**NORA:** New way.

**JAMES:** Denise opens the website and types something like "breakfast for fifty people, Tuesday at eight thirty, mix of sweet and savoury, a few vegetarians." The assistant proposes an order: a certain number of dozens of donuts in an assortment, a quantity of sausage kolaches, some fruit or cheese kolaches for the vegetarians, and maybe drinks. It shows her the total and the pickup time. She tweaks one thing, "more glazed", and checks out. The company says this kind of order takes about ten seconds rather than ten minutes.

**NORA:** And she's more confident.

**JAMES:** In our view, that's the hidden benefit. The assistant isn't just faster. It's taking on the anxiety of the decision. "How much do fifty people eat?" is a question the brand can answer better than Denise can, because the brand has seen thousands of group orders.

**NORA:** What happens next time?

**JAMES:** If the assistant remembers Denise, next month she could just say "same as last time, but for sixty". And if Shipley connected it to production, the shop would know about her order with a few days' notice and plan accordingly. That last part, as far as we know, isn't something Shipley has announced. But it's where this naturally goes.

**NORA:** Let's do a second one. A shop owner.

**JAMES:** Okay. Let's call him Raj. He's a franchisee who has just opened two Shipley shops in a new Midwest market, one of the new territories. He's under pressure. Nobody in his town knows the brand yet. He's working very early mornings, he's still learning what sells, and he's throwing away more product than he'd like because he doesn't know how many customers to expect.

**NORA:** What does Raj need?

**JAMES:** Three things, in our view. Customers, which means marketing and visibility. A production plan he can trust, so he isn't over-baking or running out. And staff scheduling that matches the real pattern of the morning rush. Today, as far as we know, Shipley's AI helps him with only part of the first, through online and group ordering.

**NORA:** And with a production forecast?

**JAMES:** Imagine Raj gets a daily suggested production plan. It's based on his recent sales, the day of the week, the weather, local events, the current LTO, and how similar shops in other new markets ramped up in their first months. Plus any group orders already booked through the assistant. It says: tomorrow, make roughly this many of each item, with this much buffer. He can adjust. And at the end of the day he records what was left over, which improves the next day's plan.

**NORA:** That's where the network effect comes in.

**JAMES:** Exactly. A single independent shop only learns from itself. A franchise system with hundreds of shops can learn from all of them, especially from how new shops in new markets behave. That's one of the strongest arguments for being part of a franchise system in the AI era.

### The challenges

**NORA:** Let's be specific about the challenges.

**JAMES:** I'd name five. First, expansion risk. Moving into Michigan, Ohio, Maryland and North Carolina means entering markets where the brand has no heritage. Customers there don't have childhood memories of Shipley. Franchisees there are taking a bet on a brand that's unknown locally. If the first shops in a new market struggle, it can slow the whole expansion.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, franchisee economics. Every new technology, every LTO and every remodel has to make sense for the franchisee's profit and loss. We don't have Shipley's unit economics, but in general, if store margins are squeezed by food and labour costs, franchisees get cautious about new spending. That could slow AI rollouts beyond the central website.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, vendor dependence. If the AI assistant is largely Saivory's technology, then competitors can buy something similar, and Shipley's differentiation comes from how well it uses it, not from owning it. That's not unusual, but it means the advantage has to come from data, integration and execution.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, competition with deep pockets. Dunkin' under Inspire has vast resources and is expanding in Houston itself. Krispy Kreme is a public company with national distribution. Duck Donuts has new private equity backing. They can all invest in digital, and some are already working with AI ordering vendors.

**NORA:** And fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, a subtle one about channels. If AI ordering moves to platforms Shipley doesn't control, say, a customer asking a general-purpose AI assistant on their phone to order breakfast, or a delivery app's own AI, then guests and their data might drift away from Shipley's own channel. That's speculative, but it's a real strategic question for every restaurant brand right now. Owning the first conversation with the customer is valuable, and it's not guaranteed.

**NORA:** Let me push back on the one in four number, then. Could it be less impressive than it sounds?

**JAMES:** It could, and it's worth saying how. If the assistant is the default, or most prominent, way to order on the new website, then a quarter of online orders going through it partly reflects design rather than preference. And if online is a small share of total sales, the effect on the whole business is modest. We don't know either of those things. My view is that it's still a strong signal, but the real test is whether those orders are bigger, more frequent or more profitable than the ones they replaced.

**NORA:** And governance?

**JAMES:** We found no public AI policy or security certifications. Online ordering takes payments, and food businesses are health-regulated, so there are obvious areas where good practice matters. A customer-facing AI also needs guardrails. It shouldn't tell someone a product is allergen-free if it isn't, for example. We have no reason to think Shipley's assistant gets this wrong, but it's the kind of thing a responsible rollout has to think about.

**NORA:** Leadership churn?

**JAMES:** I'd frame it as change rather than churn. A new chief marketing officer and three new senior leaders in the first year under a new owner is a big refresh. It could be exactly what the company needs for its next stage, and the new leaders could sponsor more digital and AI work. But new leadership teams take time to gel, and it's happening at the same time as a fast expansion.

### Opportunities

**NORA:** Okay. Opportunities. Where would you push?

**JAMES:** The first is the one the AI itself points to: extend the assistant from building catering orders into catering quoting and production planning. Today the assistant helps the guest build an order. Tomorrow it could give a quote for complex events, suggest add-ons, and pass a clear production plan to the shop. The value levers are franchisee labour hours and catering conversion.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, daily production forecasting by shop, like Raj's example. This is, in our view, the biggest operational prize. Less waste means better food cost and margin. Fewer stockouts means more sales during the rush. And it particularly helps new shops in new markets, where franchisees have no history to go on.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, personalisation and loyalty. The assistant already learns from guest orders. With consent, that could drive smarter suggestions, like recommending a kolache to a donut-only customer, or flagging a new LTO to someone who loved the last one. That's cross-sell, and it fits Shipley's dual menu perfectly.

**NORA:** Fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, voice. Phone orders and drive-thrus are huge for morning businesses. Dairy Queen is piloting voice AI in its drive-thrus, as we mentioned, though we haven't seen verified outcomes. Shipley could extend its assistant to phone ordering first, which is lower risk than drive-thru, and use the same understanding of group orders. Voice in drive-thrus is harder, because of noise, accents and speed, and the industry's experience with it has been mixed, so I'd approach it carefully.

**NORA:** Fifth?

**JAMES:** Fifth, the non-AI one that drives everything: keep signing multi-unit franchise deals in new markets. Michigan with fifteen units, Maryland with twenty-five, Ohio with three, Raleigh. Every new shop is a new royalty stream and a new source of data for the forecasting models.

**NORA:** And acquisitions?

**JAMES:** Possibly. No acquisitions have been announced, but in franchising, owners sometimes buy out large franchisees or consolidate territories. That's our speculation, not a plan we've seen. It could make sense where the brand wants tighter control in a key market.

**NORA:** One more. What about using AI for franchisee support?

**JAMES:** Good one. A franchise system runs on its operations manual, training and field support. An assistant that answers franchisees' operational questions, like "how do I set up the new LTO display" or "what's the procedure if the fryer fails", could reduce the load on field teams. That's especially useful when you're onboarding lots of new franchisees in distant markets.

### The best AI bet

**NORA:** If you had to pick one bet for the next eighteen months?

**JAMES:** Extend the AI catering and group order builder into quoting and production planning. The assistant already handles catering, and the group channel is where the stated time saving sits. Connecting the order to the shop's production plan is the bridge between a customer-facing feature and an operational system. It helps guests, helps franchisees, and creates the data foundation for daily forecasting.

**NORA:** First hundred days?

**JAMES:** Days one to thirty: understand what's actually happening. Pull the data on the assistant. What share of orders, what kinds, what average order size compared with the normal menu, where people drop off. Talk to franchisees about catering: what goes wrong, how much notice they get, how much waste. Appoint one owner, probably sitting between technology and operations, with franchisee representation.

**NORA:** Days thirty to sixty?

**JAMES:** Pick a pilot group of maybe ten to twenty shops, a mix of mature Houston shops and newer ones in new markets. For those shops, send every group order from the assistant into a simple production plan, with notice of upcoming large orders. In parallel, build a basic daily production forecast for each pilot shop, and run it in shadow mode, so the shop sees the suggestion but makes its own decision.

**NORA:** And sixty to a hundred?

**JAMES:** Measure. Did catering conversion go up? Did waste go down on days with large orders? Were the forecasts closer to reality than the shops' own plans? Did franchisees find it useful, or annoying? Then decide on rollout, and agree the terms with franchisees, including whether there's any cost to them. In franchising, how you roll something out matters as much as what you roll out.

**NORA:** What's the success metric?

**JAMES:** Two numbers I'd want. Waste as a share of production in pilot shops, and catering revenue per shop. If both move the right way, you have a story franchisees will sell to each other.

**NORA:** Second-best bet?

**JAMES:** Phone ordering with voice AI, using the same order logic. It's a natural extension of a proven assistant into a channel where many group orders still arrive.

### Lessons for other businesses

**NORA:** Let's step back. What can other businesses, especially other franchise businesses, learn from Shipley?

**JAMES:** I'd pull out four. First, put AI where customers actually struggle. Shipley didn't put AI into ordering a single donut, which is easy. It focused on group orders, where customers genuinely struggle with quantities and mix. That's why people use it.

**NORA:** Second?

**JAMES:** Second, report adoption, not launch. The most powerful thing about Shipley's story is the "one in four" number. It's a company claim, but it's the right kind of claim. Any business launching AI should decide upfront what usage it expects and then tell people what happened. That builds credibility with customers, franchisees and investors.

**NORA:** Third?

**JAMES:** Third, in a franchise, centralised AI is the easiest AI. A feature on the brand's own website reaches the whole system without every franchisee buying and installing something. If you run a franchise system, look for AI you can deliver from the centre, where franchisees get the benefit without the hassle.

**NORA:** And fourth?

**JAMES:** Fourth, connect the front and the back. Customer-facing AI creates data about demand. If that data stays in marketing, you've captured half the value. Feed it into production, staffing and purchasing, and you turn a nice feature into a better business.

**NORA:** And a fifth, about buying versus building?

**JAMES:** Yes. Buying from a vendor like Saivory got Shipley to market quickly, and for a business this size that's usually the right choice. But plan for how you'll stay ahead when competitors can buy the same tools. The answer is usually data, integration and how well your people use it.

### Risks and what to watch

**NORA:** Let's run the risks.

**JAMES:** One, AI-native ordering vendors working with bigger competitors, like Precitaste with Dunkin', closing the gap on Shipley's digital lead. Two, Dunkin' expanding in Houston, attacking the home market. Three, franchisee economics: if margins compress, AI and other investment may stall. Four, expansion risk in markets where the brand is unknown. Five, ordering moving to third-party AI platforms, taking customers and data with it.

**NORA:** And for the owner?

**JAMES:** It's early, about fifteen months in. The task is execution: make the new leadership team work, keep the expansion pipeline converting into open, profitable shops, and turn the AI story from one feature into an operational advantage. If that happens, Shipley could become a reference case for AI in franchising.

**NORA:** What should listeners watch?

**JAMES:** Four things. First, any update on the AI assistant's usage. Is it still around one in four, has it gone up, has it spread to phone or other channels? Second, any announcement about AI in production or forecasting. Third, how the Michigan, Maryland and Raleigh shops perform, which we'll see in the pace of openings and further deals. And fourth, whether the new leaders talk about digital and AI as a strategic priority.

**NORA:** And what's missing from the public record?

**JAMES:** Revenue, unit economics, average order value through the assistant versus the normal menu, how much catering contributes, and franchisee satisfaction. Those would tell you how much the AI is really worth.
