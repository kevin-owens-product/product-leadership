### INTRO (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** Welcome back to The Forge Podcast, where we dig into the intersection of private equity, software transformation, and what it actually means to build for an AI-native future. I'm Alex, and I'm here once again with Kevin. Last episode we covered how PE firms think about technology transformation at the portfolio level. Today we are going way deeper on the technical side, because there is one architecture pattern that keeps coming up in every serious enterprise AI conversation we are part of, and that is multi-agent systems. Kevin, before we get into the weeds, I want to set the stage. There is a lot of confusion out there right now. People hear "AI agent" and they think of a chatbot with a nicer UI. They hear "multi-agent" and they think it is some kind of science fiction scenario where robots are negotiating with each other. So let us start from the ground floor. What does multi-agent architecture actually mean, and why should an enterprise care about it today?

**KEVIN:** Yeah, I think the confusion is completely understandable, because the terminology has gotten ahead of the reality in a lot of marketing materials. So let me draw some really clear lines. A chatbot is a stateless question-and-answer interface. You ask it something, it responds, and for the most part it does not take any action in the world. A single AI agent is a meaningfully different thing. It is an LLM that has been given tools, a defined scope of responsibility, and the ability to take actions, things like reading from a database, calling an API, sending an email, or updating a record. The agent can reason about what tools to use and in what sequence. That is already a big step up from a chatbot. Now, a multi-agent system is the leap from a solo practitioner to a coordinated team. You have multiple agents, each one specialized in a different domain, each one equipped with its own set of tools, and they communicate with each other to solve problems that are too complex or too broad for any single agent to handle well.

**ALEX:** That distinction between a chatbot and an agent feels like the one that trips people up the most. The chatbot just talks. The agent actually does things in the world. And the multi-agent system is a team of agents that can divide and conquer. Is that a fair summary?

**KEVIN:** That is exactly right. And the reason this matters for enterprises specifically is that enterprise problems are almost never single-domain problems. A customer service request might touch your CRM, your billing system, your product knowledge base, and your logistics platform all in the same interaction. Trying to build one monolithic agent that understands all of those systems deeply, has access to all of those tools, and can reason perfectly across all of those domains is a recipe for mediocrity at best and catastrophic errors at worst. Multi-agent architecture is a recognition that specialization and coordination, which is how every successful organization of humans works, is also how you should organize AI systems for complex work.

### SEGMENT 1: The Hospital Analogy — Why Specialists Beat Generalists (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** I want to stay on this point about specialization for a minute, because I think it is the core insight that makes multi-agent architecture click for people. You have used an analogy before that I found really compelling, comparing a multi-agent system to a hospital. Can you walk us through that?

**KEVIN:** Absolutely, and I keep coming back to the hospital analogy because it maps almost perfectly onto how you should think about designing agent systems. When you walk into a hospital with a complex medical issue, you do not see one doctor who does everything. You do not have a single physician who reads your MRI, performs your surgery, manages your anesthesia, prescribes your post-operative medication, handles your physical therapy plan, and then also processes your insurance claim. That would be absurd. Instead, you have a radiologist who is world-class at reading imaging. You have a surgeon who has done a thousand procedures of the specific type you need. You have an anesthesiologist who specializes in keeping you alive and pain-free during the operation. Each one of those specialists has spent years building deep expertise in their narrow domain, and the quality of care you receive depends on each one being excellent at their specific job.

**ALEX:** And the coordination piece is just as important, right? Having great specialists does not help if they are not talking to each other. I have had the experience of being a patient where my cardiologist did not know what my primary care doctor had prescribed, and that is a dangerous situation.

**KEVIN:** That is exactly the right instinct, and it maps directly onto the architecture. In a hospital, the coordination layer is a combination of things. You have the attending physician who acts as the orchestrator, deciding which specialists need to be involved. You have the patient's medical record, which is the shared memory that every specialist reads from and writes to. You have established protocols for handoffs, like how a surgeon communicates post-op instructions to the nursing team. And you have escalation paths for when something goes wrong or when a case falls outside normal parameters. Every single one of those elements has a direct analog in multi-agent software architecture. The orchestrator decides which agent handles which part of a request. The shared context store is the medical record. The communication protocols are how agents pass information to each other. And the escalation paths are how the system handles failures or ambiguous situations.

**ALEX:** So let me make sure I am connecting this to concrete software. If I am building a multi-agent system for, say, a large insurance company, what would the specialist agents actually look like?

**KEVIN:** Great example. In an insurance context you might have a Claims Intake Agent that specializes in parsing incoming claim documents, extracting structured data, and classifying the claim type. You would have a Policy Verification Agent that checks the claimant's policy details, coverage limits, and exclusions. A Fraud Detection Agent that runs the claim against known fraud patterns and anomaly models. An Adjudication Agent that applies the policy rules to the claim facts and recommends a payout amount. A Communications Agent that drafts correspondence to the claimant, whether that is a request for additional documentation or a settlement offer. And a Compliance Agent that ensures everything meets regulatory requirements for the jurisdiction. Each of those agents has a fundamentally different job. The Fraud Detection Agent needs access to historical fraud databases and anomaly detection models. The Communications Agent needs access to letter templates and tone guidelines and the claimant's communication history. Trying to stuff all of that into one agent would mean giving it an enormous system prompt, dozens of tools, and expecting it to context-switch between radically different reasoning modes. The result would be an agent that is mediocre at everything.

**ALEX:** And the specialization also helps with something else that enterprises care a lot about, which is auditability. If your Fraud Detection Agent flags something, you can look at exactly what that specific agent saw and how it reasoned, without having to untangle it from the adjudication logic or the communications logic.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. Auditability and debuggability are enormous practical advantages of the multi-agent approach. When something goes wrong, and things will go wrong, you can isolate the failure to a specific agent, examine its inputs and outputs, and fix the problem without touching the rest of the system. In a monolithic agent, a bug in fraud detection logic could subtly corrupt adjudication decisions, and you might not even notice for weeks. With separate agents, the blast radius of any individual failure is contained to that agent's domain.

### SEGMENT 2: Orchestration Patterns — Router, Pipeline, and Hierarchical (12 minutes)

**ALEX:** Alright, so we have established why you want multiple specialized agents. Now let us talk about the part that I think is the hardest to get right, which is orchestration. How do these agents actually coordinate? What are the main patterns you see in production systems?

**KEVIN:** Orchestration is the make-or-break layer of any multi-agent system, and I have seen three primary patterns that cover the vast majority of enterprise use cases. The first and simplest is the Router pattern. The second is the Pipeline pattern. And the third is the Hierarchical pattern. Most real-world systems use all three, selecting the appropriate pattern based on the nature of each incoming request. Let me walk through each one.

**ALEX:** Let us start with the Router, since you said it is the simplest.

**KEVIN:** The Router pattern is exactly what it sounds like. A request comes in, a routing layer analyzes it and determines which single agent is best equipped to handle it, and sends it directly to that agent. The agent processes the request, produces a response, and that response goes back to the user. There is no multi-agent collaboration happening at all. It is just intelligent dispatch. And honestly, for a huge percentage of enterprise interactions, maybe 60 to 70 percent, this is all you need. "What is the status of order number 4521?" That goes straight to the Order Management Agent. "What is our current inventory level for SKU 8890?" That goes straight to the Inventory Agent. The routing decision itself can be made by a lightweight classifier, often a smaller and cheaper model, that looks at the intent of the request and maps it to the right agent. The beauty of the Router pattern is that it is fast, it is cheap, and it is extremely easy to debug. If the user got a wrong answer, you look at two things: did the router send it to the right agent, and did that agent produce the right output? That is it.

**ALEX:** That makes sense. But what about the cases where a question touches multiple domains? "Compare our Q3 inventory costs against our supplier contract terms and flag any discrepancies." That is not a single-agent question.

**KEVIN:** Right, and that is where the Pipeline pattern comes in. In a Pipeline, you have a sequence of agents that each process the request in turn, with each agent's output becoming the next agent's input. For the example you just gave, the orchestrator might construct a pipeline like this. Step one: the Inventory Agent pulls the Q3 inventory cost data and structures it. Step two: the Procurement Agent retrieves the relevant supplier contract terms and pricing schedules. Step three: an Analysis Agent receives both datasets, performs the comparison, and identifies discrepancies. Step four: a Reporting Agent takes the analysis and formats it into an executive summary with specific recommendations. Each agent in that pipeline has a well-defined input and a well-defined output. The Inventory Agent does not need to know anything about supplier contracts. The Procurement Agent does not need to know how to format executive reports. They each do their one job and pass the baton.

**ALEX:** So the pipeline is sequential. Each step waits for the previous step. What about cases where you could parallelize?

**KEVIN:** That is a great question, and it leads directly to a common variant of the Pipeline which I think of as the Fan-Out-and-Merge pattern. Instead of a purely linear sequence, the orchestrator sends the request to multiple agents simultaneously, waits for all of them to return their results, and then passes the collected results to a synthesis agent. Going back to your example, the orchestrator could send the inventory data request and the contract terms request in parallel, since neither depends on the other. Then once both have returned, the Analysis Agent gets both datasets at the same time. This cuts your latency significantly compared to running everything sequentially, and in enterprise scenarios where you might be fanning out to five or six agents, the latency savings are substantial. The key architectural decision is figuring out which steps have true data dependencies and which can run concurrently. You want to maximize parallelism without ever sending an agent incomplete input.

**ALEX:** Alright, so we have the Router for simple dispatch, and the Pipeline for sequential or parallelizable multi-step workflows. What is the Hierarchical pattern?

**KEVIN:** The Hierarchical pattern is for the most complex scenarios, and it is where you start to see the real power of multi-agent systems. In a Hierarchical architecture, you have agents that can themselves delegate to sub-agents. Think of it like a management org chart. You have a top-level orchestrator that receives the request and delegates to department-level agents. Each department-level agent can further decompose its piece of the problem and delegate to specialist agents beneath it. The department-level agent is responsible for coordinating its own sub-agents and returning a coherent result to the top-level orchestrator.

**ALEX:** Can you give me a concrete example? I want to make sure I understand how this plays out in practice.

**KEVIN:** Sure. Imagine a private equity firm wants to evaluate a potential acquisition target. The top-level orchestrator receives the request: "Perform due diligence on Company X." It delegates to three department-level agents: a Financial Analysis Agent, an Operational Analysis Agent, and a Market Analysis Agent. The Financial Analysis Agent, in turn, delegates to its own sub-agents. A Revenue Agent that analyzes revenue composition and trends. A Cost Structure Agent that examines the cost base and margin trajectory. A Balance Sheet Agent that evaluates asset quality and liability risk. A Cash Flow Agent that models working capital dynamics and free cash flow generation. Each of those sub-agents does its deep analysis, reports back to the Financial Analysis Agent, which synthesizes a financial summary. Meanwhile, the Operational Analysis Agent is doing the same thing with its own sub-agents focused on technology stack, team composition, customer concentration, and so on. The Market Analysis Agent has sub-agents looking at competitive landscape, market sizing, and regulatory environment. Finally, all three department-level summaries come back to the top-level orchestrator, which synthesizes the comprehensive due diligence report.

**ALEX:** That is a genuinely impressive use case. But I imagine the complexity of managing that hierarchy is significant. How do you keep track of what is happening at each level?

**KEVIN:** You are right that it adds complexity, and this is where I want to be honest about design trade-offs. The Hierarchical pattern gives you the most power, but it also gives you the most failure modes and the hardest debugging experience. Every layer of delegation is another place where context can be lost, where an agent can misinterpret its instructions, or where a timeout can cascade. My general guidance is to start with the Router pattern, move to Pipelines when you have clear multi-step workflows, and only adopt the Hierarchical pattern when the problem genuinely has that kind of nested decomposition structure. Most enterprise use cases, maybe 80 to 85 percent, can be handled well by Routers and Pipelines alone. You should not reach for the Hierarchical pattern just because it feels more sophisticated. Sophistication in architecture should always be earned by genuine complexity in the problem, never imposed for its own sake.

### SEGMENT 3: Enterprise Use Cases — Where Multi-Agent Shines (12 minutes)

**ALEX:** Let us get really practical now. I want to walk through three specific enterprise use cases where multi-agent architecture delivers clear value, and talk about how you would actually design each one. You pick the first one.

**KEVIN:** Let us start with customer support triage, because it is one of the most common and most immediately valuable applications. In a traditional setup, a customer contacts support, and either a human agent handles everything from scratch, or a basic chatbot tries to answer the question with a canned response and fails half the time. In a multi-agent design, here is what happens instead. First, an Intent Classification Agent analyzes the incoming message and determines what category of issue this is. Is it a billing dispute, a technical problem, a feature request, a cancellation attempt, a compliance question? This agent is lightweight and fast because its only job is classification, not resolution.

**ALEX:** So the classification agent is basically the receptionist at the front desk, deciding which department you need.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. Based on the classification, the orchestrator routes to the appropriate specialist agent. A Billing Agent that has access to the customer's invoicing history, payment methods, and subscription details, and knows the company's policies around refunds and credits. A Technical Support Agent that has access to the product documentation, known bug databases, and can run diagnostic checks on the customer's account configuration. A Retention Agent that is specifically trained on de-escalation, has access to available retention offers, and understands the customer's lifetime value and usage patterns to make personalized save attempts. Here is what makes this powerful. The Billing Agent does not need to know anything about troubleshooting technical issues. The Technical Support Agent does not need to understand retention offer eligibility rules. And the Retention Agent does not need to understand the intricacies of invoice proration. Each agent is sharp in its domain. But the real magic is in the handoffs. If the Technical Support Agent is working on a customer's issue and discovers that the root cause is actually a billing error, it does not try to handle the billing side itself. It escalates to the Billing Agent with a structured summary of what it found, so the Billing Agent has full context without the customer having to repeat themselves.

**ALEX:** That handoff experience is so important, because the number one complaint people have with customer support is having to explain their problem multiple times. If the agents are sharing context correctly, the customer should never have to repeat anything.

**KEVIN:** Precisely. And there is an economic argument too. In a traditional contact center, you either train every agent on everything, which is expensive and produces mediocre generalists, or you have specialized tiers that require manual transfers, which means long hold times and customer frustration. The multi-agent approach gives you specialization without transfer friction. We have seen implementations cut average handle time by 40 percent and improve first-contact resolution by 25 percent because the right specialist is working the problem from the start with full context.

**ALEX:** Alright, let us do the second use case. How about financial analysis?

**KEVIN:** Financial analysis is a great one because it naturally decomposes into specialist domains. Picture a mid-market company, maybe 200 million in revenue, that needs to produce a monthly financial review for its PE sponsors. Today that is probably a two-week process involving the FP&A team pulling data from six different systems, the controller reconciling and validating, and someone in investor relations formatting the narrative. In a multi-agent system, you would have a Data Extraction Agent that connects to the ERP, the CRM, the HRIS, and the billing platform and pulls the relevant financial data for the period. A Variance Analysis Agent that compares actuals to budget and prior period, identifies material variances, and provides preliminary explanations by correlating with operational data. A Narrative Agent that takes the variance analysis and generates the management discussion section, using the company's tone and the specific format the PE sponsors expect. A Forecasting Agent that updates the rolling forecast based on the latest actuals and any known changes to the business pipeline. And a Quality Assurance Agent that cross-checks the entire package for internal consistency, making sure the numbers in the narrative match the numbers in the tables, that the variance explanations are mathematically coherent, and that nothing violates basic accounting identities.

**ALEX:** That Quality Assurance Agent is interesting. It is essentially an automated reviewer.

**KEVIN:** It is, and it catches an astonishing number of errors. In manual processes, the most common mistakes are not math errors in the core financials. They are inconsistencies between different parts of the report. The narrative says revenue grew eight percent, but the table shows seven point six percent because someone rounded differently. The forecast section references a headcount number that does not match the headcount in the actuals section because they were updated at different times. A QA Agent can catch all of those inconsistencies in seconds, every time, without fatigue.

**ALEX:** Let us do one more. Supply chain feels like a natural fit for multi-agent given how many different domains it spans.

**KEVIN:** Supply chain is arguably the use case where multi-agent architecture delivers the most value relative to alternatives, precisely because of that domain breadth. A supply chain decision involves demand forecasting, inventory optimization, supplier management, logistics routing, cost analysis, and risk assessment. No single agent or single human, for that matter, can hold all of those domains in their head simultaneously and make optimal trade-offs. Here is how I would design it. A Demand Forecasting Agent that analyzes historical sales data, seasonality patterns, promotional calendars, and external signals like economic indicators or weather data to predict demand at the SKU level. An Inventory Optimization Agent that takes the demand forecast and determines optimal stock levels, reorder points, and safety stock quantities, balancing carrying costs against stockout risk. A Supplier Management Agent that tracks supplier performance, lead times, capacity constraints, and pricing, and can recommend alternative suppliers when primary suppliers have issues. A Logistics Agent that optimizes shipping routes, carrier selection, and warehouse allocation. And a Risk Agent that monitors for disruptions, things like port congestion, geopolitical events, supplier financial distress, or raw material price spikes, and triggers contingency plans.

**ALEX:** And the key insight is that these agents are not operating in isolation. The Demand Forecasting Agent's output feeds into the Inventory Optimization Agent. The Risk Agent's alerts might cause the Supplier Management Agent to activate backup sourcing. It is a genuinely interconnected system.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And the orchestration for supply chain tends to be a mix of all three patterns we discussed. Day-to-day queries, like "what is our current inventory position for this product category," use the simple Router. Replenishment planning uses the Pipeline, flowing from demand forecast through inventory optimization through supplier ordering. And strategic decisions, like "should we shift our sourcing mix given the new tariff environment," use the Hierarchical pattern, with multiple agents doing deep analysis that gets synthesized into a recommendation. That mix of orchestration patterns within a single system is what makes multi-agent architecture so well suited to supply chain. The problem itself is multi-modal, sometimes simple queries, sometimes complex workflows, sometimes strategic analysis, and the architecture can flex to match.

### SEGMENT 4: Agent Communication and Failure Handling (10 minutes)

**ALEX:** We have talked about the big patterns, but I want to go a level deeper on how agents actually communicate with each other. What does the message passing look like between agents, and what happens when an agent fails or returns garbage?

**KEVIN:** Agent communication is one of those areas where the implementation details really matter, and where a lot of teams get into trouble by under-specifying the contract between agents. The fundamental principle is that agents should communicate through structured, typed messages, not through free-form natural language. When the Intent Classification Agent passes a request to the Billing Agent, it should not just forward the customer's raw text. It should send a structured object that includes the classified intent, the extracted entities like account ID and invoice number, the customer's sentiment score, and any relevant context from prior interactions. That structured handoff means the Billing Agent does not have to re-parse the customer's message. It gets clean, validated input that it can act on immediately.

**ALEX:** That makes sense. You are basically defining an API contract between agents, the same way you would define an API contract between microservices.

**KEVIN:** That is exactly the right mental model, and it is not a coincidence. Multi-agent architecture has a lot in common with microservice architecture, and the lessons we learned in the microservice era about well-defined interfaces, contract testing, and backward compatibility all apply here. One pattern I recommend strongly is defining a shared schema registry for inter-agent messages. Every agent publishes the schema of messages it can receive and the schema of messages it produces. The orchestrator validates messages against these schemas before forwarding them. That catches a huge category of errors before they propagate. If the Demand Forecasting Agent changes the format of its output, the schema validation will catch the incompatibility before the Inventory Optimization Agent tries to parse it and produces nonsensical results.

**ALEX:** Alright, now let us talk about failures, because I think this is where the rubber meets the road for enterprise adoption. Executives will tolerate a lot of things, but they will not tolerate a system that fails catastrophically with no explanation.

**KEVIN:** Failure handling is arguably the most important design consideration in any multi-agent system, and I think about it at three levels. The first level is individual agent failure. An agent might time out, return a malformed response, or produce a result that fails a quality check. For each agent, you need a clear fallback strategy. The simplest fallback is retry with a slightly different prompt or with additional context. If the retry also fails, you can try a different model, maybe the agent normally uses a smaller model for cost reasons, but on failure you escalate to a more capable model. And if all automated retries fail, you need a graceful degradation path, which usually means routing to a human with full context about what the agent tried and why it failed.

**ALEX:** So graceful degradation means the system gets worse gradually, not all at once. It is like how a car with a flat tire can still limp to the shoulder at low speed rather than flipping over.

**KEVIN:** Right. And the key word is "graceful." The system should never just return an error message to the user and leave it at that. If the Billing Agent fails, the system might say, "I was not able to fully resolve your billing question automatically. I have gathered the following information so far," and then present whatever partial results it has, along with a message that a human specialist will follow up within a certain timeframe. The customer gets a partial answer immediately plus a clear expectation for full resolution, which is vastly better than a blank error screen. The second level of failure handling is pipeline failure. In a multi-step workflow, what happens if agent three in a five-step pipeline fails? You need to decide: can you skip that step and continue with a degraded result, or is that step's output essential for downstream agents? For each step in a pipeline, you should have a classification of whether it is critical or enrichment. Critical steps, if they fail, halt the pipeline and trigger escalation. Enrichment steps, if they fail, get skipped and the result is flagged as incomplete but still useful.

**ALEX:** That critical versus enrichment distinction feels very practical. Can you give an example?

**KEVIN:** Sure. In the financial analysis pipeline we discussed earlier, the Data Extraction Agent and the Variance Analysis Agent are critical. Without accurate data and variance calculations, the rest of the report is meaningless. But the Narrative Agent is enrichment. If it fails, you can still deliver the financial tables and variance analysis to the PE sponsor with a note that the narrative commentary will follow. The numbers are what matter most, and the narrative is valuable but not essential. That means a Narrative Agent failure does not block the entire financial review. The third level is systemic failure, where the orchestrator itself has issues, or where you are seeing correlated failures across multiple agents. This is where your monitoring and alerting infrastructure becomes critical. You need to track per-agent success rates, latency distributions, and error types in real time. If your Fraud Detection Agent's success rate drops from 98 percent to 70 percent over an hour, that might indicate a data source outage or a model degradation, and you want to catch that before it causes downstream problems in every pipeline that depends on fraud scoring.

**ALEX:** This reminds me of the circuit breaker pattern from distributed systems design. If an agent is failing consistently, you stop sending it traffic and route around it rather than letting every request fail.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. Circuit breakers are directly applicable here. If an agent has failed five times in the last minute, trip the circuit, stop routing to that agent, and either use a fallback agent, degrade gracefully, or queue the requests for when the agent recovers. The distributed systems community spent two decades figuring out how to build resilient systems from unreliable components, and almost all of those patterns, circuit breakers, bulkheads, timeouts, retries with exponential backoff, apply directly to multi-agent architectures. The fact that the components happen to be LLM-based agents rather than traditional microservices does not change the fundamental reliability engineering principles.

### SEGMENT 5: Cost Management for Multi-Agent Systems (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** I want to shift to a topic that comes up in every single conversation I have with PE operating partners about AI, which is cost. Multi-agent systems sound great in theory, but you are making potentially dozens of LLM calls per user interaction. How do you keep the economics viable?

**KEVIN:** Cost management is absolutely critical, and it is one of the areas where naive implementations get into trouble fast. Let me walk through the main levers you have. The first and most impactful lever is model tiering. Not every agent needs the most expensive model. Your Intent Classification Agent that is essentially doing pattern matching against a known taxonomy of request types? That can run on a small, fast, cheap model and perform just as well as the frontier model for that specific task. Your Narrative Generation Agent that needs to produce fluent, nuanced prose? That probably does need a more capable model. In a well-designed system, you might have 60 to 70 percent of your agent calls running on models that cost one-tenth or one-twentieth what the frontier model costs per token. That alone can reduce your overall LLM spend by 50 percent or more compared to a naive approach of running everything on the most capable model.

**ALEX:** And I imagine the latency benefits compound on the cost benefits, because the cheaper models also tend to be faster, which means shorter processing times per interaction.

**KEVIN:** Exactly right. Faster models mean lower latency for the user, and in systems that are processing high volumes, they also mean you need less concurrent capacity. The second lever is intelligent context management. One of the biggest cost drivers in LLM-based systems is the size of the context window you are sending with each call. If you are naively forwarding the entire conversation history plus the full shared memory state to every agent, you are paying for a massive number of tokens on every call, most of which are irrelevant to that specific agent's task. Instead, each agent should receive a curated context that includes only the information it needs. The Billing Agent does not need to see the full technical diagnostic history from the Technical Support Agent's prior analysis. It needs a structured summary: here is the customer, here is the issue, here is what has been tried. Aggressive context pruning can reduce your average token usage per agent call by 40 to 60 percent.

**ALEX:** So you are essentially paying attention to the information architecture of what flows between agents, not just throwing everything into the context and hoping the model figures out what is relevant.

**KEVIN:** Precisely. And this connects back to the structured message schemas we discussed earlier. When your inter-agent messages are well-structured, it is easy to select exactly the fields that a downstream agent needs. When they are free-form text, you end up forwarding everything because you cannot easily extract the relevant subset. The third lever is caching. Many agent calls, especially for reference data lookups, produce the same output for the same input. If ten customers ask about the same return policy, the Policy Agent should not be making a fresh LLM call each time. A semantic caching layer that recognizes when an incoming request is substantially similar to a recently processed request can serve the cached result in milliseconds at zero token cost. In high-volume customer support scenarios, caching can handle 30 to 40 percent of interactions.

**ALEX:** What about the total cost per interaction? Do you have benchmarks for what a well-optimized multi-agent system costs versus a single-agent system versus a human?

**KEVIN:** The numbers vary enormously based on the complexity of the domain and the volume of interactions, but here are some rough benchmarks from systems I have been involved with. For a customer support interaction of moderate complexity, a well-optimized multi-agent system might involve four to six agent calls, with an average blended cost of maybe two to five cents per interaction. A single-agent approach handling the same interaction might cost one to three cents because it is making fewer calls, but it also has a lower resolution rate, so more interactions escalate to humans. A human agent handling that same interaction costs somewhere between five and fifteen dollars when you account for salary, benefits, training, tooling, and management overhead. So even if the multi-agent system costs two to three times more in LLM spend than a single-agent system, the incremental cost is pennies, and it is buying you a significantly higher automated resolution rate, which saves dollars per interaction in avoided human escalations. The economic argument for multi-agent is not that each individual LLM call is cheap. It is that the higher resolution rate drives massive overall cost savings compared to the alternative of more human handling.

**ALEX:** That framing is important. You are optimizing for total cost of resolution, not for cost per LLM call.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. And the fourth lever, which is more strategic, is building feedback loops that continuously improve each agent's performance. As your agents get better at their specific tasks over time, they need fewer retries, fewer escalations, and produce higher quality results with shorter prompts. That creates a virtuous cycle where cost per interaction naturally decreases as the system matures. The best multi-agent systems I have seen reduce their per-interaction cost by 30 to 50 percent over the first six months of operation purely through agent improvement and prompt optimization.

### SEGMENT 6: When NOT to Use Multi-Agent Architecture (8 minutes)

**ALEX:** Alright, we have spent most of this episode talking about why multi-agent is great and how to do it well. But I want to flip the script, because I think you feel strongly that there are many situations where multi-agent is the wrong choice. When should you not use this pattern?

**KEVIN:** I feel very strongly about this, and I appreciate you bringing it up, because the single biggest mistake I see teams make is reaching for multi-agent architecture when a simpler approach would work just as well or better. Let me give you my simplicity principle: you should always use the simplest architecture that meets your requirements, and you should have to justify every incremental unit of complexity with a concrete, measurable benefit. Multi-agent is not inherently better than single-agent. It is better for problems that have certain characteristics, and worse for problems that do not.

**ALEX:** So what are the specific situations where you would say, "Do not build this as multi-agent"?

**KEVIN:** The first is when the problem is single-domain. If all you need is a system that answers questions about your product documentation, that is a single-agent RAG application. You do not need a multi-agent system. Adding agents for the sake of having agents adds latency, cost, and failure modes without adding capability. I have seen teams decompose a simple Q&A system into a "retrieval agent," a "ranking agent," a "synthesis agent," and a "formatting agent," and the result was four times slower, three times more expensive, and no better at answering questions than a well-designed single agent with good retrieval. That is complexity theater, not architecture.

**ALEX:** Complexity theater. I like that term. It is building something complicated to look sophisticated rather than to solve a genuine problem.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. The second situation is when your volume does not justify the investment. A multi-agent system has significant upfront engineering cost in designing the orchestration layer, defining agent boundaries, building the communication protocols, implementing failure handling, setting up monitoring, and so on. If you are processing fifty interactions a day, that investment may never pay off. You might be better served by a single well-prompted agent that handles 80 percent of cases and escalates the other 20 percent to humans. The math changes when you are processing five thousand or fifty thousand interactions a day, because at that scale the efficiency gains from specialization compound enormously.

**ALEX:** That is a good point about scale. Is there a rough threshold where multi-agent starts to make sense?

**KEVIN:** It depends heavily on the complexity of the domain, but as a rough heuristic, I would say multi-agent starts to earn its keep when you have at least three genuinely distinct domains involved in your workflow, when you are processing hundreds of interactions per day or more, and when the cost of errors is high enough that specialist-grade quality matters. If any of those three conditions is not met, strongly consider a simpler approach first. The third situation where I advise against multi-agent is when the problem does not have natural decomposition boundaries. Not every problem breaks cleanly into specialist domains. Some problems are inherently holistic, where the reasoning requires simultaneously weighing factors from multiple domains in a way that cannot be decomposed and recomposed without losing important nuance. Creative strategy, for instance, or complex negotiation. If you find yourself forcing artificial boundaries between agents, that is a sign the problem might be better served by a single powerful agent with broad context.

**ALEX:** What about the team dimension? I imagine the team's ability to build and maintain this kind of system matters too.

**KEVIN:** Tremendously. A multi-agent system is a distributed system, and distributed systems require distributed systems expertise. You need engineers who understand asynchronous communication, failure handling, observability, and debugging across service boundaries. If your team does not have that expertise, the operational burden of a multi-agent system can be crushing. I have seen teams build impressive multi-agent prototypes that worked beautifully in demos but were completely unmaintainable in production because nobody could debug the inter-agent communication failures or understand why the orchestrator was making certain routing decisions. My advice is always: start with a single agent, prove the value of AI automation for your use case, learn what works and what does not, and then decompose into multiple agents only when you hit concrete limitations of the single-agent approach. Let the problem pull you toward multi-agent rather than pushing the architecture onto the problem.

**ALEX:** That is really wise counsel. Start simple, prove value, and let complexity be earned by real needs rather than imposed by theoretical elegance.

**KEVIN:** And I want to add one more thing. Even within a multi-agent system, you should constantly be asking whether each agent is earning its place. If your Formatting Agent is basically just applying a template, maybe that should be a simple function call, not a full LLM-powered agent. If your Classification Agent is mapping inputs to one of eight categories with 99 percent accuracy, maybe a traditional ML classifier or even a rules engine would be faster, cheaper, and more reliable. The best multi-agent architectures are hybrid systems that use LLM-powered agents where you need flexible reasoning, and use simpler components everywhere else. Not every node in the graph needs to be an AI agent. Some of them should just be code.

### SEGMENT 7: Designing for the Future — Putting It All Together (5 minutes)

**ALEX:** We are coming to the end of our time, and I want to close with a forward-looking question. We are still in the early chapters of multi-agent architecture in the enterprise. What do you think changes over the next two to three years, and what advice would you give to someone designing a multi-agent system today that needs to be maintainable and extensible as the technology evolves?

**KEVIN:** Three things I am watching closely. First, agent-to-agent communication standards are going to mature significantly. Right now every team is inventing their own inter-agent messaging format, their own orchestration protocol, their own shared memory architecture. We are going to see industry standards emerge, similar to how REST and GraphQL standardized API communication. When that happens, you will be able to mix and match agents from different vendors, swap out individual agents without redesigning the whole system, and benefit from shared tooling around debugging and monitoring. Teams that design with clean interfaces between their agents today will be best positioned to adopt those standards when they arrive.

**ALEX:** So the principle is: invest in the interfaces between your agents, because those interfaces are what will allow you to evolve each agent independently.

**KEVIN:** Exactly. The second thing I am watching is the cost curve. Model inference costs have been dropping by roughly 50 percent every six to nine months. Use cases that are marginally economical today become overwhelmingly economical in a year or two. This means you should design your agent boundaries based on what makes architectural sense, not based on what you can afford today. If the right design has eight agents but you can only afford to run five with current model costs, build the architecture for eight and use simpler fallbacks for the three you cannot afford yet. When costs drop, you flip a switch and activate the full system.

**ALEX:** Plan for the architecture you want, price for the architecture you can afford today.

**KEVIN:** That is a great way to put it. And the third thing is that the models themselves are getting better at following complex instructions, using tools reliably, and maintaining coherence over long interactions. That means the boundary between "this task needs a full agent" and "this task can be handled by a tool call" is constantly shifting. Something that required a dedicated agent six months ago might work perfectly as a tool call today. You should build in a regular review cadence, maybe quarterly, where you evaluate each agent and ask whether it is still pulling its weight or whether the underlying model improvements have made it unnecessary. The systems that will win long-term are the ones designed for continuous evolution, not the ones built as monuments to a specific point in time in AI capability.

**ALEX:** Kevin, this has been an incredibly rich conversation. To summarize for our listeners: multi-agent architecture is a powerful pattern for enterprise problems that span multiple domains and require specialist-grade quality. The core design decisions are where you draw agent boundaries, which orchestration patterns you use, how agents communicate through structured protocols, and how you handle failures gracefully. The economics work when you tier your models, manage context intelligently, cache aggressively, and optimize for total cost of resolution rather than per-call cost. And critically, you should only use multi-agent when the problem genuinely demands it, because complexity that is not earned by real requirements is just overhead.
